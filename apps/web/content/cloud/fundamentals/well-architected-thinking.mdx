## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  A design that works today can still let you down, so ask it six plain questions on purpose and write down what each answer costs the others.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

Every night at 01:00, Brightpost's export job writes the day's orders to one file in the orders bucket. At 06:00 the printing partner's system downloads that file and prints the posters. On a Tuesday afternoon, Asha ships a change that renames one column in the orders table. The shop keeps taking orders, and every page loads. That night the export job asks for the old column name and crashes. It crashes again on Wednesday and on Thursday. Each crash writes one error line to the logs, and nothing reads them. The partner finds no new file and prints nothing. On Friday at 9 a.m. the partner phones to ask whether the shop has closed. About 1,200 orders now ship 3 days late, and the support agent answers about 300 "where is my order?" emails.

Nothing was down. The design "worked". Every step below asks one plain question of the shop as it stands today, and the first question would have caught this on Tuesday night.

## How it works

Three words first. A **workload** is the set of parts that together do one job for the business, such as Brightpost's shop. A **pillar** is one area of concern, such as security or cost. A **review** is a meeting where the team asks each pillar's question of one workload and writes down what it finds.

### Step 1: Six questions for one design

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/well-architected-thinking-1" step={1} minWidth={620} caption="Brightpost's shop today: three tiers across three zones, plus a nightly export to the printing partner and a resize worker." />

This is the shop as it runs today. A <Term id="load-balancer">load balancer</Term> spreads customers over 2 app servers in 3 <Term id="availability-zone">zones</Term>. The orders database has a standby in a second zone and a backup every night at 03:00. An export job feeds the printing partner, and a resize worker makes thumbnails of product photos.

All three big providers publish a "well-architected" framework built on nearly the same pillars. Each pillar boils down to one question you can ask of any design:

1. **Operational excellence.** If this part stopped tonight, who would know, and what would they do?
2. **Security.** Who and what can reach this data, and do they need to?
3. **Reliability.** When a part breaks or data is lost, how does it come back, and how long does that take?
4. **Performance efficiency.** Does each part keep up with its busiest minute?
5. **Cost optimisation.** What are we paying for that does no work?
6. **Sustainability.** What work, data or machines could we not have at all?

These are the [non-functional requirements](/system-design/fundamentals/non-functional-requirements) of a system, asked again of the system that is already running.

### Step 2: Operations: would anyone know?

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/well-architected-thinking-1" step={2} minWidth={620} caption="The failure: the export job crashed three nights running, and the only record was an error line nobody read." />

Brightpost watches what customers see: pages load, payments succeed. Nobody watches the jobs that run at night. Such a job can fail for a week, and the first signal is a customer.

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  Tuesday's change renames a column. At 01:00 the export job asks for the old name and stops. The bucket gets no file, so at 06:00 the partner downloads nothing and prints nothing. The same thing happens on Wednesday and Thursday. By Friday, about 1,200 orders are 3 days late, and the only evidence is three log lines.
</Callout>

The question has two halves. **Would anyone know?** needs an **alert**: a rule that watches a signal and tells a person when it goes wrong. **What would they do?** needs an **on-call** engineer, the person the alert rings this week. It also needs a **runbook**: a short page of steps for this exact failure. [SLIs, SLOs, Alerting and On-Call](/cloud/operations/slos-alerting-and-on-call) covers both.

### Step 3: Security: who can read the data?

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/well-architected-thinking-1" step={3} minWidth={620} caption="The printing partner can read every export file in the bucket, but it needs only last night's." />

The orders bucket holds 730 files with the names and home addresses of 90,000 customers. The printing partner's access lets it read all of them. It needs one file a night.

Ask what a thief would get if the partner's own systems were broken into. Today the answer is all 730 files. That is the **blast radius**: the damage one stolen credential can do. The shop's own servers follow <Term id="least-privilege">least privilege</Term> since the leak on [Identity and Access](/cloud/fundamentals/identity-and-access). The partner, a different company, was never reviewed.

The fix is a **signed link**: a web address for one file with a signature and an expiry time inside it. Each morning the partner gets a link to last night's file, valid for 24 hours. A thief now gets at most about 400 orders, and only for a day.

### Step 4: Reliability: can we get it back?

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/well-architected-thinking-1" step={4} minWidth={620} caption="The database has a nightly backup, but nobody has ever restored one, so nobody knows how long a restore takes." />

Brightpost has already handled machines failing. Health checks drop a dead server in about 30 seconds. If the database's zone fails, the standby takes over in about 2 minutes. That is <Term id="failover">failover</Term>, and [Regions, Zones and the Edge](/cloud/fundamentals/regions-zones-edge) covers it.

A standby does not protect against bad data. If a broken change deletes half the orders, the standby copies the delete within a second. Only the backup can bring the orders back. Two numbers describe a recovery. <Term id="rpo">RPO</Term> is how much recent data you can afford to lose. <Term id="rto">RTO</Term> is how long you can afford to be down.

Brightpost has never restored one of its backups, so its RTO is a guess. The fix is a **restore drill**: once a month, restore the latest backup into the test account and time it. The first drill takes 50 minutes. Now the team can promise "back within 1 hour" and mean it.

### Step 5: Performance: does it keep up at 9:00?

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/well-architected-thinking-1" step={5} minWidth={620} caption="On the sale day, 2,000 shoppers arrive at 9:00, and each new server needs about 4 minutes to start." />

<Term id="autoscaling" /> adds a server when the others pass about 60% processor use. One server serves about 200 shoppers at once, and each new one takes about 4 minutes to start. On a normal day, with about 40 shoppers online at the busiest moment, that is fine.

On the sale day, 2,000 shoppers arrive at 9:00. The 2 running servers can serve 400 of them. The rule reacts, but it adds servers a few at a time, 4 minutes apart. For the first 10 minutes or so, pages crawl and some checkouts time out.

Performance efficiency asks about the busiest minute, not the busiest hour. When a rush has a known start time, do not wait for the rule. Schedule 10 servers from 8:30 a.m. on sale days, then let the normal rule take over. [Autoscaling: The Control Loop](/cloud/compute/autoscaling) covers the rule itself.

### Step 6: Cost: what are we paying for?

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/well-architected-thinking-1" step={6} minWidth={620} caption="The database was sized for 4,000 orders a day and runs about 10% busy on the other days of the year." />

The orders database was sized for the biggest sale day, about 4,000 orders. On a normal day it takes about 400, and its processor is about 10% busy. The servers and the database are most of the bill, so this is a large line spent on room nobody uses.

The fix is <Term id="rightsizing">rightsizing</Term>: pick the size that fits real use. Brightpost moves the database one size down for most of the year. A week before each sale it moves back up. On a managed database with a standby, a resize usually means a failover, about 2 minutes. So the team does it at 6 a.m., when almost nobody shops.

Notice that this fix spends a little reliability to save money. The Trade-offs section comes back to that.

### Step 7: Sustainability: what work can we drop?

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/well-architected-thinking-1" step={7} minWidth={620} caption="The resize worker remakes about 3,600 thumbnails every night, but only about 40 photos are new." />

Every machine-hour uses electricity and a share of the hardware built to run it. Sustainability asks how much you could avoid.

The resize worker runs about 2 hours a night. At about 2 seconds per photo, that is about 3,600 photos. Brightpost adds about 300 new photos a week, about 43 a night. So about 99% of each night's work repeats the night before.

The fix: resize each photo once, when it is uploaded. That is about 90 seconds of work a night instead of 2 hours. The same fix shrinks the bill, which is common: sustainability and cost usually point the same way. Other sustainability questions are which <Term id="region">region</Term> to run in, how long to keep data, and whether a machine needs to run at night.

### Step 8: The fixes, one per question

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/well-architected-thinking-1" step={8} minWidth={620} caption="Every weakness is replaced by its fix, and an on-call alert now fires if no file has arrived by 02:00." />

The review ends with six findings, and each one gets a fix, an owner and a date.

With the alert in place, Tuesday's crash pages the on-call engineer at 02:00. The runbook says: fix the column name, re-run the job, check the file. That takes about 10 minutes, and the partner's 06:00 download finds the file.

### Step 9: Making review a habit

A design changes every week, so its answers drift. Brightpost reviews its shop at four moments:

1. **Before anything new goes live**, such as the export job, which went live without one.
2. **After every incident**, starting with the question the incident proves was skipped.
3. **Before each sale day**, with the performance and reliability questions first.
4. **Every 6 months**, even if nothing has happened.

A review takes the 3 engineers about 90 minutes. Each finding gets an owner and a date, or a written decision to accept the risk. "We keep one region and accept a few hours down if London fails" is a design choice. Forgetting to ask is not.

## On AWS, Google Cloud and Azure

All three providers publish a framework with nearly the same pillars, a way to run the review, and a service that checks running resources against it.

<Tabs title="On each cloud">
  <Tab label="AWS">
    - **Framework:** the AWS Well-Architected Framework.
    - **Pillars:** operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimization and sustainability.
    - **Review tool:** the AWS Well-Architected Tool in the console. You define a workload, answer the questions for each pillar, and get a list of risks and an improvement plan.
    - **Live checks:** AWS Trusted Advisor checks running resources and flags problems such as idle machines, open access and missing redundancy.
    - **Extra guidance:** lenses, extra sets of questions for one kind of workload, such as serverless apps.

    **The difference worth knowing.** The tool keeps every review of a workload, and you can save a milestone after each one. The next review starts from the risks the last one found, so the habit in Step 9 has a place to live.
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Google Cloud">
    - **Framework:** the Google Cloud Well-Architected Framework.
    - **Pillars:** operational excellence; security, privacy and compliance; reliability; cost optimization; performance optimization; sustainability.
    - **Review tool:** the framework's recommendations for each pillar, which the team works through itself.
    - **Live checks:** Active Assist recommenders flag running resources, such as idle VMs, oversized machines and unused access.
    - **Extra guidance:** perspectives, which apply every pillar to one kind of workload, such as AI and ML.

    **The difference worth knowing.** The names differ slightly. Security also covers privacy and compliance, and the performance pillar is called performance optimization. The questions underneath are the same six this page asks.
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Azure">
    - **Framework:** the Azure Well-Architected Framework.
    - **Pillars:** reliability, security, cost optimization, operational excellence and performance efficiency.
    - **Review tool:** the Azure Well-Architected Review, an online assessment for one workload that gives recommendations.
    - **Live checks:** Azure Advisor scans running resources and files its recommendations under the same pillar names.
    - **Extra guidance:** workload guides and service guides. Sustainability is one of the workload guides rather than a pillar.

    **The difference worth knowing.** Advisor's recommendations use the framework's own pillar names. So the review and the day-to-day checks speak one language, and a finding in Advisor maps straight to a pillar question.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Trade-offs

The pillars pull against each other. Making one answer better usually makes another worse, so a review records the choice instead of hiding it.

<Compare
  columns={["Change", "Helps", "Costs", "Brightpost's numbers"]}
  rows={[

    ["Database one size down outside sale weeks", "Cost, sustainability", "Reliability, performance", "10% busy most days; a 2-minute failover at each resize; less room for a surprise rush"],
    ["10 servers from 8:30 on sale days", "Performance", "Cost", "8 extra servers for about 30 minutes before the first shopper"],
    ["Signed link that expires in 24 hours", "Security", "Operations", "A missed download needs a new link, sent by hand"],
    ["Alert and on-call rota", "Operational excellence", "People's sleep", "Someone may be woken at 02:00; the runbook keeps the fix to about 10 minutes"],
    ["A second region", "Reliability", "Cost, operations", "Survives losing London; roughly doubles what runs, for a shop of 400 orders a day"],
  ]}
  caption="Brightpost's trade-offs. Every row helps one pillar by spending on another."
/>

Brightpost takes the first five and declines the sixth for now, and writes down why. No design scores best on every pillar. A good one makes each trade-off on purpose.

## Common pitfalls

- **Reviewing once, at launch.** The design changes every week, so the answers go stale. Review before each launch, after each incident and every 6 months.
- **Watching only what customers see.** Brightpost's pages were green while its export failed for 3 nights. Every job, including the ones that run at night, needs an alert and an owner.
- **Ticking boxes without proof.** "We have backups" is not an answer until a restore has been timed. Ask for evidence: a drill result, an alert that fired in a test.
- **Maximising one pillar.** Two regions for a 400-order shop buys reliability nobody asked for and roughly doubles the bill. Match each answer to what the business needs.
- **Findings with no owner.** A list of risks with no names and dates becomes the same list at the next review. Give each one an owner, or write down that you accept it.

## How to say it in an interview

"I review a design against six questions, one per pillar. Would anyone know if a part failed, and what would they do? Who can reach the data, and do they need to? How does it recover, and have we timed a restore? Does it keep up in its busiest minute? What are we paying for that does no work? And what work could we drop? The pillars pull against each other, so I write down each trade-off. A smaller database saves money but costs a 2-minute failover at each resize. Every finding gets an owner and a date. I repeat the review before launches, after incidents and every few months."

## Interview questions

<InterviewQuestions>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is a pillar in a well-architected review, and why are there several?" level="Junior" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** A pillar is one area of concern a design must answer for: operations, security, reliability, performance, cost and sustainability. There are several because a design can be strong on one and weak on another, and each has its own question.

    **Why.** Brightpost's shop was fine on reliability: three zones, a standby database, failover in about 2 minutes. Yet its export job failed silently for 3 nights, which is an operations problem. Its partner could read all 730 files, which is security. A single "is it good?" question misses these. Six questions make the team look at the design six ways. They also make trade-offs visible, because fixing one pillar often costs another.

    <AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/well-architected-thinking-1" step={8} caption="Six review cards around one design, each with the fix its question led to." />

    **Follow-ups the interviewer may ask.**
    - Which pillar do teams most often skip?
    - Name two pillars that usually point the same way.

    **Watch out for.** Reciting pillar names with no example. Tie each one to a real weakness in a real design.
  </InterviewQuestion>

  <InterviewQuestion question="Give one case where reliability and cost pull in opposite directions." level="Mid" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** A standby database. It makes failover take minutes instead of hours, but you pay for a second database that serves nothing until the first one fails. The reverse also holds: shrinking an oversized database saves money but leaves less room and adds a failover at each resize.

    **Why.** Brightpost's database was sized for 4,000 orders and runs about 10% busy on normal days. Moving one size down saves a large line on the bill. A resize means a failover, about 2 minutes, so the team does it at 6 a.m. and only twice a year, before each sale. They keep the standby, because without it a zone failure means a restore of about 50 minutes. The decision and its cost are written down.

    <AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/well-architected-thinking-1" step={6} highlight={["cost", "rel", "db"]} caption="Two review cards point at the same database: one asks what it costs, one asks how it comes back." />

    **Follow-ups the interviewer may ask.**
    - When would you drop the standby to save money?
    - How would you justify a second region to the finance lead?

    **Watch out for.** Saying "always choose reliability". A 400-order shop that doubles its bill for a second region may lose more than an outage would cost.
  </InterviewQuestion>

  <InterviewQuestion question="A nightly batch job failed three nights in a row and nobody noticed. What would you change?" level="Mid" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** Alert on the outcome, not only on errors: page the on-call engineer if the job's output has not arrived by a set time. Add a runbook for the likely fixes, and test the job in the deploy pipeline so a schema change breaks the build instead of the night.

    **Why.** Brightpost's export job wrote an error line each night, but nothing read the logs. The printing partner noticed first, on Friday, after about 1,200 orders were 3 days late. An alert that fires when no file exists by 02:00 catches any cause: a crash, a hang, or a job that never started. The partner downloads at 06:00, so a 10-minute fix at 02:00 hides the failure from customers completely.

    <AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/well-architected-thinking-1" step={8} highlight={["export", "bucket", "oncall", "ops"]} caption="The alert checks the bucket for the night's file and pages the on-call engineer if it is missing." />

    **Follow-ups the interviewer may ask.**
    - Why alert on the missing file rather than on the error line?
    - Who should be paged, and what if they do not answer?

    **Watch out for.** Only adding more logging. Logs record a failure; an alert with an owner gets it fixed.
  </InterviewQuestion>

  <InterviewQuestion question="Run a five-minute review of a single-zone web app with a nightly backup." level="Senior" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** Ask the six questions in order of risk, and expect reliability and operations to dominate. One zone means a zone failure takes everything down. A nightly backup that has never been restored means the recovery time is unknown. Then check who is alerted, who can reach the data, the busiest minute and idle spend.

    **Why.** Reliability: losing the zone loses the app, and only the backup remains. Ask the RPO; with one backup a night it can be up to 24 hours of data. Ask for a timed restore; until there is one, the RTO is a guess. Operations: does the backup job itself alert when it fails? Brightpost's export job proves silent nightly jobs happen. Security: who can read the backups? Performance and cost: is the single server sized for peak and idle the rest of the time? Finish with the two or three fixes that remove the most risk, such as a second zone and a restore drill.

    <AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/well-architected-thinking-1" step={4} highlight={["rel", "db"]} caption="The reliability card: a backup exists, but no restore has ever been timed." />

    **Follow-ups the interviewer may ask.**
    - The business says 4 hours of downtime is acceptable. What changes in your review?
    - Which single change gives the biggest improvement here?

    **Watch out for.** Jumping straight to multi-region. Ask what downtime and data loss the business can accept first; a second zone and a tested restore may be enough.
  </InterviewQuestion>
