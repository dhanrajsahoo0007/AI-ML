## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Write down every action in your cloud, let a program read those records day and night for anything strange, and make sure what it finds reaches a person or a fix within minutes.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

On a Friday at 17:40, Asha asks for help with a load-test tool on a public forum. She attaches the tool's settings file. Inside sits an old key for Brightpost's test account, one the clean-up after the storage leak missed. At 01:50 on Saturday, a stranger's script uses the key from a network abroad. It asks for 40 GPU machines to mine cryptocurrency. The test account's <Term id="quota">quota</Term> allows 4 servers, so 4 start. At 02:10 Brightpost's threat detector raises a finding marked high: new machines, started by a key from an unknown network, talking to a known mining pool. The finding waits in a weekly email. Asha reads it on Monday at 09:10. The machines have run for 55 hours, and the weekend costs as much as 6 weeks of the shop's normal bill.

Detection worked in 20 minutes. Everything after it failed. The steps below build the whole chain, then repair the part that broke.

## How it works

Three words first. An **event** is one action in the cloud, such as "start a machine". A **finding** is a short report that something looks wrong, with a severity: low, medium or high. **Severity** says how fast someone must act.

### Step 1: A record of every call

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={1} minWidth={620} caption="Each call an engineer or a tool makes is written to the audit trail, and a locked copy goes to the log archive." />

An **audit log** is the cloud's own record of every request made to it. Each line answers five questions: who called, what they asked for, on which resource, from which network address, and whether it was allowed. Your app's own logs say what your code did. The audit log says what was done *to your cloud*, by people, tools and stolen keys alike.

Brightpost's audit trail copies every line to the log archive in the audit account. The archive is **locked**: records can be added, but nobody can change or delete them for 1 year, not even an administrator. The [landing zone](/cloud/fundamentals/landing-zones) set this up for every account. Common attacks and the defences against them are covered in [Common attacks](/system-design/fundamentals/common-attacks).

### Step 2: Two kinds of events

Clouds split audit events into two kinds, and they behave differently.

The stranger's 40 machine requests were management events, so they were in the audit trail without any extra setting. Data events cost more to store, because there are far more of them. Brightpost records them only on buckets that hold customer data. After a breach, those are the lines that say what was taken.

### Step 3: Network and DNS logs

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={2} minWidth={620} caption="The network keeps two more records about the test machines: who they talked to, and which names they looked up." />

The audit log sees calls to the cloud. It cannot see what a machine does once it runs. Two network logs fill that gap.

- **Flow logs** record each connection a machine makes: source address, destination address, port, bytes and whether it was let through. They do not record the contents.
- **DNS logs** record each name a machine looks up. DNS is the internet's phone book, which turns a name into an address.

A mining program has to reach its pool, and malware has to reach its owner. Both look up a name first, and both show up as traffic to addresses the shop has never used. On Saturday, the 4 machines looked up a mining pool's name within 3 minutes of starting.

### Step 4: A detector raises findings

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={3} minWidth={620} caption="The threat detector reads the audit trail, flow logs and DNS logs and sends each finding to one hub." />

A **threat detector** is a managed service that reads those three streams all the time. You do not write its rules. It compares what it reads with two things: lists of known bad addresses and names, and what is normal for your account. It learns, for example, which networks each key usually calls from, and at what hours.

Each finding names the resource, what happened, when, and a severity. Saturday's finding combined three signals: a key calling from a network it had never used, 4 new GPU machines, and lookups of a mining pool. Any one alone might be innocent. Together they made it high.

All findings go to a **findings hub**, one list for every account and every region. In the naive setup, the hub's only output is a weekly email.

### Step 5: Posture checks and vulnerability scans

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={4} minWidth={620} caption="A posture check and a vulnerability scan send their own findings to the same hub." />

Detection asks "is something bad happening?" Two other checks ask "could something bad happen?"

A **posture check** reads your settings and compares them with a **benchmark**, a published list of safe settings such as the CIS Benchmarks. "This bucket is public" and "this database port is open to the internet" are posture findings. Brightpost's check runs every hour, as its landing zone set up.

A **vulnerability scan** looks inside machines and container images. An **image** is a packaged copy of an app and everything it needs; images wait in an <Term id="image-registry">image registry</Term>. The scan lists installed software and matches each version against public lists of known flaws. Last month it flagged a flaw in the image library the resize worker uses, two days after the flaw was published.

Posture and scans catch the open door. Detection catches someone walking through it.

### Step 6: The finding nobody saw

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={5} minWidth={620} caption="The failure: the leaked key starts 4 GPU machines at 01:50, and the high finding raised at 02:10 waits in the weekly email." />

Every part of the chain worked, except the last one.

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  At 01:50 the stranger's script asks for 40 machines and the quota lets 4 start. The audit trail records all 212 of the key's calls, including one attempt to create a second key, which its policy refused. At 02:10 the detector raises a high finding, and the hub adds it to the weekly email. Nobody is told. The machines mine for 55 hours, until Asha reads the email on Monday at 09:10.
</Callout>

A finding that reaches nobody protects nothing. The time that matters is not "how fast did we detect it?" but "how fast did someone or something act?" Here those were 20 minutes and 55 hours.

### Step 7: Route by severity

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={6} minWidth={620} caption="An alert rule sends high findings to the on-call engineer's phone. Low findings stay in the weekly email." />

An **alert rule** watches the hub and acts on each finding by its severity. Brightpost's 3 engineers now take turns being **on call**, one week each: that person's phone rings for anything urgent, day or night.

1. **High:** page the on-call engineer. The phone rings within 2 minutes.
2. **Medium:** open a ticket that the team reviews the next working day.
3. **Low:** add it to the weekly email.

Paging for everything is the opposite mistake. A phone that rings 30 times a night gets muted, and then high findings are missed again. Brightpost's hub raises about 2 high findings a month, so a page means something. More on rotas and paging in [SLOs, Alerting and On-Call](/cloud/operations/slos-alerting-and-on-call).

### Step 8: An automatic first response

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={7} minWidth={620} caption="The alert rule also starts a response function. It disables the key and isolates the machines, and the stranger's next call is refused." />

A person needs minutes to wake up and understand a page. A **response function** acts in seconds. It is a small program that the same alert rule starts. For a leaked key, Brightpost's runbook, its written list of steps, does four things.

1. **Disable the key**, not delete it. Disabling can be undone, and the key's history stays in the audit trail.
2. **Isolate each machine:** swap its firewall rules for "deny everything", so it can no longer reach the pool or anything else.
3. **Take a <Term id="snapshot">snapshot</Term>** of each disk, so the evidence survives.
4. **Post a summary** to the on-call engineer: which key, which machines, what was done.

Had this run on that Saturday, the key would have been disabled at about 02:11, and the stranger's next call refused. Automate only steps that are safe and reversible. Deleting the machines also deletes the evidence.

### Step 9: Test your own defences

A detector you have never seen fire is a guess. There are three ways to test the chain, and each needs permission first.

- **Attack simulation:** run harmless copies of real attacks, such as a call from an unusual network or a lookup of a test name that detectors know. Then check that a finding, a page and the response all happen.
- **Penetration test:** hire testers to break in on purpose, within a written scope that lists what they may touch and when.
- **Game day:** a planned drill where the team plays out an incident, so the runbook gets tested along with the tools.

The rules come in two layers. Inside the company: written approval from the account's owner, the scope, the time window, and a warning to on-call. Outside it: the provider's own testing policy. You may test only your own resources, never other customers or the provider's shared systems. Each cloud publishes what you may test without asking, as the tabs below show. Brightpost now runs a game day every quarter in the test account.

## On AWS, Google Cloud and Azure

The chain is the same on all three clouds: an audit log, detectors that read it with network logs, posture and vulnerability checks, one place for findings, and rules that act on them.

<Tabs title="On each cloud">
  <Tab label="AWS">
    - **Audit log:** AWS CloudTrail. Management events are recorded by default and kept for a short time. A trail sends them to an S3 bucket, and an organization trail covers every account. Data events are switched on per trail. S3 Object Lock locks the archive, and log file integrity validation can prove nobody edited it.
    - **Detector:** Amazon GuardDuty reads CloudTrail events, VPC Flow Logs and DNS query logs from its own feed, so it works even if you never switched those logs on. Amazon Detective builds a graph from the same data to help investigate a finding.
    - **Posture and scans:** AWS Security Hub checks settings against standards such as the CIS AWS Foundations Benchmark, using AWS Config rules. Amazon Inspector scans EC2 instances, container images and Lambda functions for known flaws.
    - **Findings and response:** Security Hub gathers findings from GuardDuty, Inspector and others across accounts. An Amazon EventBridge rule matches high findings and calls Amazon SNS to page, or AWS Lambda to respond.
    - **Testing rules:** AWS lists services you may test without asking first. Flood tests follow a separate policy.

    **The difference that changes a design.** GuardDuty and Security Hub work per Region. A Region where they are off is a Region nobody watches, and an attacker can start machines there. So turn both on in every account and every Region you allow, with one delegated administrator account to manage them, and gather findings in one Region. Brightpost's guardrail allows only 2 Regions, which keeps that list short.
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Google Cloud">
    - **Audit log:** Cloud Audit Logs. Admin Activity logs are always on and cannot be switched off. Data Access logs are mostly off by default and are switched on per service. An organization-level log sink copies every project's logs to a central bucket, and a bucket lock locks it.
    - **Detector:** Event Threat Detection, part of Security Command Center, reads logs that reach Cloud Logging, including VPC Flow Logs and Cloud DNS logs.
    - **Posture and scans:** Security Health Analytics, also in Security Command Center, checks settings, with reports against benchmarks such as CIS. Artifact Analysis scans container images in Artifact Registry. VM Manager can report known flaws in VM operating systems.
    - **Findings and response:** Security Command Center lists findings for the whole organization. A notification config publishes chosen findings to Pub/Sub, and a Cloud Run function subscribed to it pages or responds.
    - **Testing rules:** you need not tell Google before testing your own projects. Tests must follow its Acceptable Use Policy and terms of service.

    **The difference that changes a design.** Event Threat Detection can only read what reaches Cloud Logging. Flow logs are switched on per subnet and DNS logging per network, so a subnet with flow logs off is invisible to it. Data reads are invisible until you switch on Data Access logs. So the logging plan is part of the detection plan. Activate Security Command Center at the organization, so new projects are covered on day one.
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Azure">
    - **Audit log:** the Azure Activity Log records management operations for each subscription and keeps them for a limited time. A diagnostic setting sends it on to a Log Analytics workspace or a storage account. Data operations are resource logs, switched on per resource. Immutable blob storage locks the archive.
    - **Detector:** Microsoft Defender for Cloud plans, switched on per resource type such as servers, storage or Resource Manager, raise security alerts. Microsoft Sentinel is a separate service that collects logs from many sources into a Log Analytics workspace and raises incidents from rules.
    - **Posture and scans:** Defender for Cloud scores your settings against the Microsoft cloud security benchmark and lists recommendations. Its server and container plans scan VMs and container images for known flaws.
    - **Findings and response:** Defender for Cloud lists alerts and recommendations across subscriptions. Workflow automation starts an Azure Logic App on an alert, and Sentinel playbooks do the same for incidents.
    - **Testing rules:** no advance notice is needed. Tests must follow Microsoft's rules of engagement for penetration testing.

    **The difference that changes a design.** Detection and posture live in Defender for Cloud, but searching and joining logs from many sources lives in Sentinel, on top of a Log Analytics workspace. So decide early which workspace every subscription's logs go to, usually one central one. Azure Policy can add the diagnostic setting to each new subscription, so none starts unlogged.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

Use all three, sorted by severity. Automate the safe, reversible first step, then let a person decide the rest.

## Common pitfalls

- **Findings with no route.** A hub that only sends a digest is a diary, not an alarm. Route every high finding to a person who can act within minutes.
- **Detection on in some places only.** A new account or region without it is where an attacker goes. Turn detection on from the top of the <Term id="organization">organization</Term>, so new accounts are covered from their first minute.
- **No data events where data lives.** After a breach, management events say a key was used, but not which files it read. Record data events on every store that holds customer data.
- **Paging for everything.** Thirty pages a night teach people to mute the phone. Page only for high findings, and review the rule every month.
- **Automating a destructive fix.** A function that deletes a "bad" machine also deletes the evidence, and on a false alarm it deletes a working server. Disable, isolate and snapshot instead.

## How to say it in an interview

"I split security monitoring into two jobs. Posture asks whether anything is configured wrongly right now, and vulnerability scans ask whether known flaws are installed. Detection asks whether something bad is happening. The detector reads the audit log, which records every call to the cloud, plus flow and DNS logs. The audit log goes to a locked archive in a separate account, and I switch on data events where customer data lives. Every finding lands in one hub for all accounts. A rule routes high findings to on-call and starts a reversible first response, such as disabling a key and isolating a machine. Then I test the whole chain with attack simulations and game days."

## Interview questions

<InterviewQuestions>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What goes in an audit log, and how is it different from an application log?" level="Junior" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** An audit log is the cloud's record of every call made to it: who called, what they asked for, on which resource, from where, and whether it was allowed. An application log is your own code's record of what it did. The first describes actions on your cloud; the second describes your app.

    **Why.** At Brightpost, the app's logs said nothing about the stranger, because the stranger never touched the app. The audit trail recorded all 212 calls the leaked key made, including the 40 machine requests and the refused attempt to create a second key. The cloud writes audit logs for you, so a careless or hostile admin cannot skip them. That is also why they go to a locked archive in a separate account.

    <AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={1} caption="The cloud writes each call to the audit trail, and a locked copy goes to the archive." />

    **Follow-ups the interviewer may ask.**
    - What is the difference between management events and data events?
    - Who should be allowed to read the archive?

    **Watch out for.** Saying "we log everything in the app, so we are covered". A stolen key that calls the cloud directly never passes through your app.
  </InterviewQuestion>

  <InterviewQuestion question="Posture or detection: which catches a public bucket, and which catches a stolen key in use?" level="Mid" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** A posture check catches the public bucket, because it is a setting that is wrong right now. Detection catches the stolen key, because the key's settings look normal and only its behaviour is strange.

    **Why.** A posture check reads settings and compares them with a benchmark, so "this bucket is readable by anyone" is a finding within one scan. Brightpost scans every hour. A stolen key has no bad setting: it is a valid key. Only the pattern shows the theft. Brightpost's key called from a network it had never used, started 4 GPU machines, and those machines looked up a mining pool. The detector raises that from the audit, flow and DNS logs.

    **Follow-ups the interviewer may ask.**

    **Watch out for.** Treating them as one tool. A clean posture score says nothing about a valid key in the wrong hands.

  <InterviewQuestion question="Your detector raised a high finding at 02:10 on a Saturday, and nobody acted until Monday. What do you change?" level="Mid" category="Cloud">

    **Why.** At Brightpost, the time to detect was 20 minutes and the time to act was 55 hours. The fix sorts findings by severity. High findings page a phone within 2 minutes. Medium ones become tickets for the next working day, and low ones stay in the weekly email. Page sparingly: Brightpost gets about 2 high findings a month, so a page is never ignored. For leaked keys, a function disables the key at once.

  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you check that your detection really works, and what are you allowed to test on a public cloud?" level="Mid" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** Run harmless attack simulations and regular game days, and hire penetration testers for a wider check. Test only your own resources, with written approval and a scope, and follow the provider's published testing policy.

    **Why.** A detector you have never seen fire is a guess. Every quarter, Brightpost calls the cloud with a test key from an unusual network, in the test account. Outside it, each cloud says what you may test without asking, and no cloud allows tests on other customers or on its shared systems.

    **Follow-ups the interviewer may ask.**
    - How is a game day different from a penetration test?
    - What would you do if the drill finds that nobody was paged?

  <InterviewQuestion question="Design the automated response to a leaked access key." level="Senior" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** A high finding about a key starts a function. It disables the key, isolates every machine the key started, snapshots their disks, and pages a person with a summary. Everything it does is reversible, and a human decides the rest.

    **Why.** A person needs minutes to wake up; the function acts in seconds. On Brightpost's Saturday, the key would have been disabled at about 02:11 instead of Monday morning. Disabling keeps the key's history, while deleting it makes the investigation harder. Isolating swaps the machine's firewall rules for "deny everything", so it stops mining but stays available for evidence. The function runs with least privilege of its own: it may disable keys and change firewall rules, nothing more. Afterwards, the audit trail lists every resource the key created, so nothing is left running.

    - What if the finding is a false alarm on a production key?
