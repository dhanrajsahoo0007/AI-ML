## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Record every action in your cloud, let a program watch those records for anything strange, and make sure what it finds reaches a person or a fix within minutes.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

On a Friday at 17:40, Asha asks for help with a load-test tool on a public forum. She attaches the tool's settings file. Inside sits an old key for Brightpost's test account, one the clean-up after the storage leak missed. At 01:50 on Saturday, a stranger's script uses the key from a network abroad. It asks for 40 GPU machines to mine cryptocurrency. The test account's <Term id="quota">quota</Term> allows 4 servers, so 4 start. At 02:10 Brightpost's threat detector raises a finding marked high: new machines, started by a key from an unknown network, talking to a known mining pool. The finding waits in a weekly email. Asha reads it on Monday at 09:10. The machines have run for 55 hours, and the weekend costs as much as 6 weeks of the shop's normal bill.

Detection worked in 20 minutes. Everything after it failed. The steps below build the whole chain, then repair the part that broke.

## How it works

Three words first. An **event** is one action in the cloud, such as "start a machine". A **finding** is a short report that something looks wrong, with a severity: low, medium or high. **Severity** says how fast someone must act.

### Step 1: A record of every call

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={1} minWidth={620} caption="Each call an engineer or a tool makes is written to the audit trail, and a locked copy goes to the log archive." />

An **audit log** is the cloud's own record of every request made to it. Each line answers five questions: who called, what they asked for, on which resource, from which network address, and whether it was allowed. Your app's own logs say what your code did. The audit log says what was done *to your cloud*, by people, tools and stolen keys alike.

Brightpost's audit trail copies every line to the log archive in the audit account. The archive is **locked**: records can be added, but nobody can change or delete them for 1 year, not even an administrator. The [landing zone](/cloud/fundamentals/landing-zones) set this up for every account. Common attacks and the defences against them are covered in [Common attacks](/system-design/fundamentals/common-attacks).

### Step 2: Two kinds of events

Clouds split audit events into two kinds, and they behave differently.

The stranger's 40 machine requests were management events, recorded with no extra setting. Data events are far more numerous and cost more to keep, so Brightpost records them only where customer data lives. After a breach, they say what was taken.

### Step 3: Network and DNS logs

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={2} minWidth={620} caption="The network keeps two more records about the test machines: who they talked to, and which names they looked up." />

The audit log sees calls to the cloud, not what a machine does once it runs. Two network logs fill that gap.

- **Flow logs** record each connection a machine makes: source, destination, port, bytes, and whether it was allowed. Not the contents.
- **DNS logs** record each name a machine looks up. DNS is the internet's phone book, which turns a name into an address.

A mining program must reach its pool, and malware must reach its owner. Both look up a name first, then send traffic to addresses the shop never uses. On Saturday, the 4 machines looked up a mining pool within 3 minutes of starting.

### Step 4: A detector raises findings

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={3} minWidth={620} caption="The threat detector reads the audit trail, flow logs and DNS logs and sends each finding to one hub." />

A **threat detector** is a managed service that reads those three streams all the time. You do not write its rules. It compares them with lists of known bad addresses and names. It also learns what is normal, such as which networks each key calls from.

Each finding names the resource, what happened, when, and a severity. Saturday's finding joined three signals: a key on a new network, 4 new GPU machines, and lookups of a mining pool. Any one alone might be innocent. Together they made it high.

All findings go to a **findings hub**, one list for every account and every region. In the naive setup, the hub's only output is a weekly email.

### Step 5: Posture checks and vulnerability scans

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={4} minWidth={620} caption="A posture check and a vulnerability scan send their own findings to the same hub." />

Detection asks "is something bad happening?" Two other checks ask "could something bad happen?"

A **posture check** reads your settings and compares them with a **benchmark**, a published list of safe settings such as the CIS Benchmarks. "This bucket is public" and "this database port is open to the internet" are posture findings. Brightpost's check runs every hour, as its landing zone set up.

A **vulnerability scan** looks inside machines and container images. An **image** is a packaged copy of an app and everything it needs, kept in an <Term id="image-registry">image registry</Term>. The scan matches each installed version against public lists of known flaws. Last month it flagged the resize worker's image library, two days after the flaw was published.

Posture and scans catch the open door. Detection catches someone walking through it.

### Step 6: The finding nobody saw

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={5} minWidth={620} caption="The failure: the leaked key starts 4 GPU machines at 01:50, and the high finding raised at 02:10 waits in the weekly email." />

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  At 01:50 the stranger's script asks for 40 machines and the quota lets 4 start. The audit trail records all 212 of the key's calls, including a refused attempt to create a second key. At 02:10 the detector raises a high finding, and the hub adds it to the weekly email. Nobody is told. The machines mine for 55 hours.
</Callout>

A finding that reaches nobody protects nothing. What matters is how fast someone or something acts: here, 55 hours after a 20-minute detection.

### Step 7: Route by severity

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={6} minWidth={620} caption="An alert rule sends high findings to the on-call engineer's phone. Low findings stay in the weekly email." />

An **alert rule** watches the hub and acts on each finding by severity. Brightpost's 3 engineers now take turns **on call**, a week each: that person's phone rings for anything urgent.

1. **High:** page the on-call engineer. The phone rings within 2 minutes.
2. **Medium:** open a ticket that the team reviews the next working day.
3. **Low:** add it to the weekly email.

Paging for everything is the opposite mistake: a phone that rings 30 times a night gets muted. Brightpost's hub raises about 2 high findings a month, so a page means something. Rotas and paging are covered in [SLOs, Alerting and On-Call](/cloud/operations/slos-alerting-and-on-call).

### Step 8: An automatic first response

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/detection-pipeline" step={7} minWidth={620} caption="The alert rule also starts a response function. It disables the key and isolates the machines, and the stranger's next call is refused." />

A person needs minutes to wake up and read a page. A **response function**, a small program the same alert rule starts, acts in seconds. For a leaked key, Brightpost's runbook, its written list of steps, does four things.

1. **Disable the key**, not delete it. Disabling can be undone, and the key's history stays in the audit trail.
2. **Isolate each machine:** swap its firewall rules for "deny everything", so it can no longer reach the pool or anything else.
3. **Take a <Term id="snapshot">snapshot</Term>** of each disk, so the evidence survives.
4. **Post a summary** to the on-call engineer: which key, which machines, what was done.

On that Saturday, the key would have been disabled at about 02:11. Automate only steps that are safe and can be undone; deleting the machines would also delete the evidence.

### Step 9: Test your own defences

A detector you have never seen fire is a guess. Three kinds of test check the chain, and each needs permission first.

- **Attack simulation:** run harmless copies of real attacks, such as a call from an unusual network. Check that a finding, a page and the response all happen.
- **Penetration test:** hire testers to break in on purpose, within a written scope that lists what they may touch and when.
- **Game day:** a planned drill where the team plays out an incident, testing the runbook as well as the tools.

The rules come in two layers. Inside the company: written approval from the account's owner, a scope, a time window, and a warning to on-call. Outside it: the provider's testing policy. You may test only your own resources, never other customers or the provider's shared systems. Brightpost now runs a game day every quarter in the test account.

## On AWS, Google Cloud and Azure

The chain is the same on all three clouds: audit and network logs, detectors, posture and vulnerability checks, one place for findings, and rules that act on them.

<Tabs title="On each cloud">
  <Tab label="AWS">
    - **Audit log:** AWS CloudTrail records management events by default. An organization trail sends every account's events to an S3 bucket; data events are switched on per trail. S3 Object Lock locks the archive, and log file integrity validation proves nobody edited it.
    - **Detector:** Amazon GuardDuty reads CloudTrail events, VPC Flow Logs and DNS query logs from its own feed, even where you never switched those logs on. Amazon Detective helps investigate a finding.
    - **Posture and scans:** AWS Security Hub checks settings against standards such as the CIS benchmark, using AWS Config rules. Amazon Inspector scans EC2 instances, container images and Lambda functions.
    - **Findings and response:** Security Hub gathers findings across accounts. An Amazon EventBridge rule matches high findings and calls Amazon SNS to page, or AWS Lambda to respond.
    - **Testing rules:** AWS lists services you may test without asking first. Flood tests follow a separate policy.

    **The difference that changes a design.** GuardDuty and Security Hub work per Region. A Region where they are off is a Region nobody watches, and an attacker can start machines there. So turn both on in every account and allowed Region, run them from one delegated administrator account, and gather findings in one Region. Brightpost's guardrail allows only 2 Regions.
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Google Cloud">
    - **Audit log:** Cloud Audit Logs. Admin Activity logs are always on. Data Access logs are mostly off until you switch them on per service. An organization-level log sink copies every project's logs to a central bucket, and a bucket lock locks it.
    - **Detector:** Event Threat Detection, part of Security Command Center, reads logs that reach Cloud Logging, including VPC Flow Logs and Cloud DNS logs.
    - **Posture and scans:** Security Health Analytics, also in Security Command Center, checks settings and reports against benchmarks such as CIS. Artifact Analysis scans container images. VM Manager can report known flaws in VM operating systems.
    - **Findings and response:** Security Command Center lists findings for the whole organization. A notification config publishes chosen findings to Pub/Sub, and a Cloud Run function pages or responds.
    - **Testing rules:** you need not tell Google before testing your own projects. Tests must follow its Acceptable Use Policy and terms of service.

    **The difference that changes a design.** Event Threat Detection reads only what reaches Cloud Logging. Flow logs are switched on per subnet and DNS logging per network, so a subnet with flow logs off is invisible to it. The logging plan is part of the detection plan. Activate Security Command Center at the organization, so new projects are covered on day one.
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Azure">
    - **Audit log:** the Azure Activity Log records management operations for each subscription, for a limited time. A diagnostic setting sends it to a Log Analytics workspace or a storage account. Data operations are resource logs, switched on per resource. Immutable blob storage locks the archive.
    - **Detector:** Microsoft Defender for Cloud plans, switched on per resource type such as servers, storage or Resource Manager, raise security alerts. Microsoft Sentinel collects logs from many sources into a Log Analytics workspace and raises incidents from rules.
    - **Posture and scans:** Defender for Cloud scores your settings against the Microsoft cloud security benchmark. Its server and container plans scan VMs and container images for known flaws.
    - **Findings and response:** Defender for Cloud lists alerts and recommendations across subscriptions. Workflow automation starts an Azure Logic App on an alert, and Sentinel playbooks do the same for incidents.
    - **Testing rules:** no advance notice is needed. Tests must follow Microsoft's rules of engagement for penetration testing.

    **The difference that changes a design.** Posture and alerts live in Defender for Cloud. Searching and joining logs from many sources lives in Sentinel, on a Log Analytics workspace. So decide early which workspace every subscription's logs go to, usually one central one. Azure Policy can add the diagnostic setting to each new subscription.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

Use all three, sorted by severity. Automate the safe first step, then let a person decide the rest.

## Common pitfalls

- **Findings with no route.** A hub that only sends a digest is a diary, not an alarm. Route high findings to a person who can act within minutes.
- **Detection on in some places only.** A new account or region without it is where an attacker goes. Turn detection on from the top of the <Term id="organization">organization</Term>, so new accounts are covered from their first minute.
- **No data events where data lives.** Management events show a key was used, not which files it read. Record data events on every store of customer data.
- **Paging for everything.** Thirty pages a night teach people to mute the phone. Page only for high findings, and review the rule every month.
- **Automating a destructive fix.** Deleting a "bad" machine deletes the evidence, and on a false alarm a working server. Disable, isolate and snapshot instead.

## How to say it in an interview

"I split security monitoring into two jobs. Posture asks whether anything is configured wrongly, and vulnerability scans ask whether known flaws are installed. Detection asks whether something bad is happening. The detector reads the audit log, which records every call to the cloud, plus flow and DNS logs. The audit log goes to a locked archive in a separate account, and I switch on data events where customer data lives. Every finding lands in one hub for all accounts. A rule routes high findings to on-call and starts a reversible first response, such as disabling a key and isolating a machine. Then I test the whole chain with attack simulations and game days."

An application log is your own code's record of what it did. The first describes actions on your cloud; the second describes your app.

    **Why.** At Brightpost, the app's logs said nothing about the stranger, who never touched the app. The audit trail recorded all 212 of the leaked key's calls, including the 40 machine requests and a refused attempt to create a second key. The cloud writes audit logs itself, and a locked archive in a separate account keeps them safe from a hostile admin.

  <InterviewQuestion question="Posture or detection: which catches a public bucket, and which catches a stolen key in use?" level="Mid" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** A posture check catches the public bucket, because it is a setting that is wrong right now. Detection catches the stolen key, because the key's settings look normal and only its behaviour is strange.

    **Why.** A posture check compares settings with a benchmark, so "this bucket is readable by anyone" is a finding within one scan; Brightpost scans every hour. A stolen key has no bad setting: it is a valid key. Only its behaviour shows the theft. Brightpost's key called from a new network and started 4 GPU machines that looked up a mining pool.
