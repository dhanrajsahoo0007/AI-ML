## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  An autoscaler is a loop: it checks how busy your servers are, adds or removes servers to stay near a target, then waits to see what changed.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

Brightpost sends its newsletter in three batches of 30,000 customers, at 18:00, 18:15 and 18:30. Each batch brings about 700 shoppers within a few minutes. At 18:00 the shop runs 2 app servers, with room for about 400 shoppers. Its <Term id="autoscaling">autoscaling</Term> rule aims for 60% processor use, so it asks for 4 more servers. Each takes about 4 minutes to start, and they are ready at about 18:05, as the first wave fades. To save money, Asha had set the rule to remove servers after 2 quiet minutes. At 18:09 it removed all 4 at once and cut off the checkouts running on them. At 18:15 the second wave met 2 servers again, and at 18:30 the third did too. In 45 minutes the shop started 12 servers and stopped 12, and about 90 orders were lost.

The rule did what it was told, every time. Each step below explains one part of the loop and the setting Brightpost should have chosen.

## How it works

Four words first. A **metric** is a number measured again and again, such as average processor use per minute. A **target** is the value you want it to stay near. A **server group** is a set of identical servers that grows or shrinks when you change one number, the desired count. Each new server starts from an **image**: a saved disk with the operating system and app installed.

### Step 1: A loop: measure, compare, act, wait

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-loop" step={1} minWidth={620} caption="The loop: servers report processor use every minute, and the autoscaler compares the average with 60%." />

An autoscaler does four things, over and over. It **measures**: each server reports its processor use to a metrics service every minute. It **compares** the group's average with the target, 60% for Brightpost. It **acts**: too busy, and it raises the desired count; too idle, and it lowers it. Then it **waits**, because new servers need minutes before their numbers mean anything.

At 18:01 the average is near 100%. One server serves about 200 shoppers, so at 60% it carries about 120. The wave needs 700 ÷ 120, about 6 servers, so the autoscaler asks for 4 more. [Capacity planning and bottlenecks](/system-design/fundamentals/capacity-planning) covers how many servers a load needs; this page covers the loop that changes the count.

### Step 2: Scaling out takes minutes

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-loop" step={2} minWidth={620} caption="The autoscaler raises the count to 6. Servers 3 to 6 boot from the image and take shoppers after warm-up." />

**Scaling out** means adding servers; **scaling in** means removing them. The group starts each new server from the image. The server boots, starts the app and passes the <Term id="load-balancer">load balancer</Term>'s health checks. Only then does it get shoppers.

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-1" step={1} minWidth={620} caption="A minute to measure, half a minute to decide, about 4 minutes to start: the new servers serve from 18:05:30." />

Add up the lag: a minute of data, seconds to decide, about 4 minutes to start. For five and a half minutes, 700 shoppers share room for 400.

**Warm-up** is the time after a server starts during which the autoscaler ignores its numbers. A booting server reports odd values, and counting them makes the loop add servers it does not need. Set warm-up to the real start time, about 4 minutes at Brightpost. Some rules use a **cooldown** instead: a fixed pause after any change.

### Step 3: Minimum and maximum, the safety rails

A metric can be wrong, so the desired count always stays between two hard limits.

- **Minimum: 1.** If the metric stops arriving or reads zero, the shop still has a server.
- **Maximum: 10.** A bug that spins the processor, or a flood of bots, would otherwise rent servers without end. Ten servers serve about 2,000 shoppers, Brightpost's largest crowd.
- **Quota.** The cloud also caps how many servers an account may run; Brightpost's test account allows 4. A maximum above the quota fails quietly. [Quotas, Throttling, Retries and Backoff](/cloud/resilience/quotas-and-retries) covers raising one.

### Step 4: Flapping

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-loop" step={3} minWidth={620} caption="The failure: two quiet minutes after the wave, the autoscaler removes 4 servers at once, and the next wave meets 2." />

**Flapping** is a group that grows and shrinks again and again, faster than demand really changes. Every cycle pays the start-up lag again. Every removal can cut someone off.

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  By 18:07 the first wave has faded to about 200 shoppers, and 6 servers sit at about 17%. Two quiet minutes later the rule cuts the count to 2. It removes 4 servers at once, without asking the balancer to drain them, and about 15 checkouts fail. At 18:15 the second wave meets 2 servers, and the 4-minute lag starts again. Each wave gets about 5 minutes of timeouts.
</Callout>

Flapping has a second cause that needs no waves: thresholds set too close. Say a rule adds 2 servers above 70% and removes 2 below 40%. Two servers at 75% become four at about 38%, so two go, and the pair is back at 75%. Keep thresholds far apart, or use one target.

### Step 5: Scaling in safely

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-loop" step={4} minWidth={620} caption="Scale-in now waits 15 quiet minutes, drains each server for 60 seconds and removes one at a time." />

Adding a server late costs shoppers. Removing one late costs a few server-minutes. So make scale-out fast and scale-in slow. Brightpost's fix has four parts:

1. **Wait longer.** The rule removes a server only after 15 quiet minutes, so the next wave arrives before any server goes.
2. **One at a time.** One server leaves per decision, and the loop measures again before the next.
3. **Drain first.** **Connection draining** means the balancer sends the server no new requests and lets open ones finish. Brightpost allows 60 seconds, the same as for deploys on the [load balancing](/cloud/networking/load-balancing) page. Then the server stops.
4. **Protect busy servers.** **Scale-in protection** is a flag that tells the group not to pick this server. A server running a long job sets it on itself and clears it when done.

With these settings the 18:15 wave meets 6 servers.

### Step 6: Four kinds of rule

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-loop" step={5} minWidth={620} caption="A schedule raises the floor before known rushes, and a forecast starts servers before the usual daily rise." />

Reactive rules always run behind demand, by the lag of Step 2. Schedules and forecasts act ahead. They raise the floor, and target tracking handles anything above it. A forecast needs weeks of history, so it cannot see a one-off newsletter. Brightpost now schedules 6 servers from 17:55 to 19:00 on newsletter evenings.

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-1" step={2} minWidth={620} caption="With the schedule, 6 servers are running from 17:55, and the wave meets full room." />

### Step 7: Warm pools and hibernation

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-loop" step={6} minWidth={620} caption="A warm pool of 4 stopped servers, already installed, joins the group in about a minute." />

A **warm pool** is a set of servers already created and installed, then stopped. On scale-out the group takes servers from the pool first. Brightpost keeps 4 in its pool, and they join in about 1 minute instead of 4.

A stopped server costs its disks, not its processor time. A running spare costs everything, but joins at once. **Hibernation** goes further: the server writes its memory to disk before it stops. It resumes with the app running and its caches full, so it skips boot and warm-up. Only some server types support it.

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-1" step={3} minWidth={620} caption="With a warm pool, the start takes about a minute and 6 servers serve from 18:02:30." />

Also shrink the start itself: install the app into the image, not at boot. [Virtual Machines, Instance Families and Images](/cloud/compute/virtual-machines) explains baking an image.

### Step 8: Choosing the signal

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-loop" step={7} minWidth={620} caption="Receipt workers scale on the queue's backlog per worker, not on processor use." />

The metric must rise when users start to suffer. Processor use works for Brightpost's shop servers. It fails for work that waits.

<Compare
  columns={["Signal", "Rises when", "Fits", "Misses"]}
  rows={[
    ["Processor use", "Servers compute harder", "App servers like the shop's", "Work that waits on slow calls or holds connections"],
    ["Requests per server", "More requests arrive at the balancer", "Web tiers with similar requests", "Requests that cost very different amounts"],
    ["Queue backlog per worker", "Messages wait longer", "Workers that read a queue", "Work that does not go through a queue"],
    ["Open connections per server", "Sockets pile up", "Real-time servers: Brightpost adds one above 600", "Short requests"],
  ]}
  caption="Pick the number that goes up first when users wait."
/>

Paid orders go to a queue, a list of messages waiting to be handled. Receipt workers make a receipt for each order and email it. Each email call waits about 1 second, so a worker sits near 15% processor use however long the queue grows. A processor rule would never add a worker.

So the workers scale on backlog per worker. One worker clears about 2 orders a second, and a receipt should arrive within 300 seconds. One worker can own 2 × 300 = 600 waiting orders, so 1,800 waiting means 3 workers. Kubernetes runs this same loop for containers; see [Kubernetes Concepts](/cloud/compute/kubernetes-concepts).

## On AWS, Google Cloud and Azure

The loop is the same on all three clouds: a group of identical servers, a rule on a metric, two limits, and settings for the wait.

<Tabs title="On each cloud">
  <Tab label="AWS">
    - **Group:** an EC2 Auto Scaling group with a minimum, a maximum and a desired count. A launch template says what each new instance looks like.
    - **Rules:** target tracking, step scaling and simple scaling on CloudWatch metrics: average CPU, the load balancer's request count per target, or a custom metric. A default instance warm-up keeps new instances out of the average.
    - **Schedules and prediction:** scheduled actions change the limits or the desired count at a time or on a recurring schedule. Predictive scaling forecasts from past load and launches instances ahead of it.
    - **Warm capacity:** a warm pool of instances kept stopped, running or hibernated beside the group.
    - **Scale-in controls:** instance scale-in protection, termination policies that choose which instance leaves, and lifecycle hooks. A group attached to a target group waits for its deregistration delay, the drain, before it terminates.

    **The difference that changes a design.** Lifecycle hooks make both ends of the loop yours. A launching instance waits in a hook until your script says the app is ready; a leaving one waits until it has finished its work. Warm-pool instances pass through the same hooks. Target tracking also scales in more gradually than it scales out, by design.
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Google Cloud">
    - **Group:** a managed instance group (MIG) built from an instance template, zonal or regional. Its autoscaler keeps the size between a minimum and a maximum number of instances.
    - **Rules:** a target for average CPU use, for load-balancing serving capacity, or for a Cloud Monitoring metric. A Pub/Sub subscription's backlog is one such metric.
    - **Schedules and prediction:** scaling schedules hold a minimum number of instances for a time window. Predictive autoscaling forecasts from past load, for the CPU signal.
    - **Warm capacity:** an initialisation period tells the autoscaler how long a new VM needs before its numbers count. A group can also keep a standby pool of stopped or suspended VMs to resume.
    - **Scale-in controls:** scale-in controls cap how many VMs may go within a time window you choose. A stabilisation period sizes scale-in for the peak of the last several minutes. A mode that only scales out turns scale-in off.

    **The difference that changes a design.** One autoscaler holds every signal. It works out a size for each signal and uses the largest. So a schedule is one more signal that acts as a floor, while CPU or requests can still push the group higher.
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Azure">
    - **Group:** Virtual Machine Scale Sets, with an autoscale setting in Azure Monitor that holds a minimum, a maximum and a default count.
    - **Rules:** a scale-out rule and a scale-in rule. Each has a metric, a time window, a threshold, an action such as "add 2", and a cooldown in minutes. The metric can come from the VMs or from another resource, such as a Service Bus queue's message count.
    - **Schedules and prediction:** profiles. Default, recurrence (every weekday evening) and fixed-date (a sale day) profiles each carry their own limits and rules. Predictive autoscale forecasts CPU load for scale sets from past days.
    - **Warm capacity:** standby pools keep VMs created ahead of time, stopped or running, ready to join a scale set.
    - **Scale-in controls:** a scale-in policy picks which VM goes first, the newest or the oldest, and instance protection keeps chosen VMs from removal.

    **The difference that changes a design.** Scale-out and scale-in are separate rules, so keeping their thresholds apart is your job. Before a scale-in, autoscale checks whether the smaller count would trip the scale-out rule at once. If it would, it skips the scale-in. That stops the 75%-to-38% loop of Step 4, not Brightpost's waves. For containers, Azure Container Apps scales replicas on KEDA-based rules, such as HTTP traffic or queue length, down to zero.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

/>

Speed costs money when nobody is shopping. Pay for running spares only before a rush you can name, and let the pool and the image cover the rest.

## Common pitfalls

- **Scale-in as fast as scale-out.** Removing servers 2 minutes after a wave left Brightpost short for the next one. Wait 15 minutes or more, and remove one server at a time.
- **Removing servers without draining.** Open requests die, and new ones keep arriving until the health checks notice. Let the group drain each server for longer than your slowest request.
- **Processor use for work that waits.** Receipt workers and socket servers sit at 15% while users wait. Scale on backlog per worker or on open connections.
- **Warm-up shorter than the real start.** The loop counts booting servers and adds more than it needs. Measure the start, about 4 minutes at Brightpost, and set warm-up to that.
- **Waiting for the rule before a known rush.** A reactive rule is always minutes late. Schedule servers before the sale or the newsletter.

## How to say it in an interview

"I treat autoscaling as a control loop: measure a metric, compare it with a target, change the count, then wait for warm-up. I pick the metric that rises first when users wait: processor use for web servers, backlog per worker for queue workers, open connections for sockets. A minimum keeps the service up, and a maximum stops a bug from renting servers forever. I scale out fast and in slowly, one server at a time, with draining, which prevents flapping. New servers take minutes, so I schedule capacity before known rushes and keep a warm pool for the rest."

## Interview questions

<InterviewQuestions>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is target tracking, in your own words?" level="Junior" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** You give the autoscaler one metric and a value to stay near, such as 60% processor use. The service works out how many servers would bring the metric back to that value, and changes the count.

    **Why.** It works like a thermostat: you set the temperature, not how long the heater runs. At Brightpost, one server carries about 120 shoppers at 60%. A wave of 700 needs about 6 servers, so the autoscaler adds 4 to the 2 it has. Warm-up keeps booting servers out of the average.

    <AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-loop" step={1} highlight={["servers12", "metrics", "autoscaler"]} caption="The servers report a metric, and the autoscaler compares the average with the target." />

    **Follow-ups the interviewer may ask.**
    - Why aim for 60% and not 90%?
    - What happens if the metric stops arriving?

    **Watch out for.** Saying the autoscaler reacts at once. It works from metrics that are a minute old, and new servers take minutes to start.
  </InterviewQuestion>

  <InterviewQuestion question="Why scale queue workers on queue depth rather than CPU?" level="Mid" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** Because the queue is where the waiting shows up. A worker that waits on another service uses little processor, so a CPU rule never fires. Scale on backlog per worker: messages waiting divided by workers, against what one worker clears in your time limit.

    **Why.** Brightpost's receipt workers wait about 1 second on each email call, so they sit near 15% processor use. One worker clears about 2 orders a second, and a receipt should arrive within 5 minutes. So one worker can own 600 waiting orders, and 1,800 waiting asks for 3 workers.

    <AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-loop" step={7} highlight={["queue", "autoscaler", "workers"]} caption="The queue's backlog, not processor use, drives the worker count." />

    **Follow-ups the interviewer may ask.**
    - The email service slows to 2 seconds a call. What happens to the target?
    - What stops more workers from overloading the service they call?

    **Watch out for.** Scaling on the total queue length with one threshold, such as "add 1 above 1,000". It adds the same one worker at 1,001 as at 30,000.
  </InterviewQuestion>

  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you remove servers from a web tier without dropping requests?" level="Mid" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** Drain each server before it stops, remove servers slowly and one at a time, and protect servers that are doing long work. Keep no user state on the server, so any server can finish a shopper's visit.

    **Why.** Brightpost's group once removed 4 servers at once with no drain, and about 15 checkouts failed. Now the balancer stops sending a leaving server new requests and gives open ones up to 60 seconds; Pay takes about 4. Scale-in waits 15 quiet minutes and removes one server per decision. Baskets live in a shared database, so a shopper's next click works on any server.

    <AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-loop" step={4} path="autoscaler>servers36>lb" caption="The autoscaler picks one server, the balancer drains it, and only then does it stop." />

    **Follow-ups the interviewer may ask.**
    - What about WebSocket connections that never finish?
    - What if a job runs longer than the drain timeout?

    **Watch out for.** Relying on health checks to notice. A health check finds a dead server after about 30 seconds of failed requests. A drain avoids those failures entirely.
  </InterviewQuestion>

  <InterviewQuestion question="Your fleet scales out and in every few minutes all day. Diagnose it and fix it." level="Senior" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** That is flapping. I would plot the scaling history next to the metric and the incoming load, then look for one of three causes. The thresholds are too close, scale-in or warm-up is too short, or the load comes in waves shorter than the start-up lag.

    **Why.** Thresholds of +2 above 70% and −2 below 40% loop by themselves: 2 servers at 75% become 4 at about 38%, and back. A warm-up shorter than the real start counts booting servers and overshoots. In Brightpost's case, a 2-minute scale-in removed 4 servers minutes before each next wave. The fixes match the causes: one target instead of a pair, 15 minutes before scaling in, warm-up set to the measured start, and schedules for waves you can predict.

    <AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-loop" step={3} highlight={["autoscaler", "servers36", "nextwave"]} caption="Removed two minutes after one wave, the servers are missing for the next." />

    **Follow-ups the interviewer may ask.**
    - How do you show that the fix worked?
    - What does flapping cost apart from money?

    **Watch out for.** Raising the minimum to the peak to make it stop. The flapping ends, but you pay for peak capacity all day: 10 servers all year is 87,600 server-hours, against about 10,000 used.
  </InterviewQuestion>

  <InterviewQuestion question="A scale-out takes six minutes and the sale lasts five. What are your options?" level="Senior" category="Cloud">
    **Short answer.** A reactive rule cannot help in time, so have the capacity before the sale or shorten the lag. In order: schedule servers ahead of the start, keep a warm pool, shrink the start time, and leave headroom in the target. Then protect the back end from what still gets through.

    **Why.** Six minutes is a minute of metric, the decision and about 4 minutes of start. Servers that arrive after a 5-minute sale serve nobody and are paid for anyway. Brightpost schedules 10 servers from 8:30 on sale days. A warm pool cuts the start to about a minute. Aiming at 60% leaves room for a jump of about 1.6 times before servers are full.

    <AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/cloud/autoscaling-1" step={3} caption="A schedule removes the wait; a warm pool cuts it from five and a half minutes to two and a half." />

    **Follow-ups the interviewer may ask.**
    - What does the schedule cost on a day the sale is cancelled?
    - How do you protect the database when 10 servers start at once?

    **Watch out for.** Raising the maximum. The maximum is not the limit here; time is.
  </InterviewQuestion>
</InterviewQuestions>
