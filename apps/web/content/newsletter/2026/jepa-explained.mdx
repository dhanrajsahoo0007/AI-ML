## Why this matters

A toddler knocks a cup off a table and watches it fall. After a few tries they stop being surprised. Nobody
explained gravity to them, and they can't read yet. They learned how the world behaves by watching it.

A large language model (LLM) learns the other way round. It reads a huge library of text and learns to guess the
next word. Everything it knows about cups and tables, it picked up from sentences about them.

Yann LeCun thinks that second route has a ceiling. He is one of the researchers who made modern AI possible, and
he spent years as Meta's chief AI scientist. In 2022 he proposed a different design called JEPA. In 2026 he
co-founded a company, AMI Labs, to chase the same idea. It raised $1.03 billion before it had a product.

You'll hear the word JEPA more often from here on. This issue explains what it is and how it learns. It also
separates what has been shown from what is still a bet.

## The idea in plain English

Pause a film halfway through a penalty kick and ask a friend what happens next. They'll say "the ball goes in
the net" or "the keeper saves it". They won't paint the next frame, blade of grass by blade of grass. They
predict the gist and skip the rest.

That is the whole idea. JEPA stands for Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture. In plain words: turn each thing
you see into a short summary, then predict one summary from another.

![An LLM predicts the exact next word, a JEPA predicts a summary of what comes next](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/jepa-explained/word-vs-world)

An LLM has to commit to one exact next word. A JEPA commits only to a summary. That summary is a list of numbers
called an **embedding**. Inputs that mean similar things get similar numbers.

> [!NOTE]
> **In plain English:** an LLM fills in the blank with the exact missing word. A JEPA fills in the blank with
> "something like this", and is graded on whether the meaning is right.

## How it works: how an LLM learns

Start with what you already use. An LLM's training is one small game, played billions of times.

![An LLM learns in four steps: read, guess the next word, check, adjust](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/jepa-explained/llm-training)

The model reads some text, guesses the next word and checks itself against the real one. Then its **weights**,
the numbers inside the model, are nudged so the right word becomes a little likelier. Strictly, it works on
word-pieces called tokens, but "word" is close enough here.

Two things make this work so well. The answer key is free, because the next word is already there in the text.
And language has a fixed list of pieces, so the model can give every possible next piece a probability.

The second point is where pictures cause trouble. A video frame has no fixed list of possible answers. There are
endless ways the next frame could look, and nearly all of them differ in details that don't matter.

## How it works: how a JEPA learns

A JEPA plays a similar hide-and-guess game, but it changes what counts as the answer. The first working version,
I-JEPA, plays it with images.

![A JEPA hides part of a picture and predicts the summary of the hidden part](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/jepa-explained/jepa-training#steps)

Here are the four steps again, a little slower.

1. **Hide.** The picture is cut into small squares called patches. The model is shown one block of them. Several
   other blocks are held back as targets.
2. **Encode.** An **encoder** turns the visible patches into embeddings: a summary of what it can see.
3. **Predict.** A smaller network, the **predictor**, takes that summary and the positions of the hidden blocks.
   It guesses the summary of each one.
4. **Compare.** A second encoder, the **target encoder**, looks at the whole picture and produces the real
   summaries. Training shrinks the gap between the guess and the real thing.

Notice what never happens. The model is never asked to draw the hidden patches. Its score depends only on the
distance between two summaries.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Key idea:** in a JEPA, both the guess and the answer are summaries. The model is free to leave out anything
> it can't predict, because nobody asks it for the pixels.

Nobody labels the pictures, either. As with an LLM, the answer key comes free from the data. The I-JEPA team
trained a large image model this way in under 72 hours on 16 GPUs. They report that it took over ten times less
computing than a well-known method that rebuilds pixels.

## How it works: why predict meaning

Imagine a clip of a ball rolling under a tree on a windy day. Now predict the next second.

![Predicting pixels means every leaf and shadow, predicting meaning keeps only the ball](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/jepa-explained/ball-and-leaves)

A model that must produce pixels has to decide where every leaf ends up. That can't be done, because the wind is
random. So it spends most of its effort on detail that has nothing to do with the ball.

A model that predicts a summary can drop the leaves. Where the ball goes is predictable, so that stays. The
exact flutter of leaf 4,012 isn't, so it goes.

The I-JEPA paper tested this directly. The authors swapped the target from summaries back to pixels and kept
everything else the same. The model got clearly worse at recognising what was in images.

> [!NOTE]
> **In plain English:** a weather forecaster says "rain tomorrow afternoon". They don't predict where each drop
> lands. Leaving that out is what makes the forecast possible.

## How it works: the collapse problem

There's a catch, and it's the hardest part of making a JEPA work. The model writes its own answer key. So it can
cheat.

![Healthy embeddings spread out, collapsed embeddings all sit on one point](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/jepa-explained/collapse)

The cheat is simple. Give every input the same summary. A cat, a truck and a blank wall all become the same list
of numbers. The predictor is now right every time, and the model has learned nothing. Researchers call this
**collapse**.

An LLM can't cheat this way. Its answer key is the real next word, which the model doesn't control.

There are two main fixes so far.

- **A slow target.** In I-JEPA and V-JEPA the target encoder isn't trained directly. It is a slow-moving average
  of the main encoder. The answer key always lags behind the learner, which makes the cheat hard to reach.
- **A spread-out rule.** LeJEPA, from November 2025, adds one term to the training goal. It pushes the summaries
  to stay spread out in every direction. The authors say this removes the slow target and several other tricks,
  and that the core fits in about 50 lines of code.

> [!WARNING]
> **Reading "low training error" as "learned something".** A collapsed model has a perfect score. Fix: test the
> summaries on a real task, such as sorting images into classes, before trusting the number.

## How it works: the family

JEPA isn't one model. It's a recipe, and each paper applies it to a harder kind of data.

![The JEPA family from the 2022 proposal to AMI Labs in 2026](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/jepa-explained/family-timeline)

- **2022, the proposal.** LeCun's position paper, "A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence", describes
  JEPA as the centre of a larger design for machines that can plan.
- **2023, I-JEPA.** The first working version, for still images.
- **2024, V-JEPA.** The same game on video, trained on 2 million clips. It used no text and no labels.
- **2025, V-JEPA 2.** Trained on over 1 million hours of internet video, then taught to plan robot moves.
- **2025, LeJEPA.** A simpler and more stable way to train the whole family.
- **2026, AMI Labs.** LeCun's new company. Its site doesn't use the name JEPA, but it describes the same recipe:
  learn abstract summaries of sensor data and predict in that space.

## A worked example

V-JEPA 2 is the clearest picture of what the idea is for. Here is how it gets a robot arm to pick up a cup and
put it somewhere else.

First the model watched over 1 million hours of ordinary internet video. That taught it to summarise scenes.
Then the team added a second predictor and trained it on under 62 hours of robot video. This one learned a new
question: "if the arm moves like this, what will the summary look like next?"

After that the robot got no task training at all. It was handed one thing: a photo of the goal.

![A robot imagines three moves and picks the one whose result is nearest the goal](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/jepa-explained/plan-in-latent)

Each step runs like this.

1. Turn the camera view into a summary. Do the same for the goal photo.
2. Make up a batch of candidate moves. The real system tries 800 at a time and refines them ten times.
3. For each move, ask the predictor what summary would follow.
4. Pick the move whose imagined summary sits closest to the goal's.
5. Do only the first bit of that move. Look again and start over.

No picture is ever generated. All the imagining happens in summaries, which is why it's fast: about 16 seconds
of thinking per move. The paper reports about 4 minutes for a rival approach that generates video.

The team tested it on arms in two labs the model had never seen. It moved a cup to the right place in 80% of
trials, and a box in 65%. Reaching a point worked every time. Grasping was the weak spot: 65% for the cup and
25% for the box.

> [!NOTE]
> **In plain English:** you plan a route across a room by thinking "past the chair, round the table". You don't
> picture every floorboard. The robot plans the same way.

The paper lists its own limits. The planner is sensitive to where the camera sits. Errors pile up on long tasks.
Goals have to be photos, so you can't yet tell it what to do in words.

## How it works: side by side

Put the two next to each other and the difference in purpose is plain.

![LLM and JEPA compared on what they learn from, predict, output, do well and where they're used](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/jepa-explained/llm-vs-jepa)

The row to remember is "Gives you". An LLM's output is text, which a person can read straight away. A JEPA's
output is a summary in numbers. It's only useful when something else reads it, such as a planner or a
classifier.

That is why there's no JEPA chatbot to try. A JEPA is closer to a sense of sight than to a voice.

## What goes wrong

> [!WARNING]
> **Calling JEPA "a better ChatGPT".** It doesn't write text and there's nothing to chat with. Fix: describe it
> as a way of training models to understand and predict, mostly from images and video.

> [!WARNING]
> **Stating "LLMs have no world model" as fact.** That's one side of a live argument, and there's evidence
> against it. Fix: say what has been measured, and call the rest an open question.

> [!WARNING]
> **Treating a research bet as a product.** The robot results are real but early: two labs, short tasks, 80% at
> best. Fix: follow the papers, and don't plan a roadmap around something you can't buy yet.

## The bigger picture

The disagreement is about what intelligence needs.

**LeCun's side.** Text is a thin slice of what people know. Most of what a child learns comes through eyes and
hands, long before words. A system that only predicts text will always lack that grounding, however large it
gets. AMI Labs puts it in one line on its site: "real intelligence does not start in language. It starts in the
world." On this view you first need a model of how the world behaves. Language comes on top.

**The other side.** LLMs may be building world models already, without being asked to. In one 2022 study,
researchers trained a small GPT-style model only to predict legal moves in the board game Othello. Inside it
they found a working picture of the board. A 2023 study found that Llama-2 models hold a map-like sense of where
places are and when events happened. The authors called their results suggestive, not conclusive.

And the scoreboard favours LLMs today. They're in products used by hundreds of millions of people. JEPA's best
robot results come from two labs. Meta built new tests of physical reasoning alongside V-JEPA 2, and says people
score 85% to 95% on them. Top models, V-JEPA 2 included, fall well short.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Key idea:** nobody disputes that JEPA works on images and video. The open question is whether predicting
> text alone can ever build the same understanding of the world.

**Where it may end up.** The two ideas don't exclude each other. A JEPA could be the part that watches and
predicts, and a language model the part that talks and reasons in words. V-JEPA 2's own authors list language
goals as future work, which points the same way.

Hype is a risk too. AMI's chief executive has predicted that within months every company will call itself a
world-model company to raise money. So judge the claims by the checks in this issue. What does the model
predict? In what space? What stops it collapsing? What was measured?

## Try it yourself

> [!TIP]
> Film five seconds of anything: a kettle, a street, a pet. Stop at second three and write one sentence about
> what happens next. Then count what you left out: colours, textures, the background. Your sentence is roughly
> what a JEPA predicts. Everything you left out is what a pixel model would have to get right.

## Words to know

| Term             | Plain meaning                                                                 |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| LLM              | A model trained to predict the next word in text                              |
| JEPA             | A way of training that predicts the summary of a hidden part, not its details |
| Embedding        | A summary of an input, stored as a list of numbers                            |
| Encoder          | The part that turns an input into an embedding                                |
| Predictor        | The part that guesses the embedding of something it hasn't seen               |
| Target encoder   | A second encoder that supplies the real embedding to compare against          |
| Patch            | One small square of an image                                                  |
| Collapse         | The failure where every input gets the same embedding                         |
| World model      | A model that predicts what happens next, given what you do                    |
| Generative model | A model that produces the content itself: words, pixels or sound              |
