## Why this matters

Picture a team with a support bot that keeps getting the tone wrong. They decide to fine-tune. They spend two
weeks collecting examples, two weeks cleaning them and two weeks training and testing. The tuned model is clearly
better. They ship it.

A month later a new base model comes out. Someone tries it with the old plain prompt, just to see. It beats the
tuned model. Six weeks of work now trails a model that needed none.

This story is common enough that people now ask whether fine-tuning is dead. There's fresh evidence for that
view. In May 2026 OpenAI began closing its self-serve fine-tuning to new customers.

The honest answer is no, but it has been demoted. Fine-tuning used to be the first thing teams reached for. Today
it's the last, and it wins in a much smaller set of cases. This issue shows which cases, and how to tell if yours
is one of them.

## The idea in plain English

Think of a new hire. There are three ways to get better work out of them.

- **A briefing.** You explain the task clearly and show two or three good examples. It takes minutes.
- **A reference binder.** You hand over the documents they need and let them look things up. It takes days to
  set up.
- **Months of training.** You send them on a course until the skill becomes habit. It takes weeks or more.

An AI model has the same three options, with different names.

- **A prompt** is the briefing: the instructions and examples you send with each request.
- **Retrieval** is the binder: a search step that finds the right documents and puts them in the prompt. You'll
  often see it called RAG, short for retrieval-augmented generation.
- **Fine-tuning** is the training: extra learning on your own examples, which changes the model itself.

![Three ways to change a model: prompt, retrieval, fine-tuning](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/is-fine-tuning-dead/three-ways)

You wouldn't send a new hire on a three-month course before explaining the task. The same order applies here.
Each step costs more than the one before it, and each one is harder to undo.

> [!NOTE]
> **In plain English:** a prompt changes what you ask. Retrieval changes what the model can read. Fine-tuning
> changes the model. Use the cheapest one that works.

## How it works: what fine-tuning changes

A language model is a very large pile of numbers called **weights**. They are set during training, and together
they decide what the model writes. A large model has billions of them.

Fine-tuning is more training, on a much smaller set of examples that you choose. Each example is an input paired
with the output you want. The model reads them and its weights shift a little toward your kind of answer.

The old way, **full fine-tuning**, adjusts every weight. That's slow and costly, and it leaves you with a full
copy of the model to store and run.

The common way today is lighter. The best-known method is called **LoRA**, short for low-rank adaptation. It
leaves the original weights alone and trains a small add-on beside them.

![What fine-tuning changes, step by step](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/is-fine-tuning-dead/what-changes#steps)

The add-on is called an **adapter**. The 2021 LoRA paper measured the saving against full fine-tuning of GPT-3.
It trained up to 10,000 times fewer numbers and needed about a third of the GPU memory. The adapter can also be
merged back into the model, so answers come out just as fast.

That's why a fine-tune no longer needs a research team. On a small open model, a training run can take an
afternoon on one rented GPU.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Key idea:** the training run got cheap. Everything around it didn't. Hold that thought until the iceberg
> below.

## How it works: the moving baseline

Here is the problem the team in the opening story ran into.

A fine-tune is a snapshot. You take one base model on one day and train it. From then on your tuned model stays
exactly as good as it was. The base models around it keep improving.

![The moving baseline: a flat fine-tune line and a rising staircase of base models](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/is-fine-tuning-dead/moving-baseline)

The chart is a sketch, not data. But the pattern is well documented. In November 2023 a Microsoft research team
published a study known as Medprompt. They took GPT-4, a general model with no medical training of its own, and
used careful prompting alone. It beat models that had been specially tuned for medicine, including Google's
Med-PaLM 2. On one standard set of medical exam questions it cut the error rate by 27% and passed 90% for the
first time.

The lesson isn't that prompts always win. It's that a tuned model competes with every future base model, and you
don't control when the next one arrives.

There's a second cost hiding here. Your adapter is tied to the base model it was trained on. When that model is
retired, your fine-tune goes with it. Moving to a newer model means training again, and testing again.

## How it works: the hosted door is closing

For years the easy route was to fine-tune a hosted model. You uploaded a file of examples, clicked a button and
got back a private version of the model. That route is narrowing.

![OpenAI's three wind-down dates for self-serve fine-tuning](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/is-fine-tuning-dead/hosted-winddown)

OpenAI's guide now says it plainly: the company is winding down its fine-tuning platform. The dates come from its
own deprecations page. New organisations lost access on May 7, 2026. Inactive ones followed on July 2. From
January 6, 2027 no customer can start a new training job.

Models that are already tuned keep working, but only until their base model is retired. For GPT-4.1 nano that
date was October 23, 2026. For GPT-4.1 and GPT-4.1 mini it's January 14, 2027.

The price list tells the same story. On November 10, 2026, OpenAI listed GPT-4.1 at $2.00 per million input
tokens. A fine-tuned GPT-4.1 was listed at $3.00. So a tuned large model costs half as much again on every
request, before you count the training.

One company closing one product doesn't end a technique. What it does show is where fine-tuning now lives. It has
moved away from large hosted models and toward small **open-weight** models: ones you can download, train and run
on your own machines.

## How it works: where it still wins

So when is the training worth it? Five jobs keep coming up.

![Five jobs where fine-tuning still wins](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/is-fine-tuning-dead/where-it-wins)

1. **A strict output format.** Every reply must fit the same structure, and a prompt gets it right most of the
   time but not all of it.
2. **A fixed house style.** A voice that's easy to recognise and hard to describe. Examples in a prompt get
   close, and hundreds of training examples get closer.
3. **Narrow sorting at volume.** Putting messages into a fixed set of categories, millions of times.
4. **A small, cheap, fast model.** A small model that's weak in general can be trained to do one job well. It then
   runs at a fraction of the cost, and often faster.
5. **Data that must stay in-house.** If your data can't leave your own servers, a hosted model is out. A small
   open model that you tune and run yourself may be the only choice.

Look at what the five share. Each is one narrow job, done the same way every time. None of them is "make the
model smarter" or "teach the model our facts".

That second one deserves a closer look, because it's the most common mistake. Fine-tuning is a poor way to add
knowledge. Training nudges how a model behaves. It doesn't store your documents like a database. A model tuned on
last year's price list will state last year's prices with full confidence. Facts that change belong in retrieval,
where you can update a document in seconds.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Key idea:** fine-tune for behaviour, retrieve for facts. If the thing you want changes weekly, don't train it
> in.

## How it works: the real cost

The training run is the part people price. It's also the smallest part.

![The real cost of a fine-tune, drawn as an iceberg](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/is-fine-tuning-dead/true-cost)

Take OpenAI's list price as a guide. Training on GPT-4.1 was listed at $25 per million tokens. A thousand
examples of 500 tokens each, read three times, is 1.5 million tokens. That's under $40. On an open model with a
rented GPU, the bill is often about the same size.

Now look below the water.

- **Collecting and labelling examples.** Someone has to find hundreds of good input and output pairs and check
  each one. This is usually weeks of a skilled person's time.
- **Building the eval set.** You need a test that tells you whether the tuned model is better. [Issue
  #3](/newsletter/evals-for-llm-apps) covers how to build one.
- **Hosting the tuned model.** If you run it yourself, you pay for a GPU around the clock, and someone must keep
  it running.
- **Watching for drift.** Your users and products change. A model trained on last spring's messages slowly gets
  worse on this autumn's.
- **Redoing it for each new base model.** Every upgrade means a new training run and a new round of tests.

A prompt has almost none of these costs. That's the real reason to try it first.

## A worked example

Let's put numbers on the strongest case: narrow sorting at volume.

A bank gets customer messages and must send each one to the right team. "My card hasn't arrived" goes to one
team. "Why was I charged twice?" goes to another. There are 77 possible categories, and the bank handles about 2
million messages a month.

In September 2026 the engineer Azad Djan published a careful test of exactly this job. He used a public dataset
called Banking77, with about 10,000 training messages and 3,080 test messages. He compared four ways of doing the
work and reported the accuracy and the running cost of each. The score is a standard accuracy measure called
macro F1, where 1.0 is perfect.

![Four ways to sort 2 million messages a month, with score and cost](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/is-fine-tuning-dead/routing-math)

The scores are Djan's. He reported the prompted models' costs per million messages, and we've doubled them for 2
million. The two fixed costs are his monthly figures, unchanged.

Three things stand out.

**The biggest model wasn't the best.** Claude Opus 5 and Claude Haiku 4.5 scored the same, near 0.90, when each
was shown ten examples of every category. Opus cost about seven times as much. On a narrow job, extra
intelligence bought nothing.

**The small tuned model won on both counts.** ModernBERT is tiny by today's standards, with 395 million weights.
Trained on the 10,000 examples, it scored 0.933. It runs on a single GPU for about $584 a month, however many
messages arrive. Against Haiku, it pays for itself above roughly 270,000 messages a month.

**The cheapest option had no language model in it at all.** An old method that counts words and draws lines
between categories scored 0.913. It beat both prompted models, for about $12 a month.

That last row is the most useful one. Before you fine-tune anything, check whether a much simpler tool does the
job.

> [!NOTE]
> **In plain English:** these are the results of one test on one dataset, and they count running costs only. The
> labelled examples, the eval work and the engineer's time aren't in the table. They're the part of the iceberg
> below the water.

## How it works: the decision

Put the whole issue into one picture and you get four questions.

![Should you fine-tune? Four questions in order](asset:diagrams/svg/newsletter/is-fine-tuning-dead/decision-tree)

1. **Got an eval set?** If you can't measure better or worse, stop and build one. OpenAI's own guide says to
   invest in fine-tuning only after setting up evals.
2. **Missing facts?** If the model fails because it lacks knowledge, use retrieval. Training won't fix it.
3. **Prompt done properly?** Clear rules, a few good examples, and a format to follow. Many "we need to
   fine-tune" problems end here.
4. **Narrow and high volume?** If the job is broad, or small, the cost of owning a model won't pay back. Keep
   prompting.

Only a job that passes all four should reach fine-tuning. Even then, choose a small model, and plan to test it
against every new base model that arrives.

## What goes wrong

> [!WARNING]
> **Fine-tuning to add facts.** The tuned model sounds sure of itself and is out of date within weeks. Fix: keep
> facts in documents and use retrieval. Train only behaviour.

> [!WARNING]
> **No eval set.** The team "feels" the tuned model is better and has no numbers. Fix: build the test first, and
> score the plain prompt on it before you train anything.

> [!WARNING]
> **Too little data, or messy data.** OpenAI's guide allows as few as 10 examples and suggests starting with 50.
> For a hard job you'll need far more, and one wrong label teaches the wrong habit. Fix: read every example
> yourself before training on it.

> [!WARNING]
> **Forgetting the upgrade cost.** The fine-tune ships, the base model is retired a year later, and nobody
> planned for the rebuild. Fix: keep the training data, the script and the eval set together, so a re-train is
> one command.

## The bigger picture

Fine-tuning is turning into a tool for making models cheaper, not smarter.

The clearest sign is **distillation**. You take a large, expensive model and have it answer thousands of real
requests. Then you train a small model on those answers. The small model learns to copy the large one on that
single job. You get most of the quality at a fraction of the running cost.

The pieces also combine. A system can retrieve the facts, prompt for the task, and use a small tuned model for the
one step that runs a million times a day. These aren't rival camps. They're three tools, and a mature system
often uses all three.

So the question has changed. It used to be "how do we fine-tune a model for our company?". Now it's "which single
step is narrow enough, and busy enough, to deserve its own small model?". For most teams the answer is none. For
a few it's worth a great deal.

## Words to know

| Term             | Plain meaning                                                                |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Weights          | The numbers inside a model that decide what it writes                        |
| Base model       | The general model you start from, before any of your own training            |
| Fine-tuning      | Extra training on your own examples, which changes the model's weights       |
| Full fine-tuning | Adjusting every weight in the model                                          |
| LoRA             | A light method that freezes the model and trains a small add-on beside it    |
| Adapter          | That small add-on: the only part that learns                                 |
| Retrieval (RAG)  | Searching your documents and putting the results into the prompt             |
| Open weights     | A model you can download and run on your own machines                        |
| Distillation     | Training a small model to copy a large one on a single job                   |
| Drift            | The slow drop in quality as real inputs move away from the training examples |
