The cache and the shards are in place, then one product goes viral and one node melts while the others idle.

- Interviewers ask about hot keys to see whether you notice that hashing spreads keys, not traffic, and whether you can protect one key without redesigning the system.

## Overview

- A **hot key** is one key that takes a large share of the traffic: a celebrity's profile, a product on sale, a global counter.
- **Skew** shows up as one shard, one cache node or one database row at its limit while the rest sit idle. **Adding shards does not help**: the key still hashes to one place.
- Read-hot keys: **request coalescing** (single flight) turns many identical misses into one fetch; an **in-process cache** with a 1 s TTL absorbs the rest; **salted copies** `key#1..N` spread what is left.
- Write-hot keys: **sharded counters** split one row into N rows; **buffered increments** batch many writes into one per second.
- **Detect** hot keys before they hurt: count requests per key at the proxy or client, or sample the cache's access frequencies.

<Callout tone="violet" title="Interview edge: how this sets a senior answer apart" icon="sparkles">
  - **What most candidates say:** "Shard the products by id and put Redis in front."
  - **Why the interviewer pushes back:** "One product takes a third of all reads at noon. Which node serves it, and what happens to that node?"
  - **The answer that stands out:** coalesce concurrent misses with single flight, cache the hot key in each server's memory for one second, and salt it across several cache nodes if that is not enough. Name the cost: up to one second of staleness and N copies to invalidate.
</Callout>

## Mental model

- Think of a bakery with four counters where every customer is free to queue at any of them.
- On a normal day the queues even out. Then one cake goes viral, and only counter 2 sells it.
- Opening more counters does not help: the cake is still at counter 2.
- The fixes are to put a tray of that cake at every counter (an in-process cache or salted copies) and to have one baker refill the tray while the queue waits (single flight).

## Core concepts

- The running example is Brightpost's flash sale: at 12:00 the kettle's page takes **30 % of 2,300 reads/s = 690 reads/s**, all on one key, served by **40 app servers**; orders peak at **300/s**, every one of them a kettle.

### How skew shows up

- Hashing gives each shard about the same number of keys. It does nothing about how often each key is read.
- So one shard (or cache node, or row) runs at its limit while the others have room to spare.

**How to read it:**

- Each bar is one shard against an illustrative limit of 1,000 reads/s.
- Shard 1 holds the kettle: its orange part alone is 690 reads/s, and the red part is what it cannot serve.

**The numbers:**

- Kettle: 0.3 × 2,300 = 690 reads/s. Other keys: 2,300 − 690 = 1,610 reads/s, about 403 per shard.
- Shard 1: 690 + 403 = 1,093 reads/s against 1,000. Shards 2 to 4: 403 each, 40 % of their limit.
- Doubling to 8 shards leaves shard 1 at 690 + 201 = 891 reads/s: still hot, because the kettle cannot be split by adding shards.

### Request coalescing (single flight)

- When a hot key is missing from the cache, every request for it misses at the same moment.
- **Single flight** makes the first request the leader; the others wait on the leader's call and share its result.
- It works per process (Go's `singleflight` package) or per key across a fleet when requests for one key are routed to one place (Discord routes by key to its data services, then coalesces).

### Distributed counters

- A counter on one row (likes, views, stock) is a **write-hot key**: every increment locks the same row.
- **Sharded counters:** keep N rows for one counter, add to a random one, and read the sum. N rows take N times the writes.
- **Buffered increments:** each server adds in memory and writes the total once a second. One write replaces hundreds.
- Both trade exactness for throughput: the sum is a little behind, and a crashed server loses its unflushed buffer.
- For stock, a buffer must never oversell: reserve stock in blocks per server, or keep the decrement exact and buffer only the display count.

## Architecture

Three defences for the kettle, added one at a time.

## Technical mechanics

### Detecting hot keys

- Count requests per key where they pass: in the client library, the proxy or the cache tier. Sample if counting every request costs too much.
- Keep only the heavy hitters with a top-k sketch (see [Top-K, Heavy Hitters and Leaderboards](/system-design/patterns/top-k-and-leaderboards)).
- Redis can report a key's access frequency with `OBJECT FREQ` when it runs an LFU eviction policy.
- Act on known hot keys ahead of time: a planned flash sale is a hot key you can warm and salt before 12:00.

### Choosing a defence

- **Read-hot, tolerates a second of staleness:** in-process cache with a short TTL. Cheapest and most effective.
- **Read-hot, bursty misses:** single flight, always on; it costs nothing when keys are cold.
- **Read-hot beyond one node even after that:** salted copies, or read replicas of the cache node.
- **Write-hot:** sharded counters or buffered increments. Salting does not help writes that must land in one place.

### Worked example: the kettle at 12:00

At 12:00 the flash sale opens. Follow the kettle's reads and writes through the four steps of the Architecture figure.

1. **No defences.** 690 reads/s hit cache node 1, and when its entry expires, a burst of misses reaches the database.
2. **Single flight plus a 1 s in-process cache.** Each of 40 servers fetches the kettle at most once a second, and concurrent misses inside a server share one fetch.
3. **Salting into 4 copies.** The 40 fetches a second spread over 4 nodes.
4. **Buffered stock writes.** 300 decrements a second become one flush per server per second.

**The numbers:**

- Reads leaving the fleet: 40 servers × 1 per second = 40 reads/s, against 690: 17 times fewer.
- Per cache node after salting: 40 ÷ 4 = 10 reads/s for the kettle.
- Staleness: a price change reaches every shopper within 1 s (the TTL), plus the time to update 4 copies.
- Writes to the stock row: 40 flushes a second of about 300 ÷ 40 = 7.5 each, instead of 300 single writes.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Single flight | Free when cold; kills miss bursts | Only coalesces within one process (or one routed node) | Always, in front of any expensive fetch |
| In-process cache, short TTL | Removes most reads from the network | Up to one TTL of staleness; memory on every server | Read-hot keys that tolerate a second of staleness |
| Salted copies | Spreads one key over N nodes | N copies to write and invalidate | Keys too hot for one node even after local caching |
| Sharded counters | N times the write throughput | Reads sum N rows; slightly behind | Likes, views, any high-rate counter |
| Buffered increments | One write per second per server | Loses a crashed server's buffer; lag | Counters where a few seconds of lag is fine |

## Production systems

- **Go `singleflight`** (as of November 2026): `Group.Do` lets only one call per key run at a time; duplicate callers wait and receive the same result, and `shared` tells them so.
- **Memcache at Facebook** (NSDI 2013): leases hand out at most one token per key every 10 seconds; on keys prone to thundering herds this cut the peak database query rate from 17K/s to 1.3K/s.
- **Discord** (2023 blog, "How Discord Stores Trillions of Messages"): data services between the API and ScyllaDB route requests for the same channel to the same instance with consistent hashing, then coalesce them into one database query.
- **Used in our case studies:** [URL Shortener](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/url-shortener) · [Netflix](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/netflix) · [Twitter](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/twitter) · [Instagram](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/instagram) · [TikTok](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/tiktok)

## Failure modes & pitfalls

- **"Add more shards."** The hot key still lands on one shard. Fix the key, not the shard count.
- **Salting reads but not writes.** Copies drift apart when one update misses a copy. Write all N, or keep a short TTL on each.
- **In-process caches with no TTL.** Servers serve old prices for minutes. Keep the TTL to about a second for hot keys.
- **Single flight with no timeout.** A slow leader makes every waiter slow. Give the leader a deadline and let waiters fall back.
- **Buffered stock counts that oversell.** Reserve stock per server in blocks, and keep the final decrement exact.
- **Finding hot keys during the incident.** Measure per-key traffic all the time and warm planned hot keys before the event.

## Interview framework

1. Name the skew: one key, one shard, and why more shards do not help.
2. For reads: single flight, then an in-process cache with a short TTL, then salted copies; give the read rate after each.
3. For writes: sharded counters or buffered increments, and what they cost in exactness.
4. Say how you detect hot keys and warm planned ones.
5. State the staleness bound you accept.

**What interviewers listen for:** the arithmetic from 690 reads/s to 40, and a staleness bound attached to it.

**Red flags:** "more shards", or a counter that takes every like as a write to one row.

    - Every write must update N copies, or the copies drift and readers see different values.

    - It helps reads only; a value that must be decremented in one place, like stock, still needs a single writer or a sharded counter.
