The "My orders" page joins orders, items and shipments, which live on different shards and in different services, on every page view.

- Interviewers ask about materialized views to see whether you can turn an expensive read into a single lookup, and say how stale the answer is allowed to be.

## Overview

- A **materialized view** stores the precomputed answer to a query, so a read becomes one lookup instead of a join.
- Three common shapes: a **denormalised query table** (one row per order with everything the page shows), an **index table** keyed by another column (orders by customer), and a **projection** built from events.
- It is refreshed **on write** (same transaction), from a **change stream** (seconds behind) or on a **timer** (minutes behind).
- The view is **derived data**: it can always be **rebuilt** from the source, and a new view is **backfilled** by replaying history.
- The cost is **staleness**, **storage** and a **projector** to run.

<Callout tone="violet" title="Interview edge: how this sets a senior answer apart" icon="sparkles">
  - **What most candidates say:** "The My orders page joins orders, items and shipments."
  - **Why the interviewer pushes back:** "Those tables live on different shards and in different services. How many queries is one page view?"
  - **The answer that stands out:** precompute a my_orders view keyed by customer id, fed by the services' change streams, so a page view is one lookup. Say the cost: about a second of staleness, extra storage, and a projector that must be rebuildable from the source.
</Callout>

## Mental model

- A restaurant does not cook every dish from raw when you order: the stock is made in the morning, and the sauce is ready.
- The stock is the view: prepared in advance, kept topped up, and remade from scratch if it goes off.
- The trade is freshness for speed: the morning's stock is a little older than the vegetables delivered at noon.

## Core concepts

- The running example is Brightpost's **My orders** page: **200,000 orders a day**, the page viewed about **400,000 times a day** (two views per order, illustrative), each showing a customer's last **20 orders**.

### Why joins at read time hurt

- Orders are sharded by order id; items and shipments live in their own services.
- One page view for one customer becomes a scatter to every order shard plus a lookup per order in two more services.
- Each query is fast; the page is as slow as the slowest of all of them, and the services carry load for a page that only reads.

### Shapes of a view

- **Query table:** one denormalised row per order, holding the columns the page shows: kettle, £42, "delivered 12 Nov".
- **Index table:** the same data keyed by a different column, such as orders by customer id, so a lookup by customer reads one partition. A global secondary index is a managed index table (see [Sharding and Partitioning](/system-design/patterns/sharding)).
- **Projection:** a view built by folding events, as on the [CQRS and Event Sourcing](/system-design/patterns/cqrs-event-sourcing) page.

**The numbers:**

- Before: 8 + 20 + 20 = 48 queries per page view. After: 1.
- At about 400,000 views a day, that is 19,200,000 queries a day before and 400,000 after.

## Technical mechanics

### Keeping it fresh

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/patterns/mv-staleness" caption="Freshness moves cost: on write charges every write, a stream costs a second of lag, a timer costs minutes." />

**How to read it:**

- The change stream is ringed: it is the usual choice for views that serve users.
- The red cells are where each strategy hurts: on write slows every write and couples it to the view; a timer leaves data minutes old.

**The numbers:**

- A 5-minute timer: data up to 300 s old, 150 s on average. A stream: about 1 s (illustrative). On write: none.

- Inside one database, PostgreSQL's materialized views refresh on demand; `CONCURRENTLY` keeps the view readable during the refresh but needs a unique index:

```sql
CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW my_orders AS
  SELECT o.customer_id, o.id, i.title, o.total, s.status
  FROM orders o JOIN items i ON i.order_id = o.id
  JOIN shipments s ON s.order_id = o.id;

CREATE UNIQUE INDEX my_orders_uidx ON my_orders (id);
REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW CONCURRENTLY my_orders;  -- readers keep reading
```

### Rebuilding and backfilling

- A view is derived data: if it is wrong, drop it and rebuild it from the source or the stream.
- A new view starts with a **backfill** of history, then follows the live stream; switch reads only once it has caught up.
- Keep the projector **idempotent** (apply each change once by version), so a replay or a redelivery cannot double-count.

### Staleness in the product

- After placing an order, a shopper may open My orders before the view has the new row.
- Return the new order in the checkout response and show it directly, or wait for the view to reach the order's version (see [Consistency Models](/system-design/patterns/consistency-models)).

### Worked example: My orders at the sale-day peak

At the peak, My orders is viewed about 5 times its average rate.

1. **Average rate:** 400,000 views ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 4.6 views a second; the peak is about 23 a second.
2. **Read-time join at the peak:** 23 × 48 ≈ 1,100 queries a second spread across three services, every one on the page's critical path.
3. **View at the peak:** 23 lookups a second on one table, by key.
4. **Freshness:** a new order appears in the view about 1 s after checkout, and the checkout page shows it directly in the meantime.

**The numbers:**

- View size: about 146,000,000 orders after two years at about 300 bytes a row (illustrative) is about 44 GB, partitioned by customer id.
- Rebuilding it at an illustrative 50,000 rows a second: 146,000,000 ÷ 50,000 = 2,920 s, under an hour.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Join at read time | Always fresh; no extra storage | Many queries per read; cross-service load | Low traffic, one database |
| View updated on write | Never stale | Slower writes; couples writers to the view | One database, the view must be exact |
| View from a change stream | Fast reads; writers unaffected | About a second of staleness; a projector to run | User-facing pages across services |
| View on a timer | Simple; no stream | Minutes of staleness; refresh cost | Reports, dashboards |
| Index table | Lookup by another key | Two writes or a lagging index | Queries by a non-shard column |

## Production systems

- **PostgreSQL** (as of November 2026): `CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW` stores a query's result; `REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW CONCURRENTLY` keeps it readable during a refresh, needs a unique index and a populated view, and is faster than a plain refresh only when few rows change.
- **DynamoDB**: a global secondary index is a managed index table with its own key, kept up to date asynchronously; reads from it are eventually consistent only.
- **Used in our case studies:** [Twitter](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/twitter) · [Instagram](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/instagram) · [TikTok](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/tiktok)

## Failure modes & pitfalls

- **A view nobody can rebuild.** Logic lives only in the view's past updates. Keep projectors replayable from the source.
- **Non-idempotent projectors.** A redelivered change counts twice. Apply each change once, by version.
- **Showing a stale view right after a write.** The shopper's new order is missing. Show the write's result directly.
- **Refreshing a huge view on a timer** with `CONCURRENTLY`. Every refresh diffs the whole view. Use a stream for large, busy views.
- **Views on the write path.** Updating three views in every checkout transaction slows checkout. Move them to a stream.

## Interview framework

1. Count the queries one read costs today.
2. Shape a view for that read and pick its key.
3. Choose the refresh: on write, stream or timer, with the staleness of each.
4. Show the rebuild: replay into a new view, then swap.
5. Handle the staleness the user can see right after a write.

**What interviewers listen for:** a query count before and after, a staleness bound, and a rebuild story.

**Red flags:** a cross-shard join on every page view, or a view that cannot be rebuilt.
