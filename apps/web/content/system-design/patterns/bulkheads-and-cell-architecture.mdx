One partner's runaway price feed fills the shared thread pool, and checkout fails for every shopper. One bad deploy goes everywhere at once, and so does the outage.

- Interviewers ask about bulkheads and cells to see whether you can cap an outage to a known fraction of customers, and name that fraction, before anything has gone wrong.

## Overview

- **Shared fate:** when every caller shares one pool, one fleet or one database, one bad caller or one bad deploy reaches everyone.
- A **bulkhead** gives each dependency or tenant its own pool of threads, connections or workers, so one can fill its own pool and no other.
- **Shuffle sharding** gives each customer a random small set of workers. Two customers rarely share the whole set, so a poison request takes out one customer's shard, not a whole shard of customers.
- A **cell** is a full copy of the stack serving a fixed slice of customers behind a **thin router**. A failure or a bad deploy stays inside one cell: with 10 cells, 10 % of customers.
- The **control plane** changes the system (adds cells, moves customers); the **data plane** serves requests. **Static stability** means the data plane keeps serving when the control plane is down.

<Callout tone="violet" title="Interview edge: how this sets a senior answer apart" icon="sparkles">
  - **What most candidates say:** "Add circuit breakers and autoscale the fleet."
  - **Why the interviewer pushes back:** "One tenant's poison request kills every worker it reaches. Retries spread it, and new instances die too."
  - **The answer that stands out:** shuffle-shard the workers so each tenant reaches 2 of 8 (one of 28 combinations), and split the stack into cells behind a thin router, deployed one cell at a time. Name the fraction hit (1 in 28, or 1 in 10) and the cost: more fleets to run and spare capacity in every cell.
</Callout>

## Mental model

- A ship's hull is split into watertight compartments. A hole floods one compartment and the ship stays afloat.
- A hull with no walls floods from one hole. A bigger pump (autoscaling) does not help when the water arrives faster than it pumps.
- A bulkhead is a wall inside one service; a cell is a whole compartment with its own engine; shuffle sharding is giving each passenger cabins in two random compartments.
- The question is always the same: when one compartment floods, what fraction of passengers get wet?

## Core concepts

- The running example is Brightpost: **2,000,000 shoppers a day**, **2,300 reads/s** at peak, **300 orders/s** on sale day and **40 app servers** in London.

### Shared fate and bulkheads

- A thread held by a slow call is a thread nobody else can use. **Little's law** gives the threads busy: arrivals per second × seconds each request holds a thread.
- A **bulkhead** splits the pool: a fixed number of threads, connections or queue slots per dependency or per tenant. Size each from its own demand; when one fills, its callers get a fast error and the others never notice.

- With a 20-thread bulkhead the feed gets 20 ÷ 0.5 s = 40 updates/s, and the other 360/s get an immediate `429`.

### Shuffle sharding

- **Plain sharding** splits 8 workers into 4 shards of 2. A **poison request** (one that crashes whatever runs it) kills both workers of its shard, so 1 customer in 4 is down.
- **Shuffle sharding** gives each customer its own random pair from the same 8 workers. There are 8 × 7 ÷ 2 = **28** pairs.
- The poison request kills only its own customer's 2 workers. Every other pair shares at most one of them, so its clients retry on the other worker.
- The pairs overlap, so this needs clients that retry on another worker in their shard, and requests that any worker in the shard can serve.

**The numbers:**

- Shards that include W1 or W4: 7 + 7 − 1 = 13. One is A's; 12 lose one worker; 28 − 13 = 15 lose none.
- Fully down: 1 ÷ 4 = 25 % with plain sharding, 1 ÷ 28 = 3.6 % with shuffle sharding: 7 times fewer.

### Cells

- A **cell** is a complete, independent copy of the workload (app servers, caches, database) that shares no state with other cells.
- A **partition key** (shopper id, tenant id) decides the cell. A **cell router** maps the key to a cell and does nothing else: no business logic.
- Each cell has a **maximum size**, tested at full load. When cells are full, add a cell; never grow one past what was tested.
- Deploys go out **in waves**, one cell first, then more, with a pause to watch alarms between waves.

### Control plane, data plane and static stability

- The **control plane** makes changes: provisioning cells, moving customers, writing the cell map. It is complex and changes often.
- The **data plane** does the work: routing and serving requests. It must be simple and always up.
- **Static stability:** the data plane keeps working on what it already has when the control plane fails. The router serves from its own copy of the map.
- Provision capacity ahead of time instead of launching it during an outage: a recovery that needs the control plane fails when the control plane is the thing that broke.

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  A router that asks the control plane's database for every request turns a control-plane outage into a full outage. Keep the map in the router's memory and refresh it in the background.
</Callout>

### Tenant isolation models

- **Silo** (AWS SaaS Lens terms): a tenant gets dedicated resources, from its own database to a whole stack. Strongest isolation, highest cost per tenant.
- **Pool:** tenants share the same compute, storage and queues. Cheapest and easiest to run; a noisy neighbour needs per-tenant limits and bulkheads.
- **Bridge:** a mix per layer or per service. The SaaS Lens example pools the web tier and silos each tenant's application tier and storage.

## Technical mechanics

### Choosing the partition key

- Pick the key most requests already carry and that rarely needs two cells: shopper id for a shop, tenant id for SaaS.
- Requests that span keys (a seller's view of all orders) need a separate path or a copy of the data, not a cross-cell call.
- A tenant bigger than one cell needs its own cell or its own split; plan for it before it arrives.

### Routing and moving customers

- The router uses a **mapping table** (key → cell), not plain hashing, so one customer can move without reshuffling the rest.
- Moving a customer is a control-plane job: copy the data, freeze writes briefly, flip the map entry, then delete the old copy.

### Worked example: a bad deploy and a poison basket

Follow the Architecture figure through one bad afternoon at Brightpost.

1. **Routing.** Shopper ids map to ten cells of 200,000 shoppers a day each, and 4 of the 40 app servers each.
2. **Bad deploy.** Version 2 has a bug that fails checkout. It reaches cell 1 only; alarms fire and the pipeline rolls back.
3. **Poison basket.** Inside cell 2, one shopper's basket crashes every worker that loads it. Cell 2's 8 basket workers (illustrative) are shuffle-sharded in pairs, so it kills 2 workers and only that shopper's shard is down.
4. **New cell.** The control plane adds cell 11 for new sign-ups; the router keeps serving from its map even while the control plane works.

**The numbers:**

- Bad deploy: 1 cell of 10 = 10 % of shoppers, 200,000 a day, instead of all 2,000,000.
- Poison basket: 8 choose 2 = 28 shards in the cell, so 1 in 28 of cell 2's shoppers lose both workers: 0.36 % of all shoppers.
- At a larger scale: 100 workers, shards of 5 → 75,287,520 combinations, so two shoppers share all five workers with odds of 1 in 75 million.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One shared pool | Best utilisation; simplest | One caller can starve all | Small systems, trusted callers |
| Bulkhead per dependency or tenant | Caps one caller; cheap | Idle threads in each pool; sizes to tune | Any service with several downstreams or noisy tenants |
| Shuffle sharding | Impact drops to 1 combination | Clients must retry within the shard; harder to reason about | Multi-tenant fleets exposed to poison requests |
| Cells | Caps deploys and whole-stack failures to 1 ÷ N | N stacks to run; a router and control plane to build | Large services where a full outage is unacceptable |
| Silo per tenant | Strongest isolation | Highest cost per tenant | Regulated or very large tenants |

## Production systems

- **Amazon Route 53** (AWS Builders' Library, 2019): 2,048 virtual name servers, each domain on a shuffle shard of 4, about 730 billion possible shards; no two domains share more than two name servers.
- **Amazon EC2** (Builders' Library, "Static stability using Availability Zones"): existing instances keep running when the EC2 control plane is impaired. Services across three zones run 50 % spare capacity, about 66 % load each, so losing a zone needs no new instances.
- **Slack** (engineering blog, August 2023): treats each availability zone as a cell and aims to drain one in 5 minutes, shifting Envoy weights in 1 % steps from its own control plane.
- **Netflix Hystrix**: per-dependency thread pools; Netflix's API ran 40+ pools of 5 to 20 threads, most at 10. As of November 2026 Hystrix is in maintenance mode and Netflix recommends resilience4j.
- **Used in our case studies:** [Rate Limiter](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/rate-limiter) · [ChatGPT](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/chatgpt)

## Failure modes & pitfalls

- **A fat router.** Business logic in the router makes it the one shared thing that fails for everyone. Keep it to a key lookup and a forward.
- **Shared state between cells.** One global database or cache behind ten cells is still one failure. Give each cell its own data; share only the router.
- **Deploying all cells at once.** Cells only cap a bad deploy when deploys go out in waves. Start with one cell and wait for its alarms.
- **Recovery that needs the control plane.** Autoscaling during an outage fails when the control plane is down. Provision spare capacity ahead of time.
- **Retries that cross the wall.** A client that retries a poison request on every worker in the fleet undoes shuffle sharding. Retry only within the shard, with a budget.

## Interview framework

1. Name the shared fate: which pool, fleet or database every request depends on.
2. Add bulkheads per dependency or tenant, sized with Little's law, rejecting fast when full.
3. Shuffle-shard multi-tenant workers and give the combinations: 8 choose 2 = 28.
4. Split into cells behind a thin router; give the cell count, the fraction one failure reaches and the deploy waves.
5. Separate control plane from data plane and say what keeps serving when the control plane is down.

**What interviewers listen for:** a fraction of customers hit (1 in 28, 1 in 10) stated before the failure happens.

**Red flags:** "autoscaling will absorb it", or a cell design with one shared database behind it.
