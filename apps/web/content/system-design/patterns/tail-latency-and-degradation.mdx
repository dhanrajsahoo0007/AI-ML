Every service behind the product page answers within 100 ms 99 % of the time, yet four page views in ten are slow, because the page waits for 50 of them.

- Interviewers ask about tail latency to see whether you can do the fan-out arithmetic, and then cut the tail without doubling the load or taking the site down with a fallback.

## Overview

- **Tail latency** is the slow end of the latency distribution: the **p99** is the time 99 % of calls beat, and 1 % do not.
- A request that waits for **n** calls is slow if any one of them is: the chance is 1 − 0.99^n, so 50 calls make **39.5 %** of pages slow.
- A **hedged request** sends a second copy of a slow call after the p95 delay and keeps the first answer: about 5 % extra load for a much shorter tail.
- A **deadline** is the moment the caller stops waiting. Passed down the chain, it stops every hop from working on an answer nobody will read.
- **Degradation** drops optional work under stress: a slow panel is left out, and a **brownout** switches whole panels off as load rises.
- A **fallback** path that only runs during an outage is untested code that runs at the worst moment: degrade by doing less, not by doing something different.

## Mental model

- Ten friends share a taxi to the airport. Each is late one morning in a hundred, but the taxi leaves only when the last one is in.
- With ten friends, about one trip in ten starts late; with fifty, four in ten. The group is slower than anyone in it.
- The fixes: phone a second friend to bring the late one's bag (hedge), leave at 8:00 whoever is missing (deadline), and leave the golf clubs behind when the boot is full (brownout).
- In the system the taxi is the product page, the friends are the services it calls, and the golf clubs are the optional panels.

## Core concepts

- The running example: Brightpost's product page at the **2,300 reads/s** peak, fanning out to **50 services** for the kettle's page, each slow (past its 100 ms p99) **1 %** of the time; the shopper's budget is **800 ms**.

### Why the tail grows with fan-out

- If each call is slow with chance p, independently, all n calls are fast with chance (1 − p)^n. The page is slow otherwise.
- The p99 of the page is therefore set by the slowest of its calls, not by a typical one.
- Dean and Barroso's example: servers slow one time in 100, and a request that needs 100 of them is slow 63 % of the time.
- For what one call costs in the first place, see [Latency numbers that matter](/system-design/fundamentals/latency-numbers).

- At 0.1 % a call, 50 calls give 4.9 % and 100 give 9.5 %: a tail ten times thinner per call is about eight times better per page at 50.
- Brightpost at peak: 2,300 pages/s × 39.5 % = about 908 slow pages every second.

### Hedged and tied requests

- Slowness is usually not in the request itself but in the replica: a garbage-collection pause, a busy disk, a noisy neighbour. Another replica is likely fast.
- **Hedged request:** send the call to one replica; if no answer arrives by the p95 latency, send the same call to a second replica, keep the first answer and cancel the other.
- Waiting for the p95 means only about 5 % of calls get a second copy, so the extra load is about 5 %.
- **Tied request:** send the call to two replicas about 1 ms apart, each tagged with the other. The one that starts first tells its twin to drop its copy, so the duplicate rarely runs.
- Hedge only **idempotent** calls (safe to run twice), such as reads. A hedged payment can charge twice.

### Deadline budgets

- A **timeout** is a duration; a **deadline** is a point in time. Each hop that adds its own fresh timeout lets the total exceed what the shopper will wait.
- **Propagate the deadline:** each hop passes on the time left, minus its own work. gRPC sends it as a timeout so the two servers' clocks need not agree (see [RPC and gRPC](/system-design/fundamentals/rpc)).
- A hop with less time left than the callee's p99 should not make the call: the answer would arrive after nobody is waiting.
- Retries spend the same budget. A retry that cannot finish before the deadline is wasted load.

- 800 − 20 (gateway) = 780; 780 − 60 (page service) = 720; 720 − 200 (recommendations) = 520 for the feature store.

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  gRPC sets no deadline by default, so a client can wait forever. A service that drops the incoming deadline and calls on with its own 30 s timeout keeps working, and holding threads, long after the shopper has left.
</Callout>

## Technical mechanics

### Choosing the hedge delay

- Hedge at the p95 of the call's own latency, measured live: a fixed delay drifts out of date as the service changes.
- Cap the extra copies. gRPC's hedging policy sends at most `maxAttempts` copies (capped at 5), `hedgingDelay` apart.
- Stop hedging under overload: a hedge adds load exactly when the service is slowest. gRPC's retry throttling sends hedges only while its token count is above half.

### Degrading without a fallback path

- **Good enough:** give each optional panel a cut-off inside the deadline and render the page without it if it is late. Google's web search skips ads or spelling correction when they do not answer in time.
- **Brownout:** switch panels off by load (CPU, queue length or p99), most expensive and least valuable first, with per-panel flags.
- An empty panel is a smaller page, not a different code path, so it is cheap to test: run with panels off in a regular drill.
- A **fallback** to a second system is different: it runs only in an outage, so its bugs and its load surface then. Amazon's advice is to improve the primary, or convert the fallback into a failover path that runs in production all the time.

### Worked example: the kettle's page at peak

Follow the four steps of the Architecture figure, with hedging and the deadline in place throughout.

1. **Every panel on.** 2,300 pages/s × 50 calls = 115,000 calls/s, and 39.5 % of pages, about 908 a second, wait on a slow call.
2. **Hedge after the p95.** At most 5 % of calls get a second copy, and a page is slow only if some call is slow twice.
3. **The 800 ms deadline.** No page waits past 800 ms: a panel whose calls are late is left out, and no hop calls a service it cannot hear back from in time.
4. **Load passes 70 %, 80 %, 90 %.** The switch drops recommendations, then recently viewed and questions, then reviews.

**The numbers:**

- Hedging: at most 0.05 × 115,000 = 5,750 extra calls/s; a call is slow 0.01 % of the time (if the copies are independent), so 1 − 0.9999^50 = 0.5 % of pages, about 11 a second.
- Ladder: 50 → 30 → 15 → 10 calls a page; 115,000 → 69,000 → 34,500 → 23,000 calls/s at the same 2,300 pages/s.
- Without hedging, the chance of a slow page falls with each rung: 39.5 % → 26.0 % → 14.0 % → 9.6 %.
- The split of 50 services across panels (20, 7, 8, 5, 4, 6) and the 70/80/90 % thresholds are illustrative.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hedged requests | Cuts the tail for about 5 % extra load | Idempotent calls only; adds load under overload | Reads fanned out over replicas |
| Tied requests | Duplicate rarely runs; helps the median too | Servers must talk to each other to cancel | Queue-heavy back ends you control |
| Deadline propagation | No work after the caller gives up | Every hop must read and pass it on | Every call chain deeper than one hop |
| Good-enough cut-off | Page time capped; slow panels dropped | Some pages miss a panel | Panels the shopper can live without |
| Brownout ladder | Cuts calls and CPU as load rises | Lost revenue from dropped panels | Load spikes such as the noon sale |
| Fallback to a second system | Something answers in an outage | Untested path; can overload its target | Rarely; prefer an always-on failover |

## Production systems

- **Google Bigtable, hedged requests** (Dean and Barroso, CACM 2013): reading 1,000 keys spread over 100 servers, a hedge after 10 ms cut the 99.9th-percentile latency from 1,800 ms to 74 ms for 2 % more requests.
- **Google's cluster file system, tied requests** (same paper): a tied copy sent after 1 ms cut median read latency by 16 % and the 99.9th percentile by 38 % on a mostly idle cluster, for under 1 % extra disk use.
- **gRPC** (as of November 2026): no deadline by default; a passed deadline fails the call with `DEADLINE_EXCEEDED`; some implementations propagate it to outgoing calls. On success, its hedging policy cancels the other copies.
- **Amazon retail** (Builders' Library, 2019): when per-server caches of shipping speeds failed together, web servers fell back to querying the database directly, and the fallback turned a partial outage into a full-site one.
- **Used in our case studies:** [URL Shortener](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/url-shortener) · [Netflix](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/netflix) · [Rate Limiter](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/rate-limiter) · [ChatGPT](/system-design-case-studies/case-studies/chatgpt)

## Failure modes & pitfalls

- **Hedging everything at once.** Two copies of every call double the load. Wait for the p95, cap the copies, and throttle hedges under overload.
- **Hedging writes.** A second copy of a non-idempotent call runs twice. Hedge reads, or add an idempotency key first.
- **Timeouts that do not shrink.** Each hop adds its own 1 s timeout and the page takes 4 s. Propagate one deadline and pass on the time left.
- **Working past the deadline.** A server finishes a request its caller abandoned. Check the deadline before starting, and drop expired requests from the queue.
- **An untested fallback.** It runs for the first time in an outage and overloads the database behind it. Prefer failover that runs all the time, and test degraded pages in drills.
- **Degrading the core.** The switch turns off the price. Classify panels as core or optional up front, and never flag the core.

## Interview framework

1. Do the fan-out maths: n calls, p slow each, 1 − (1 − p)^n slow pages; 50 calls at 1 % is 39.5 %.
2. Cut the per-call tail: hedge idempotent reads after the p95, or tie them; state the 5 % extra load.
3. Bound the whole: one deadline from the edge, passed down as time left; no call that cannot finish in time.
4. Plan the degradation ladder: core against optional panels, cut-offs per panel, brownout thresholds.
5. Say how you test it, and why you avoid a fallback that only runs in an outage.

**What interviewers listen for:** the 1 − 0.99^n arithmetic, a hedge delay tied to the p95, and a deadline that shrinks hop by hop.

**Red flags:** "each service has a good p99, so we are fine", hedging writes, or a fallback that has never run in production.
