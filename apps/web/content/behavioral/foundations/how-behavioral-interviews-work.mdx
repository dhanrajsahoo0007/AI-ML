## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  A behavioral interview **collects written evidence**. Your interviewer writes down what you say you did, and other
  people, some of whom never meet you, use those notes to decide **whether to hire you and at what level**.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

Two candidates for the same senior role have similar coding and design results. Each is asked: "Tell me about a launch you owned that went wrong."

The first gives a smooth three minutes. "We had a caching bug during the checkout migration. The team stayed late, rolled it back, fixed a race condition and launched two days later." Asked what he changed himself, he says "we added more tests".

The second takes 20 seconds to set the scene: a payment webhook cutover at 12,000 requests a second, where repeated retries could charge a merchant twice. She then gives three things she did. She saw lock contention on the first shard to take traffic. She stopped the rollout, against her product manager's wishes. She traced the cause to a retry key that was not unique, and afterwards added a build check so that nobody could ship a write without one.

The next day both interviewers write their feedback. The second has quotes, a decision and a number. The first has one line: "Could not establish what the candidate did." Nothing he wrote is negative, but he has nothing that argues for a senior offer.

## What happens to your answer

1. **The interview.** You tell a story and the interviewer asks follow-up questions. Most of what gets written down comes from the follow-ups.
2. **Written feedback.** After the interview, each interviewer writes up what they heard against a set of criteria. Google says every candidate for a role is scored against the same rubrics and that interviewers must give structured feedback.
3. **A review by more than one person.** The feedback is read together. At Amazon, a Bar Raiser from another team leads the debrief and keeps it on the evidence. Google says the decision draws on "a number of perspectives".
4. **Hire and level.** Two questions are answered: should we hire, and at what level. Former interviewers describe these as separate decisions that need different evidence.

The diagram is a composite. It uses Amazon's "Bar Raiser" for the outside reviewer and shows a day between interview and write-up. No company we checked publishes a deadline for feedback, and the level names in it (L4, L5, L6) are the ones candidates use, not ones the companies publish.

## What the interviewer writes down

Whatever the company, the notes come back to five things.

1. **What you did yourself.** Did you find the cause, write the proposal and make the trade, or carry out tasks that someone else had defined? "I" for your actions, "we" for shared results.
2. **How open the problem was.** A ticket, a feature spec, a team problem with no clear cause, or a problem between teams that nobody owned.
3. **How you act under pressure from two sides.** A deadline against reliability, or a senior engineer who disagrees with your design.
4. **Honesty about yourself.** Where your first idea was wrong, what you underestimated and what someone else got right.
5. **What you changed afterwards.** A promise to be more careful, or a build check, an alert, a template, a runbook.

## A weak answer, then the fix

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time you took ownership of a reliability problem."_

<Tabs title="The same project, told twice">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"Last year our notification service had reliability problems at peak and was dropping push notifications. Our team decided to move from direct HTTP calls to a queue. We worked with the mobile team on the payload, wrote the consumers and rolled it out over a month. After that things were much more stable and we were paged less."*

    **What the interviewer can write:** almost nothing. Every decision is "we". There is no starting number and no result. No option was weighed.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Situation and task.** *"Our checkout notifications service handles 8,000 events a second at peak. When the push provider slowed down, our worker threads ran out, and order confirmation p99 rose to 4.2 seconds. Nobody owned the boundary between checkout and notifications, and the holiday peak was three weeks away."*
    - **Action.** *"I offered to lead the fix. First, I went through two days of thread dumps and showed that retries inside the checkout worker were holding database connections open. Second, I rejected a two-month rewrite because it would miss the holiday freeze. I put a circuit breaker in front of the push client within two days, then moved sending behind an outbox table with a key that stops double sends. Third, the mobile lead was worried about late receipts, so I measured it: 99.4 percent of notifications still arrived within 350 ms."*
    - **Result.** *"We shipped five days before the peak. At 3.2 times normal traffic, with two provider slowdowns, checkout p99 stayed at 140 ms. Pages went from 11 a week to none over the next quarter."*
    - **Learning.** *"I added a build rule that fails any network call to a third party inside a database transaction. It now runs on all 14 payment services."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- Every action has "I" and a reason.
- There is a rejected option, so the interviewer can see a judgment.
- The result has a before and an after, and the learning is a rule that exists.

## The same answer at three levels

The same question is asked from junior to principal. The scope of your answer is what places it. One published rubric, written by former Meta engineers, puts it this way: a junior story affects your own work, a senior story affects your whole team, and a staff story affects two or more teams.

## Build your own

Go through your last 18 to 24 months of work with these three questions before you write any story.

1. **What would not have happened without you?** Look at your last three projects. Find the design decision or the debugging result that was yours. Old pull requests and design comments help.
2. **What did the other side get right?** Think of a technical argument you won. In hindsight, which part of the other person's worry was valid?
3. **What rule do you follow because breaking it once hurt?** Never an unthrottled backfill, always a rollback plan. The incident behind that rule is your failure story.

## Red flags

- **"We" for three minutes.** The interviewer cannot tell what you built. **Recover:** say "my part was" and name it.
- **A philosophy in place of an event.** "I always prioritise communication." **Recover:** "Here is one time I did that", then tell it.
- **Criticising former colleagues.** **Recover:** describe what happened and what you did, without a verdict on the person.
- **Two minutes of background.** **Recover:** pause and say, "Let me go straight to the three things I did and the result, and you can ask about any part."
- **An invented story.** Follow-up questions go to details that only someone who was there would know. **Recover:** see the first common question below.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="What if I have never faced the exact situation in the question?">
    Do not invent one. Say so, and offer the nearest real thing: "I have not rolled back a schema change across regions.
    The closest was a key rotation that broke 8 percent of mobile clients." Tell that story, then spend the last 20
    seconds on what you would check differently in the case they asked about.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="How much technical detail belongs in a behavioral answer?">
    Enough to make the limit and the trade-off clear in about 20 seconds: "write locks on the orders table at 4,000
    transactions a second". Then stay on your decisions and the people involved. Keep the deeper detail ready for the
    follow-ups.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Can the behavioral round change the level of my offer?">
    Candidates at several companies report offers one level below the one they interviewed for, after feedback that
    their stories had the smaller scope. No company we checked publishes how often this happens, and we found no
    reliable figure. What you control is the scope of the stories you choose.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
