## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Prepare **eight real stories you know in depth**, and for each one know **which questions it can answer**. One project
  usually holds a conflict, a deadline, a surprise and a lesson, so eight stories cover far more than eight questions.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

An engineer finds a list of 100 behavioral questions and starts writing an answer to each. By day four she has 35 pages.

In the interview she is asked a question that is not on her list: "Tell me about a time you made a system simpler because your team could not keep it running." She searches her memory for the closest script, picks one about a rewrite and recites it. It does not quite answer the question, and it sounds recited.

A second engineer prepared eight stories and nothing else. He hears the same question and thinks of his payments migration. Early on, the goal was unclear. In the design review he disagreed with a senior engineer. The date moved up by three weeks and he cut scope. A metric looked wrong in the trial run. And at the end he removed a service the team could not staff. He tells that last part, in his own words, in two minutes.

He needed no script. He knew one project well enough to start the story at whichever part the question asked about.

## How one story answers several questions

1. **Start from real projects.** Things you did, with dates, numbers and other people. Three are shown. You want eight.
2. **Find the tensions in each.** Where was there a disagreement, a limit, a surprise, a mistake?
3. **Choose what to stress.** The situation and the result stay the same. The 70 seconds of action change with the question.
4. **Map to questions.** Each story now answers several. The diagram uses company names for some question types, but the idea is the same anywhere.

Eight slots cover what almost every loop asks. Eight is our working number, not a rule from any company.

| Slot | Kind of story                  | Main thing it shows                    | It can also answer                           |
| ---- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | Your main project, in depth    | Technical depth and ownership          | Ambiguity, trade-offs, what you would change |
| 2    | A hard technical disagreement  | Working through conflict with evidence | Changing your mind, backing a decision       |
| 3    | An outage or an honest mistake | Owning a failure                       | Finding the real cause, prevention           |
| 4    | A date that could not move     | Choosing what to cut                   | Pushback from product, delivery              |
| 5    | A vague one-line goal          | Making a plan from little              | Deciding without full information            |
| 6    | Moving another team            | Influence without authority            | Cross-team work, larger scope                |
| 7    | Helping others get better      | Mentoring, raising a standard          | Feedback, removing repeated manual work      |
| 8    | An unpopular call              | Stopping a project or cutting a cost   | Judgment, honesty about sunk effort          |

Fill the slots in the [Story Matrix](/behavioral/practice-lab/story-matrix) tool. It shows which topics have no story yet, and it can show the same stories against Amazon's Leadership Principles.

## One project, three questions

Suppose slot 1 is this project: moving a fraud-scoring API from a direct call to features computed ahead of time. The opening 15 seconds and the closing 15 seconds barely change. The middle does.

<Tabs title="One project, three starting points">
  <Tab label="A disagreement">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a senior engineer."*

    Stress the three days of debate with the staff ML engineer, who wanted features computed live inside the model server. You wanted them prepared in advance. You wrote down the strongest form of his case, which was freshness. You replayed real traffic and showed that live joins broke the 60 ms checkout budget at peak. You then agreed to stream only the two counters that truly needed to be under a second old.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="A deadline">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time you cut scope to make a date."*

    Stress week three, when a payment partner moved the cutover date up by three weeks. You ranked the 28 model features by how much each contributed. The top 9 gave 94 percent of the fraud catch rate. You shipped those 9 before the date and added the other 19 in the next sprint.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="A hidden problem">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time you noticed something in the data that others missed."*

    Stress the trial run. The overall accuracy number matched the old system. You split the results by device anyway and found that one mobile browser had 9 percent more wrongly declined payments. An upstream parser was dropping part of the network address. You fixed it before any live traffic reached the new model.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## A weak answer, then the fix

The usual mistake with a matrix is to force a favourite story onto a question it does not fit.

<Tabs title="Fitting the story to the question">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a mistake you made."*

    *"During the fraud migration, another team's parser was dropping part of the address, which nobody had noticed. I caught it in the trial run and fixed it before launch."*

    **What the interviewer writes:** did not answer. The question asked for the candidate's mistake, and the story is about catching someone else's.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a mistake you made."*

    *"I changed a cache setting at peak without a staged rollout. The cache emptied, and checkout returned errors for 22 minutes."* Then the story from slot 3: what you did in those minutes, the cause, and the staged rollout for settings that you added.

    **What the interviewer writes:** a real mistake, owned in the first sentence, with a fix.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- The candidate used the story that sits in the failure slot, even though it is less flattering.
- The first sentence answers the question that was asked.

## Try it: which story would you tell?

A candidate has four stories ready:

- **Ledger migration.** Moved the payments ledger to a new database with no downtime. Disagreed with a senior engineer about the approach. The date moved up three weeks.
- **Cache outage.** Their own settings change emptied a cache at peak. 22 minutes of errors. The first theory about the cause was wrong.
- **Shared login library.** Got three other teams to adopt it when none had planned to.
- **Two new hires.** Mentored two new engineers through their first quarter and rewrote the on-call guide with them.

## Build your own

Spend 15 minutes on these before you write anything.

1. **Longest threads.** Which three pull requests or design documents from the last two years had the longest comment threads? Those are your conflict and influence stories. The disagreement is already written down.
2. **Worst hours.** What were your two most stressful hours on call or fixing a release? Note the symptom, the wrong idea you checked first, what showed the real cause and the check you added.
3. **Nobody asked.** What tool, script or cleanup did you build because you were tired of the team doing something by hand?
4. **One product for years?** Split it into episodes by time and problem: the migration in spring, the memory leak in the sale, the API negotiation with mobile, the two new hires.

[Write it in the Story Builder](/behavioral/practice-lab/story-builder?for=ownership), one story at a time, then tick what each covers in the [Story Matrix](/behavioral/practice-lab/story-matrix).

## Red flags

- **A favourite story forced onto the wrong question.** **Recover:** if you notice midway, say "a better example of a mistake is this one" and switch.
- **Word-for-word scripts.** They sound recited and fall apart at the first follow-up. **Recover:** keep six short lines per story: the situation with its numbers, three actions, the result, the learning.
- **Eight wins.** No failure and no lost argument. **Recover:** add one of each.
- **The same story four times in one loop.** Interviewers compare notes afterwards. **Recover:** if you must reuse a project, start from a different part and say so: "This is the same migration I may have mentioned to a colleague of yours, from the deadline side."
- **Old stories only.** **Recover:** make sure most are from the last three years, or say what you would do differently now.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="Can I reuse a story within one loop?">
    No company we researched publishes a rule. A safe practice: do not tell the same part of the same story to two
    interviewers. Telling a different part of the same project, such as the conflict to one and the outage to another,
    is normal. With eight stories you will rarely need to.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="I worked on one product for three years. Is that one story?">
    No. The product is the setting. The stories are the episodes inside it, each with its own problem, people and
    result. Three years on one system often holds all eight.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Can I keep notes in front of me in a video interview?">
    A card with eight short titles and their key numbers is fine for a glance while the question is being asked. Reading
    sentences from a second screen shows in your eyes and your rhythm, and it costs trust.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
