## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  A result an interviewer can write down has **a before and an after with a unit**, the **scale** it happened at, the
  **period** it held for, and **one thing that did not get worse**. You do not need a revenue figure to have all four.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A platform engineer is asked how his six-month migration ended. He says: "It went well. The code is much cleaner and the team is happier with the new design."

The interviewer cannot use any of that. "Much cleaner" could describe a change that saved a great deal of money, or six months of renaming variables in a service that handles twelve requests a day.

That evening he spends ten minutes with his old dashboards and notes. He finds this:

- **Scale.** The service handled 6,500 requests a second at peak, on 40 pods.
- **Speed.** p99 latency fell from 480 ms to 95 ms.
- **Cost.** CPU use fell by 38 percent, so 15 servers were retired, about \$42,000 a year.
- **Delivery.** Rollbacks fell from 18 percent of deploys to under 2 percent.
- **Nothing lost.** Zero mismatches across 1.2 billion migrated records.

It is the same project. Every one of those numbers was already in a dashboard or a ticket. He had never been asked for them, so he had never looked.

1. **An adjective.** "Much faster." There is nothing to write down.
2. **A number.** "80 percent faster." Better, but 80 percent of what? Two errors a week falling to one is also 50 percent.
3. **A baseline and a unit.** "p99 fell from 480 ms to 95 ms." Now the size of the change is clear.
4. **The scale.** "At 6,500 requests a second." A fix at 50 requests a second and one at 50,000 are different pieces of engineering.
5. **A period and a guardrail.** "For 30 days, with the error rate flat." It lasted, and it was not bought by making something else worse.

A complete result is one sentence: **from X to Y, at this scale, over this period, with Z unchanged.**

When you think "I had no metrics on that project", you are usually thinking only of product metrics. There are four other places to look first.

| Where to look         | What it measures                | Numbers you can usually reconstruct                                     |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Speed and volume**  | How fast, how much              | Requests a second, p99 latency, batch run time (9 hours to 35 minutes)  |
| **Reliability**       | How often it breaks, how badly  | Error rate, time to recover (45 minutes to 4), pages a week (12 to 1)   |
| **Cost**              | What it takes to run            | Monthly cloud bill, servers retired, cost per thousand requests         |
| **Engineers' time**   | How much faster others can work | Build time (28 minutes to 6), teams using your library, onboarding time |
| **Product and model** | What users or the model do      | Conversion, accuracy on a fixed test set, share of tickets answered     |

## A weak answer, then the fix

Three results from three kinds of work, each said twice.

<Tabs title="Three results, said twice">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    - **Backend.** *"My caching change reduced database load a lot and made the feed faster."*
    - **Internal tooling.** *"I cleaned up our build so it was much quicker and less flaky."*
    - **ML.** *"We improved retrieval and the prompt, and users liked the answers much better."*

    **What the interviewer writes:** three claims with no size. Each could be a large change or a trivial one.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Backend.** *"The cache cut reads on the main database from 22,000 a second to 7,000 during the morning peak, and feed p99 went from 390 ms to 65 ms. Readers never saw data more than five seconds old."*
    - **Internal tooling.** *"Build time went from 32 minutes to 19 for our 14 engineers, and tests failing for no reason went from 22 percent of runs to 9 percent. I wanted it under 10 minutes and did not get there. The slowest test suite is still not split."*
    - **ML.** *"Correct citations on our fixed set of 400 questions went from 64 percent to 89 percent, and the share of answers users marked helpful rose by 11 percent across 50,000 questions a day. p95 response time stayed under 1.4 seconds and cost per question did not change."*

    **What the interviewer writes:** three results with a size, a scale and a limit. The middle one is modest and honest about what is left.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- Every claim has a before and an after with a unit.
- Each names something that stayed the same: how stale the data could be, response time, cost.
- The tooling result admits it fell short of its target. A modest number that is true is worth more than a large one that cannot survive a follow-up.

## Build your own

For each story in your matrix, answer these.

1. **Scale.** How big was it: requests a second, rows, daily users, or the number of people who used your tool? "An internal tool for 45 support agents handling 1,200 tickets a day" is a scale.
2. **Before and after.** What is the one number that moved, and from what to what?
3. **Guardrail.** When you improved that number, what could have got worse? Speed against accuracy, cost against availability. What happened to it?
4. **Where is the evidence?** A dashboard, a ticket, a message to your manager. If you cannot check, round the number and say it is approximate.

[Write it in the Story Builder](/behavioral/practice-lab/story-builder?for=delivery). It marks any result that has no number.

## Red flags

- **A company number claimed alone.** "My change added \$12 million in revenue." **Recover:** give the number you owned and the link: "I cut the checkout script by 180 kB, which cut time to interactive on mobile by 600 ms. The growth team's test put that at 0.8 percent more completed checkouts."
- **A percentage with no baseline.** **Recover:** add "from X to Y" in the next sentence.
- **False precision.** "Latency fell by 73.4 percent" from two years ago. **Recover:** "about three quarters".
- **A number with no scale.** **Recover:** add the traffic, the data size or the number of users.
- **A number you cannot explain.** The follow-up will be "how did you measure that?" **Recover:** if you do not know, say who measured it and how you understood the method.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="My project launched just before I left, or has not fully launched. What do I use?">
    Use what you measured before launch. A load test or a replay of real traffic: "p99 held at 42 ms against 310 ms on
    the old service". A partial rollout: "10 percent of traffic, 40,000 users, with no serious incidents". Readiness:
    "recovery from a cold start went from 12 minutes to 40 seconds". These are yours, and they are real.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="What if the interviewer asks how I know the improvement was caused by my change?">
    This is a good question to be asked. Say how you separated your change from the noise: new pods against old pods in
    the same zone at the same hour, or the whole distribution of latency and not only the average. If you could not
    separate it fully, say so.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="I am a student. My project had three users. Do I still need numbers?">
    Yes, and small ones are fine. "Page load went from 4 seconds to 1.2 on the lab's test phone" is a complete result.
    So is "our team of four cut the time to set up the project from a day to 20 minutes".
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
