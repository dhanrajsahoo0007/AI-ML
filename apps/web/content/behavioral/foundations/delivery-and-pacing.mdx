## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  The interviewer scores what they can **follow and write down**, so a clear, slower answer with pauses and signposts
  beats a faster one with better content.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

An engineer with a strong record fails two behavioral rounds in a row. A friend runs a mock interview and records it.

Listening back, the content is good. The delivery is the problem: he starts talking the instant the question ends, speaks at about 190 words a minute, never pauses, and finishes each answer by trailing off with "so, yeah". The interviewer in the recording asks "sorry, what was the result?" twice.

He changes three things: a two-second pause before answering, a one-sentence headline, and a full stop after the result. Nothing in his stories changes. He passes the next loop.

## How an answer should sound

1. **Pause before you start.** Two or three seconds to choose the story. To the interviewer it looks like thought. If you need longer, say "let me pick the best example".
2. **Open with the headline.** One sentence that says where the answer is going: "This is about a migration I led that went wrong in week two." The interviewer can now listen for the right things.
3. **Signpost each part.** Short phrases: "The situation was", "What I did", "The result". They let a note-taker keep up, and they give you a breath.
4. **Stop at the result.** Say the number, say the learning in one sentence, and stop talking. Silence after an answer is the interviewer writing.

**Pace.** Aim for about 140 words a minute, which is slower than most people speak when nervous. Slow down further on numbers, names and anything you want written down.

## Thinking time and recovering

| The moment                          | What to say                                                      |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| You need time to choose a story     | "Let me think of the best example." Then take up to ten seconds. |
| You did not understand the question | "Do you mean a conflict with a peer, or with a manager?"         |
| You realise you are rambling        | "Let me get to the point: what I did was"                        |
| You have gone blank                 | "Can I come back to that one?" Most interviewers will say yes.   |
| You picked the wrong story          | "A better example is this one." Switch once, early.              |
| The interviewer interrupts          | Stop at once and answer the new question. It is not a bad sign.  |

Asking to hear the question again, or to have a moment, is normal. Starting an answer you cannot finish costs more.

## Video calls

- **Camera at eye height**, face lit from the front, and a plain background. Look at the camera when you give the result.
- **Headphones with a microphone.** Laptop speakers cause echo, and echo makes interviewers interrupt.
- **Leave a longer gap** after the interviewer finishes. Video delay makes people talk over each other.
- **Notes are fine, reading is not.** A single page of story titles and numbers beside the screen helps. Sentences on a second monitor make your eyes move and your voice go flat.
- **Test the tool** the company uses the day before, on the same network.
- **Have a fallback.** Keep the recruiter's phone number or email open in case the call drops.

## Answering in a second language

Interviewers at international companies talk to candidates from many countries every week. They are scoring the content of the answer, and an accent or a grammar slip is not a mark against you. Clarity is what helps:

- **Use short sentences.** One idea in each. Long sentences are where grammar goes wrong.
- **Use plain words.** "I fixed" is better than a longer word you are less sure of.
- **Prepare the first sentence of each story** word for word. A clean start settles the rest.
- **Say numbers slowly, and say the unit.** "Fifteen, one five, milliseconds."
- **If you lose a word, describe it.** "The thing that stores recent results" is fine for "cache".
- **Ask them to repeat.** "Could you say that again?" is better than answering the wrong question.
- **Practise out loud more than native speakers do.** Twice the repetitions on half the stories is a good trade.

## Build your own

1. Record yourself answering one question on your phone. Do not look at notes.
2. Listen once for pace. Count the words in 30 seconds and double it. Over 170 is too fast.
3. Listen again for filler: "so", "basically", "kind of", "you know". Pick the one you say most and pause where you would say it.
4. Record again with a pause at the start, a headline and a full stop at the end.

Then run a [Mock Loop](/behavioral/practice-lab/mock-loop): its timer shows which part of the answer you should be in, which trains the pace.

## Red flags

- **Speaking faster as you get nervous.** **Recover:** breathe out before the next sentence. It forces a pause.
- **No ending.** Trailing off with "so, yeah". **Recover:** learn the last sentence of each story.
- **Answering before you understand.** **Recover:** restate the question in your own words first.
- **Reading from a screen.** The voice goes flat and the eyes move. **Recover:** notes as single words only.
- **Apologising for your English.** It draws attention to the thing that matters least. Say the answer.
