## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) tells what happened. **STAR+L** adds the **Learning**: what you changed
  afterwards. It also sets a time budget, so that most of a two-minute answer is about what **you** did and why.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

Watch ten engineers practise a behavioral answer and most of them make the same mistake. They start with the history: the company, the old system, who used to own it, how the product makes money. Ninety seconds pass. The interviewer looks at the clock. The candidate then rushes the part that matters into one sentence: _"So we refactored the service, added a cache, and latency dropped by half."_

The interviewer has almost nothing to write down. Background earns no points. An interviewer can only score four things: the **decisions you made** under a constraint, **how you dealt with people or technical problems** in the way, the **result with a number**, and **what you changed afterwards**. STAR+L is a way to spend your two minutes on those four things.

Each part of the answer has one job.

| Part                   | Time             | Its job                                                              | The usual mistake                                          |
| ---------------------- | ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Situation and task** | about 20 seconds | Name the system, what was at risk and the limit you worked under     | Company history and org charts                             |
| **Action**             | about 70 seconds | Three things **you** did, each with the reason you chose it          | "We" in every sentence, or a list of tasks with no reasons |
| **Result**             | about 20 seconds | The number before and after, and one thing that did not get worse    | "It went well"                                             |
| **Learning**           | about 10 seconds | The check, rule or habit you added so the problem does not come back | A feeling: "I learned that communication matters"          |

Two minutes is a starting length, not a rule from any company. It is long enough for three actions and short enough to leave the interviewer time to ask follow-ups, which is where most of the scoring happens. Meta's own interview guide recommends the STAR structure and asks for concrete examples in place of general statements (see Sources).

The same story with the time spent where it counts:

- **Situation and task (20 seconds).** "Our checkout service handles 8,000 requests a second for small shops. p99 had climbed to 4.2 seconds, and I had three weeks before the holiday peak."
- **Action (70 seconds).** "First, I took thread dumps under load and saw requests holding a database connection while they retried a slow payment call. Second, I had two options: a larger connection pool, or moving the payment call outside the transaction. I rejected the larger pool because it would only move the limit. Third, I moved the call out, added a circuit breaker with a 48-hour trial on 5 percent of traffic, and showed the mobile team the numbers so they would accept a new error code."
- **Result (20 seconds).** "p99 fell to 140 ms at three times normal traffic, and failed payments stayed flat. Pages for that service went from 11 a week to none."
- **Learning (10 seconds).** "I added a lint rule that fails the build when code makes a network call inside a database transaction. It has caught four since."

## The same answer at three levels

The parts stay the same at every level. What changes is the Action block: how far your decisions reached and how many people they moved.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/behavioral/scope-by-level"
  stepper
  minWidth={700}
  caption="What an interviewer listens for in the Action block at each level: how far the change reached."
/>

<Tabs title="One story, the Action block at three levels">
  <Tab label="Mid level">
    *"I reproduced the slow request on my machine and found the retry inside the transaction. I talked it through with my tech lead in code review and we agreed to move the call out. I wrote the change with unit tests and watched the deploy."*

    **What it shows:** you can find and fix a problem in your own area with some guidance. That is the right size for a mid-level story.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Senior">
    *"I proved the cause in production with thread dumps, not on my machine. I compared two designs in a short written note and chose the one that removed the limit. I ran it on 5 percent of traffic with automatic rollback, and I got the mobile team to accept a new error code before launch."*

    **What it shows:** you chose the problem's solution yourself, managed the risk of the change and brought another team along.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Staff">
    *"The same pattern was in three other services owned by other teams. I wrote the lint rule and a small library that makes the safe pattern the easy one, and I worked with those teams' leads to adopt it in one sprint. I also changed our design review template to ask about network calls in transactions."*

    **What it shows:** you fixed the class of problem across teams and changed how the org works, so it does not need you next time.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## When to break the order: headline first

On a question about a mistake or a conflict, 90 seconds of setup before the outcome can sound like you are avoiding it. Give one sentence of headline first, then tell the story in the usual order.

<Tabs title="Two ways to open">
  <Tab label="Headline first (mistakes and conflict)">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time you caused a production incident."*

    - **Headline (5 seconds).** *"I once ran a backfill with no throttle and slowed reads on our user-profile service for 14 minutes. It is why our migration tool now throttles itself."*
    - **Situation and task.** *"We were backfilling 180 million rows to add a tenant column before a compliance audit. The read target was 80 ms at <Term id="p99" />."*
    - **Action.**
      1. *"When the replica lag alert fired at minute six, I stopped my backfill and moved reads to the healthy zone. I did not try to tune it live."*
      2. *"In the review I traced the lag to large batches rewriting indexed rows."*
      3. *"I rewrote the runner to check replica lag every half second and pause above 200 ms."*
    - **Result.** *"The backfill finished over the next 36 hours with under 4 ms of added p99. We met the audit date."*
    - **Learning.** *"That lag check is now in our shared migration tool. It has run more than 40 large backfills with no pages."*

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Usual order (ambiguity and delivery)">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about an ambiguous project you scoped from nothing."*

    - **Situation and task.** *"Customers were leaving because our document assistant gave wrong citations on financial tables. I was given one line: make table answers trustworthy in six weeks, with no extra GPU budget."*
    - **Action.**
      1. *"I built a set of 200 real failed questions so we had a baseline: 41 percent correct citations."*
      2. *"I spent three days comparing two approaches. Reading the table layout and running a query on it reached 88 percent at one sixth of the token cost."*
      3. *"I agreed a rule with product and legal: below a confidence of 0.72, the assistant shows the source cells and does not guess."*
    - **Result.** *"Correct citations rose from 41 to 93 percent in an A/B test, and support escalations fell by 68 percent."*
    - **Learning.** *"No prompt or chunking change merges now without passing that 200-question set in the build."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Build your own

Take one real project and work through these three prompts with a timer.

<Quiz title="Draft one story">
  <QuizItem
    question="1. Say your situation and task in two sentences. Sentence one: the system, its scale and the problem. Sentence two: your limit and what was at stake."
    hint="Say it out loud. Reading in your head is faster than speaking."
  >
    Example: *"Our billing pipeline handles 40 million dollars a month, and invoice generation took 9 hours and missed
    its 6 am deadline. I had three weeks before quarter end to bring it under 45 minutes without changing the invoice
    format."*
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem
    question="2. In your Action block, name one option you considered and rejected."
    hint="Judgment only shows when the listener can see the road you did not take."
  >
    If you only say what you built, it sounds like there was one obvious path. Add one sentence: *"I considered keeping
    the totals in a cache, but a cache flush during billing close would have meant a four-hour rebuild."*
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem
    question="3. What is the honest learning from your hardest project last year?"
    hint="Think about what surprised you halfway through."
  >
    Ask: *"If a colleague started that project tomorrow, what would I warn them about in week one?"* Then turn the
    warning into something that exists: a check, a template or a review step.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>

Then write the whole story in the [Story Builder](/behavioral/practice-lab/story-builder). It shows how long the story takes to say, how the time is split across the four parts, and whether the result has a number.

## Red flags

- **The diary.** Monday I had a meeting, Tuesday I opened a document, Wednesday I talked to Bob. Group your actions into three moves: how you found the cause, how you chose and got agreement, how you shipped and checked.
- **"We" everywhere.** Each "we decided" leaves the interviewer unsure what you did. Use "I" for your actions and "we" for the shared result.
- **A result with nothing held steady.** "I cut latency by 40 percent" makes a careful interviewer wonder what you gave up. Add the thing that stayed the same: error rate, accuracy or cost.
- **A learning you never used.** If the lesson did not change anything you can name, it reads as a line added for the interview.

## Common questions about the method

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="How long should my first answer be before I stop talking?">
    Aim for 90 seconds to two minutes, which is roughly 220 to 280 spoken words. That covers three actions and a result,
    and it leaves the interviewer time for follow-ups. If you are asked to go deeper, you can. If you talk for four
    minutes, you cannot take it back.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="How do I say 'I' without taking credit for my teammates' work?">
    Separate your part from the whole. Not *"I built the checkout system"*, and not *"we designed the checkout system"*.
    Say: *"On a team of four, I owned the idempotency design and the outbox table, and a teammate led the payment
    webhooks. In my part, I chose to..."*
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="When should I not tell the story in order?">
    In two cases. For a mistake, a failure or a conflict, give a one-sentence headline first so you do not sound
    evasive. For a question about how you think (*"How do you approach technical debt?"*), give your approach in 15
    seconds, then say *"here is a time I did that"* and tell one story.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
