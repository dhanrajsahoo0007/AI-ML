## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Your two-minute story opens the conversation. Most of what the interviewer writes down comes from the **follow-up
  questions**: why you did not take the other option, which part was yours, and what you would change now. Answer each
  one in **30 to 45 seconds, conclusion first**.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A candidate finishes a clear two-minute story about moving a login service to short-lived tokens. He expects the next question on the list.

The interviewer stays on the story and asks four more questions over the next six minutes:

1. "You chose a 15-minute token lifetime over a revocation list. Who wrote that comparison, and which numbers persuaded the security team?"
2. "The mobile team objected to rotating refresh tokens. What was their strongest argument?"
3. "What broke in the 5 percent rollout that the design review had not predicted?"
4. "If a signing key leaked tomorrow and had to be revoked within five seconds, which part of your design fails first?"

A candidate who memorised a script hears an attack and gets shorter and more defensive with each answer. A prepared candidate hears something else. An interviewer asks follow-ups because they need detail for their written feedback, and they tend to ask more when a story is worth the time. He answers each in under a minute. On the fourth, he says plainly which part fails.

## What each follow-up is checking

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/behavioral/probe-tree"

  minWidth={820}

/>

1. **The opening.** Two minutes: the situation, three things you did, the result.
2. **The rejected option.** "Why not the simpler way?" The candidate points to a design note and the limit that ruled the other option out.
3. **Your own part.** "Which part did you write?" The candidate names one change and one bug they fixed, and says what teammates did.
4. **Hindsight and scale.** "What breaks at ten times the volume?" The candidate names the first limit the system would hit and what they would do about it.

The diagram shows three follow-ups and labels the interviewer a Bar Raiser, which is Amazon's name for an interviewer from outside the hiring team. There is no fixed number at any company. Some interviewers ask one follow-up and move on. Others spend ten minutes on one story.

Almost every follow-up is one of four kinds:

| Kind                 | It sounds like                                         | What it checks                                    | A good answer in 30 to 45 seconds                                    |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The other option** | "Why not X?" "What else did you consider?"             | Whether you chose, or followed a habit            | The limit that ruled X out: latency, effort to run, the date         |
| **Your part**        | "What did you write or decide yourself?"               | Whether "we" was hiding a small role              | The module, the document section, the test or the fix that was yours |
| **The other side**   | "Why did they disagree? Were they wrong?"              | Whether you understood the person you argued with | The fair concern they were protecting                                |
| **Hindsight**        | "What would you do differently?" "What breaks at 10x?" | Honesty, and how far ahead you think              | The first part that would fail, and the change you would make        |

The same answer, at the depth that was asked for:

_"Keeping customers apart was the part I would not trade. I put the customer id and the permission group into the cache key, so the similarity search only ever runs inside one customer's own entries. For wrong hits, I labelled 1,500 pairs of questions and tested thresholds. At 0.94, about 3 percent of hits had the wrong meaning, mostly negations: 'is X allowed' against 'is X not allowed'. I raised the threshold to 0.965 and added a small check on negation and numbers, which brought wrong hits under 0.3 percent. The platform team ran the database itself. The key design and the threshold work were mine."_

What changed:

- It answers both halves of the question, the more serious one first.
- The number comes with how it was measured.
- The boundary with the platform team is stated once, at the end, as a fact.

The other two follow-ups in the same chain:

- **"Why not an exact-match cache?"** _"We shipped that first. It took four hours and caught 14 percent of traffic, mostly retries. But 40 percent of our cost was business users asking the same compliance questions in different words, and exact match missed those."_
- **"What aged badly?"** _"Keeping the cache fresh. I started with a 24-hour expiry, so an edited policy document gave stale answers for up to a day. Two sprints later I added an index from each source document to its cache entries, so an edit clears them within five seconds."_

## Build your own

Take your main project story and write a two-sentence answer to each of these.

1. What is the simple approach an interviewer will suggest: a scheduled job, one database table, no cache? At what number did it stop working?
2. Where did a teammate do the heavy work? Write the boundary: "I designed the state machine and wrote the coordinator. A teammate owned the infrastructure code and the dashboards."
3. What surprised you in the rollout?
4. If you rebuilt it in half the time, which third of the design would you leave out?

[Write it in the Story Builder](/behavioral/practice-lab/story-builder?for=judgment), then ask a friend to read the story and ask "why?" four times. The [Mock Loop](/behavioral/practice-lab/mock-loop) does the same with a timer.

## Red flags

- **Guessing.** A made-up number or a detail from a layer you did not touch. **Recover:** "I did not write that layer. My part started at the request handler. From our load tests, here is how that boundary behaved."
- **Hearing "why?" as "you are wrong".** Interviewers sometimes challenge a correct decision to see whether you fold or explain. **Recover:** restate the limit and the number calmly. If they raise something you had not considered, say so.
- **Returning to the script.** Repeating part of the opening when asked to go deeper. **Recover:** answer only the question asked, then stop.
- **Answers that grow.** Each follow-up answer longer than the last. **Recover:** conclusion first, one reason, one number, stop. Then ask, "Shall I go further into that?"
- **Claiming the idea.** Saying a direction was yours when a lead proposed it. **Recover:** see the second common question below.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="The interviewer interrupted my story after 45 seconds. What does that mean?">
    One of two things. Your setup was running long and they want the actions. Or you just said the thing they need, and
    they want more of it now. Stop, answer what they asked in 30 to 45 seconds, then offer: "Shall I go further into
    that, or tell you how the rollout ended?"
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="What if they ask whose idea it was, and it was my tech lead's?">
    Say so, then go straight to the hard part that was yours. "Our staff engineer proposed moving to an event log. My
    part was the snapshot design and fixing the out-of-order replay bug that was blocking the cutover." If you claim the
    idea, the next follow-up will be about a decision you did not make.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="How do I answer 'what would you do differently?' when the project went well?">
    Choose one of three. Order: "I ran real traffic through it in week four. I would do that in week one, because real
    payloads showed two bugs our tests missed." Size: "I built a general rule engine for five teams when two needed it."
    Growth: "It worked at 10 shards. At 60 the slowest requests got slower, and I would plan for that from the start."
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="How many follow-ups should I expect on one story?">
    It depends on the interviewer, the company and how much time is left. Preparing answers to the four kinds above
    covers most of what you will be asked.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
