## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  When the offer comes, **slow down**: get every part in writing, check the **level** before the numbers, and make **one
  counter with a reason**. The conversation is a normal part of hiring, and a polite ask almost never costs an offer.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A recruiter calls with good news and a number, and asks, "So, what do you think?" The engineer is delighted and says, "That sounds great, I accept."

The next day she learns from a friend at the company that the level she accepted is one below the role she interviewed for, and that the equity in her offer vests over four years with nothing in the first twelve months. Both could have been discussed. Neither can be reopened easily after "I accept".

A better reply on the call: "Thank you, I am really pleased. Could you send the full details in writing? I would like to go through them properly and come back to you by Thursday."

## The five steps

1. **The offer arrives.** Thank them, say you are pleased, and do not answer. Give a date for your reply, two to five working days away.
2. **Get the whole offer in writing.** Base pay, bonus target, equity (the amount, the vesting schedule and any first-year cliff), sign-on, level, title, start date and location.
3. **Check the level first.** Level sets the pay band, the scope of your work and the bar for your next promotion. A higher number at a lower level is often the worse offer over three years.
4. **Make one counter, with a reason.** One message that asks for specific things. See below.
5. **Decide in writing by your date.** Whichever way you decide, be gracious. You may meet these people again.

## Checking the level

Ask the recruiter directly: "Which level is this offer at, and what was the feedback on level from the interviews?"

If it is lower than you expected, you have three options:

- **Ask what was missing.** "What would the interviewers have needed to see for senior?" The answer tells you whether it is worth asking for a review.
- **Ask for a review or one more interview**, if the feedback was close. Some companies allow it and some do not.
- **Accept the level and negotiate within it**, toward the top of its band, with a written note of what promotion requires.

A rule that is safe to rely on: the level is far harder to change after you join than before.

## The counter

A counter works when it is specific, has a reason, and is easy to say yes to.

_"Thank you again for the offer. I am excited about the team and I would like to make this work. Two things would let me accept: a base of 165, which matches the range for this level in the market data I have and my other process, and a sign-on to cover the bonus I would leave behind in March, about 12. If we can do that, I am ready to sign this week."_

What makes it work:

- **It says you want the job.** The recruiter now has a reason to argue for you.
- **It asks for specific numbers**, not "more".
- **Each ask has a reason**: market data, another offer, money you would leave behind.
- **It says what happens if they agree.** "I am ready to sign" lets the recruiter take a clear request to whoever approves it.

Good reasons, strongest first: a competing written offer; reliable market data for the level and location; pay or equity you would lose by leaving; the cost of relocating.

If base pay cannot move, ask about the parts that often can: sign-on, equity, start date, a review after six months.

## Questions you will be asked at this stage

| They ask                                  | Answer                                                                                                  |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "What do you think of the offer?"         | "I am pleased. I would like to see it in writing and come back to you by Thursday."                     |
| "Do you have other offers?"               | The truth. "One written offer and one process at the final stage."                                      |
| "Can you share the other offer?"          | You may share the numbers or the letter, or decline. Never invent one: letters are sometimes requested. |
| "What number would make you sign today?"  | Give your counter. If you are not ready: "I will send you that in writing tomorrow."                    |
| "This offer expires on Friday."           | "I understand. I can decide by Monday if that works." Most deadlines can move a few days.               |
| "Is pay the only thing holding you back?" | Say what else matters: level, team, start date. It may be easier for them to change.                    |

## Red flags

- **Accepting on the call.** **Recover:** if you already said yes verbally, you can still say "before I sign, I have two questions about the written offer".
- **Negotiating before you have an offer.** Numbers discussed in the loop weaken you. Wait for the offer.
- **Many small rounds.** Ask once, for everything that matters. A third round of asks reads as bad faith.
- **Negotiating against yourself.** After your counter, wait. Do not send a lower number because a day has passed.
- **Burning the bridge when you decline.** A short, warm note keeps the door open.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="Will negotiating get the offer withdrawn?">
    A polite, reasoned counter almost never does. Recruiters expect one. Offers are withdrawn over rudeness, dishonesty
    or demands that keep changing.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="I have no other offer. Can I still negotiate?">
    Yes. Use market data for the level and anything you would leave behind. Without another offer, ask for less and be
    ready to accept the first reasonable reply.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="How do I compare equity between two offers?">
    Compare what you would receive each year at today's value, check the vesting schedule and any first-year cliff, and
    remember that equity in a private company may be hard to sell. If the amounts are large, ask someone qualified. This
    page is general guidance, not financial advice.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
