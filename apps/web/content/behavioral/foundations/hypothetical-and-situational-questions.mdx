## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  A hypothetical question tests **judgment in a situation you may never have faced**, so the answer is a position and
  its reasons: **what you would do first, what you are weighing, and what would change your mind**.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

"Imagine a teammate presents your shared project to leadership and takes all the credit. What do you do?"

One candidate says, "That has never happened to me." It is honest and it ends the answer. Another says, "I would go straight to my manager." That is an answer, and it skips the person involved.

A third says: "First I would check whether it was deliberate. People compress things in a five-minute slot. I would talk to them the same day, privately, and say what I saw and that I want us both named next time. If it happened again after that, I would raise it with our manager, with the two examples. Something close to this happened to me with a launch email last year, and a direct conversation settled it."

The third answer has no story behind it for most of its length, and it still shows how the person thinks.

## The five moves

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/behavioral/hypothetical-answer-steps"

  minWidth={700}
  caption="A hypothetical answer states a position early, gives the reasons, and ends on something you have really done."
/>

1. **Clarify, once.** Ask one or two questions that would change your answer: "Is this the first time? Is the deadline fixed?" If the interviewer says "you decide", state your assumption and go on.
2. **Say what you would do first.** A position in one sentence, before the reasons. "I would talk to them directly, the same day."
3. **Give the reasons.** Name the two things you are weighing: the relationship and the record, speed and safety, the customer and the team.
4. **Say what would change your mind, and when you would escalate.** This is where judgment shows. A plan with no branch sounds like a rule you memorised.
5. **Anchor it.** If you have done something like it, say so in one sentence. It turns an opinion into evidence.

The whole answer is about 90 seconds.

**How it differs from a story question.** "Tell me about a time" asks what you did, and the answer is in the past tense with a result. "What would you do if" asks how you decide, and the answer is in the future tense with a branch. If you have a real story that fits exactly, you may say "that happened to me" and tell it, briefly.

## Common hypotheticals, and what each one weighs

| The situation                                       | What you are weighing                    | A sound first move                                        |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| A teammate takes credit for shared work             | The relationship and the record          | A private, direct conversation the same day               |
| You find a serious bug the day before a launch      | The date and the damage                  | Size the damage, then tell the owner with options         |
| Two senior engineers give you opposite instructions | Speed and alignment                      | Put both in one room or thread with the trade-off written |
| Your manager asks for something you think is wrong  | Your judgment and their context          | Ask what they know that you do not, then say your concern |
| A teammate is struggling and it is slowing the team | Their dignity and the team's delivery    | Offer help to them first, privately                       |
| You are given a task with almost no requirements    | Starting and being right                 | Write down your assumptions and confirm them in a day     |
| You will miss a deadline you committed to           | Your reputation and other people's plans | Say so early, with a new date and what you would cut      |
| You see a colleague being talked over in meetings   | Speaking for them and speaking over them | Hand the floor back to them in the meeting                |

Use the table to practise, not to memorise. The interviewer will change one detail ("what if it happens again?", "what if your manager is the problem?") to see whether your answer bends.

## The follow-up that changes the facts

Almost every hypothetical has a second question that moves one fact:

- **"What if that does not work?"** Give the next step up. This is where escalation belongs.
- **"What if you are wrong?"** Say how you would find out, and what you would do then.
- **"What if you had one hour, not one week?"** Say what you would skip and what you would never skip.

Answer the new situation. Do not defend your first answer: the interviewer is not saying it was wrong.

## Build your own

1. Take three rows from the table above. For each, say your first move out loud in one sentence.
2. Add the two things you are weighing.
3. Add the branch: "If that did not work, I would".
4. For each, find the closest real thing you have done and write it in one sentence.

The real examples from step 4 are stories in their own right. Write the strongest in the [Story Builder](/behavioral/practice-lab/story-builder?for=judgment).

## Red flags

- **"It depends", and nothing after it.** **Recover:** say what it depends on, pick the likely case, and answer that.
- **A rule with no branch.** "I would always escalate." **Recover:** say when you would not.
- **Skipping the person.** Going to a manager before the colleague, in a conflict hypothetical. **Recover:** say what you would try directly first.
- **A long story in place of an answer.** If they asked what you would do, answer that first, then anchor it.
- **Asking five clarifying questions.** One or two. More sounds like avoiding the question.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="Which companies ask hypotheticals?">
    They are reported most often in Google loops, alongside past-behavior questions, and in engineering manager
    interviews at many companies. Prepare a few even if your target company mostly asks for stories.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Is there a right answer?">
    Usually not. Two different first moves can both score well if the reasons are sound and the answer bends when the
    facts change. An answer with no reasons scores badly whichever move it picks.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Can I answer with a real story?">
    Yes, if it truly fits. Say "something close to that happened", tell it in under a minute, and then say what you
    would do the same or differently in the situation they described.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
