## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  The last five minutes are your interview of the team. Ask each person about **the thing only they can tell you**, ask
  about **something that happened** and not about opinions, and use the answers to decide whether you want the job.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

Forty minutes into a hard interview, the interviewer says: "We have five minutes left. What questions do you have for me?"

Most candidates are tired and ask one of two things. "What does a typical day look like?" Or: "What do you like most about working here?" Neither is wrong. Neither tells you whether the on-call engineer is woken four nights a week, or whether the plan changes every fortnight.

Another candidate asks the hiring manager this: "If I joined, and we looked back after six months, what would I have done for you to call this a good hire?"

The manager's answer takes a minute, and it tells her three things. There is a specific problem waiting, a billing migration that has stalled twice. The manager has thought about what success looks like. And the problem is one she would enjoy. A vague answer would have told her as much: a manager who cannot say what the new hire will do has probably not decided.

She asks the same question in a later round, to the tech lead. He names a different problem. She now has a third question, for the recruiter.

## Who to ask what

1. **The recruiter** knows the process, the level being considered and the pay range. Ask about those here, and nowhere else.
2. **A peer engineer** knows what the work is like day to day: the code, the releases, the on-call.
3. **The hiring manager** knows what the team lacks and how you would be judged.
4. **A senior leader** knows where the organisation is heading and what it is betting on.
5. **Compare.** Ask two people what the team's top priority is for the next six months. If the answers differ, ask why.

| Who                | A question worth asking                                                                             | What the answer tells you                                         |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Peer engineer**  | "What is the worst piece of technical debt the team has now, and what has stopped it being fixed?"  | Whether engineers get time to fix causes                          |
| **Hiring manager** | "Looking at the team today, what skill or point of view is missing that you hope this hire brings?" | The expectation you will be measured against in your first months |
| **Senior leader**  | "What is the one technical bet for the next year that changes things if it works?"                  | Whether there is a clear direction                                |
| **Anyone**         | "Tell me about a recent decision where two teams disagreed. How was it settled?"                    | Whether decisions are made on evidence or on rank                 |

Questions about something that happened ("tell me about the last incident") get more honest answers than questions about how things are in general ("is on-call bad?").

## A weak set of questions, then the fix

A candidate has five minutes with a peer engineer from the team.

<Tabs title="The same five minutes, twice">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    1. *"What does the company do in the payments area?"*
    2. *"What is the salary range for this role?"*
    3. *"Do you like working here?"*

    **What happens:** the first is on the company's website. The second belongs with the recruiter, and a peer usually cannot answer it. The third gets "yes, the people are great" from almost everyone. The candidate leaves knowing nothing new.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    1. *"Tell me about the last serious incident the team had. What came out of the review, and did those fixes get done?"*
    2. *"How long does it take from merging a ten-line change to seeing it live?"*
    3. *"What would you change about how the team works, if it were up to you?"*

    **What happens:** the engineer describes an outage three weeks ago and one fix that shipped, with two still waiting. Releases take about 40 minutes. He would like fewer meetings on Thursdays. The candidate now knows how the team treats reliability, how fast it ships and what annoys the people on it.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- Each question is one that this person, and only this person, can answer well.
- Two of the three ask about a specific event or a number.
- None could be answered by five minutes of reading beforehand.

## Reading the answers

A few questions and what to listen for.

- **"Did the fixes from your last incident review get done?"** A good sign is a named change that shipped within weeks. A warning sign is "we have a backlog of review items we hope to reach next quarter".
- **"How is time split between features and reliability or debt work?"** A good sign is a rule, such as a fixed share of each sprint. A warning sign is "people fit it in when they can".
- **"When an engineer wants to propose a larger design change, what happens?"** A good sign is a short written proposal that anyone can start. A warning sign is that only certain people may propose.
- **"Tell me about a time engineering pushed back on a date. How did leadership respond?"** A good sign is a real example where scope was cut. A warning sign is no example.
- **For ML teams: "How do you test a model change before release, and what is hardest to catch before users see it?"** A good sign is a fixed test set and an honest account of its gaps.

One vague answer means little. The same vague answer from three people is a pattern.

## Build your own

Decide what you need before you choose your questions.

1. Think of the team where you learned fastest. What three conditions made that possible: freedom, a good mentor, contact with customers, fast releases?
2. Think of the time you were most worn out at work. What early sign could you have spotted in the interview?
3. Turn each of those into one question about something that happened: "Tell me about the last time the plan changed in the middle of a quarter."
4. Write two questions for each kind of interviewer and keep them on a card.

The first prompt is also your answer to "what are you looking for in your next role?" [Write it in the Story Builder](/behavioral/practice-lab/story-builder?for=motivation).

## Red flags

- **A question a search would answer.** "What does your company do?" **Recover:** ask about something behind the public fact: "I read about the move to the new platform. What was hardest about it?"
- **Pay, leave or visas to a technical interviewer.** **Recover:** say "I will take that up with the recruiter" and ask about the work.
- **An interrogation.** "Why are your reviews online so poor?" **Recover:** ask with curiosity about one event: "How does the team handle a quarter when priorities change?"
- **No questions.** It suggests you have not thought about the job. **Recover:** keep one question that works for anyone: "What surprised you most after you joined?"

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="My interviewer is not on the team I would join. What do I ask?">
    This is common at large companies, where interviewers are drawn from across the organisation and the team is chosen
    later. Ask about how the company works: how design reviews run across teams, what surprised them about how people
    are promoted, which internal tool they would miss most. Save team questions for the hiring manager or the
    team-matching conversations.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Can I use this time to fix an answer I gave earlier?">
    Briefly, for a behavioral answer that was cut short. Take 20 seconds: "Before my question, on the migration story, I
    left out the check we added afterwards, which is what stopped repeats." Then ask your question. Do not reopen a
    coding problem. It uses the time and reminds the interviewer of the stumble.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Are my questions scored?">
    We found no company that says so in public, and no good evidence that they are. Treat the five minutes as yours: the
    purpose is to learn whether you want the job. Thoughtful questions do no harm, and "no questions" can read as low
    interest.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="How many questions should I prepare?">
    Two for each interviewer, in case the first is answered during the interview. Five minutes is time for two questions
    with real answers.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
