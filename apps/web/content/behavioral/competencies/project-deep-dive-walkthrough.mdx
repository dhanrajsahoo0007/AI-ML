## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  A project deep dive is scored on **your decisions**, so give a **two-minute overview**, show the design in four boxes,
  and spend most of the time on the two or three hardest choices you made: what you rejected, what broke and what you
  would change.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

Two engineers get the same 30-minute round: "Walk me through a system you built."

The first chooses a platform that 40 people built over three years. He draws 15 services and explains each one. At minute 14 the interviewer interrupts for the first time: "Which of these did you design?" He points at two boxes. There are now 16 minutes left to talk about them, and half of that goes on questions he could have answered at the start.

The second chooses a smaller system that four people built. She spends two minutes on the problem, the scale and her part. She draws four boxes. Then she says: "There were three hard problems here. Which would you like to start with?" The interviewer picks one. For the next 20 minutes they talk about a decision she made, the option she rejected, the bug that followed and what she would do now.

Both systems worked. The second interviewer's notes are full of the candidate's own decisions. The first interviewer's notes are mostly a list of services.

## The layers an interviewer drills into

1. **The two-minute overview.** The problem, the scale in numbers and exactly what you did. Nothing else.
2. **The design in four boxes.** Group everything you did not own into one box called "upstream" or "other services". Then offer three topics.
3. **A decision you made.** The options you had, the numbers that decided it and what you gave up. Most of the round belongs here.
4. **What broke.** Something did. Say how you found out, what it affected and what you did. For example: "During the migration, 0.2 percent of customers were counted twice for six hours. Our nightly comparison caught it and we issued credits the next day."
5. **What you would change.** With what you know today. "Nothing" ends the conversation at the point where a senior candidate has the most to say.

A plan for a 25-minute round:

| Minutes  | Layer                 | What to cover                                                              |
| -------- | --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 0 to 2   | Overview              | The user problem, the scale, the team size and your part                   |
| 2 to 5   | Design                | Four boxes, then: "Which of these three problems shall I go into?"         |
| 5 to 15  | Two decisions         | For each: the options, the measurement or sum that chose one, the downside |
| 15 to 20 | What broke            | The rollout, a failure, how the system behaves when a dependency is down   |
| 20 to 25 | What you would change | What stopped fitting as usage grew, and the design you would choose now    |

The interviewer will interrupt and change the order. The plan is there so that you know which layer you are in and can return to it.

The same opening in about two minutes:

- **The project and its scale (30 seconds).** "I will walk you through the usage metering system for our API platform. It counts 120,000 events a second for 40,000 business customers. It has to answer 'is this customer over their limit' in under 15 ms, and the monthly bill has to match usage to within 0.005 percent."
- **My part (20 seconds).** "We were four engineers. I wrote the design document, built the limit-checking protocol myself and led the move from the old system."
- **The design (40 seconds).** "There are three parts. Each gateway keeps a small local allowance per customer. A regional coordinator hands those allowances out. A ledger records every event and is compared with the bill each night."
- **Three topics (30 seconds).** "There were three hard problems. One: a few very large customers overloading a single partition. Two: counting each event exactly once when a region fails over. Three: moving 40,000 live customers with no lost events. Which would you like to start with?"

What changed:

- No history. The first sentence is the project.
- Four boxes at most, and the candidate's own part is named.
- The ending hands the choice to the interviewer, who can go straight to what they need to score.

## "What would you change today?"

Almost every deep dive ends here.

<Tabs title="One question, two answers">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"Honestly, it worked well and met all its targets, so I would not change much."*

    **What the interviewer writes:** no reflection. Either the candidate has not looked at the system since launch, or will not admit a flaw.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    *"It met the 15 ms target. Six months later we hit something I had not planned for. Sales began selling to groups of companies, where 50 workspaces share one limit. I had keyed everything by a single customer id, so a shared limit meant coordinating across partitions, and limit checks for those customers took twice as long."*

    *"If I designed it today, I would key the allowance by the billing owner from the first day, with smaller allowances under it. I would also drop about 1,800 lines of recovery code I wrote. The database has a built-in feature for it now, which was not ready when we started."*

    **What the interviewer writes:** knows how the system aged, names their own design choice as the cause, has a specific alternative.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Build your own

1. Which project of the last three or four years had the hardest decisions that were yours to make? It does not have to be the largest or the newest.
2. Say the overview aloud with a timer: the problem, the scale, your part, three topics. Stop at two minutes.
3. For each of the three topics, write the option you rejected and why.
4. Write two sentences each for "what broke" and "what I would change".

[Write it in the Story Builder](/behavioral/practice-lab/story-builder?for=strategy). Then practise the drawing on a blank page until four boxes take you under three minutes while you talk.

## Red flags

- **Credit for what you inherited.** **Recover:** say it first. "The database was chosen before I joined. My part was the partition design and fixing the compaction failures." Interviewers respect this and ask about your part.
- **The 15-box tour.** **Recover:** stop, say "let me simplify", and redraw with four boxes.
- **No rejected options.** **Recover:** for the decision you are on, name one alternative and the reason it lost.
- **"Nothing went wrong."** **Recover:** offer the smallest real failure you have. A six-hour double count is enough.
- **Confidential detail.** Customer names, internal code or unreleased products. **Recover:** describe the system with general terms and rounded numbers. No interviewer needs the real names.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="My recent work was simple. Can I present a project from three years ago?">
    Yes. A project from the last few years where the hard decisions were yours is a better choice than a recent one with
    little to discuss. Refresh the numbers, the data model and the failures before the interview, because you will be
    asked about them.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="The interviewer interrupts me every minute. Is it going badly?">
    Usually it is going well. Interruptions mean the interviewer is looking for the edge of what you know. Answer in 30
    to 45 seconds with the conclusion first, ask "shall I go further on that?", then return to the layer you were in.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="I am a new graduate. What project do I use?">
    An internship project, a final-year project or a serious personal one. The layers are the same at a smaller size:
    what it did, your part of it, one choice you made, one thing that went wrong. See the page on new grad and intern
    interviews for more.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
