## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Another team changes how it works when the change **solves a problem they already have** and **costs them almost
  nothing to adopt**. An influence story shows how you found their problem, did most of the work yourself and let the
  first team's result persuade the rest.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A security engineer needs a payments team to move 14 endpoints from an old auth token to a new one. The payments team does not report to her. She sends a design document and a request. The reply comes the same day: the quarter is fully planned, try again in three months.

She does not go to her director. She reads the payments team's on-call log for the last quarter and counts: 30 percent of their pages came from the old token expiring at the wrong moment. She brings that number to their tech lead, as his problem, with her migration as the fix.

He is interested but still has no time. So she pays the cost herself. She builds a small proxy that accepts both tokens, writes a script that rewrites the call sites, and opens a draft pull request in their repository. Their effort drops from about two weeks of work to a 30-minute review.

The payments team merges it. Their token pages go to zero. She writes up the result with their engineers named first, and four more teams ask for the same pull request.

## What the interviewer scores

  spec="animated/behavioral/influence-coalition"

1. **The request meets a wall.** A fair refusal, for a fair reason. A story where the other team agreed at once has nothing to score.
2. **Their problem first.** She found a number in their own on-call log. The proposal became relief for them.
3. **You pay the cost.** A proxy, a rewrite script and a ready pull request. The other team reviews work; it does not do it.
4. **A shared result.** The first team's numbers and names are in the write-up. Other teams now ask, and nobody has to be pushed.

| What is scored            | Strong signal                                                      | Weak signal                                           |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **Finding their problem** | You learned what the other team was already worried about          | A list of what your team needs by your date           |
| **Lowering their cost**   | A script, a pull request or a tool that turns weeks into a review  | A 20-page document that tells 12 teams to do the work |
| **One team first**        | One willing team, a measured result, then the others               | A meeting with every team on day one                  |
| **Credit**                | Their engineers are named in the demo and the update to leadership | "I led a migration across five teams"                 |

The order matters. Many candidates start their story at step three, with the tool they built. Without the refusal and the reason behind it, the tool sounds like ordinary project work, and the interviewer cannot tell that anyone needed persuading.

## When the answer is still no

Some teams will not move. A story that admits this is more believable than one where everyone agreed. Three things are worth saying when it happened to you.

- **What you did on your own side.** A shim, an adapter or a default that kept your project safe without them. In the story above, the proxy that accepted both tokens meant no team had to move on a fixed date.
- **What you learned about timing.** Teams say yes more easily at the start of a planning cycle, after an incident of their own, or when one of their deadlines depends on the change. If you asked in the third week of a full quarter, say so.
- **What you chose not to do.** Perhaps you could have forced it through a director, and you decided the working relationship was worth more than six weeks. That is a judgment an interviewer can score. Say what the delay cost, so the trade is clear.

Then end the story with the count as it really was: nine of eleven, three of four. Say which team has not moved and why.

## A weak answer, then the fix

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time you needed another team to do something that was not on their plan."_

<Tabs title="One story, told twice">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"I work on our design system. Four product teams were still using their own date pickers and modals, which was hurting accessibility. I wrote a detailed migration guide and sent it to all four teams. Nobody did anything for a month, which was frustrating because the guide was very clear. I raised it with my director, who told the teams to prioritise it. Most of them moved over eventually and accessibility got better."*

    **What the interviewer writes:** sent a document, waited, escalated. No sign of what the other teams needed. No numbers.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Situation and task.** *"I work on our design system. Four product teams used their own date pickers and modals, and together they had 22 open accessibility bugs a quarter. None of them had the migration planned."*
    - **Action.** *"My first try was a migration guide sent to all four teams. Nothing happened for a month. So I asked each tech lead what the guide would cost them. The answer was about a sprint each, which they did not have. I did three things. I wrote a script that swaps the imports and props in about 80 percent of cases. I picked the checkout team first, because they had the most bugs, and opened the pull request myself. Then I showed their bug count in the frontend guild meeting, with their engineer presenting."*
    - **Result.** *"Three of the four teams merged within six weeks, and accessibility bugs fell from 22 a quarter to 7. The fourth team was in the middle of a redesign and moved three months later. I did not get them sooner."*
    - **Learning.** *"I no longer send a guide on its own. Any change I ask another team to make now arrives as a pull request they can review."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- The failed first attempt is still there, and now it is the reason for the change of approach.
- The other teams have a reason for saying no, and the candidate removed it.
- The result has a number and an honest gap: one team took three months longer.

## Build your own

1. Think of a proposal of yours that other teams were slow to adopt. How many days of work did it ask of each team?
2. What was that team already worried about: pages, a deadline, an audit, a slow build? Where would you have found the number?
3. What could you have built so that their part was a review?
4. Who on the other team did the most to make it work, and did their manager hear about it from you?

## Red flags

- **Escalation first.** Going to a director to force a peer team to comply. **Recover:** say what you tried directly, and if you did escalate, say that the other lead knew and came with you.
- **"I wrote a document and asked them to follow it."** A document with no tooling is a request for free work. **Recover:** name what you built to cut their effort, even if it was only a template pull request.
- **"They blocked us for two quarters."** The story ends with you waiting. **Recover:** say what you did while blocked: a workaround on your side, or the work done in their repository.
- **All the credit.** "I migrated five teams." **Recover:** name what the other teams' engineers did, and say what you did to make that visible.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="How do I show this if my company has only one engineering team?">
    The people outside your authority are founders, product, sales, support, a customer or a vendor. It also counts when
    you get your own peers to take up a testing or monitoring practice that nobody ordered. Show how you proved it on a
    small case and how many people chose to use it.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Is it ever right to escalate?">
    Yes, when a decision cannot wait and the two teams' goals truly conflict. That is what shared managers are for. Say
    what you tried first, and say that you escalated in the open: both leads, one summary, both options.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="What does starting with one team mean in practice?">
    Pick one team that is willing and visible. Work closely with them until the rough parts of your change are fixed and
    you have one result to show. Other teams are then persuaded by a peer's numbers, which works better than your
    argument.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
