## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  When a date cannot move, a strong answer shows **what you kept in full, what you cut and what you took as debt with a
  payback date**. When you ask for time to pay debt down, a strong answer **prices the debt in the terms the business
  already counts**: days, incidents and money.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A payments engineer has three weeks until a banking regulator's deadline. Missing it means a fine of \$50,000 a day. Three pieces of work remain, and together they are estimated at six weeks: an audit ledger that cannot be altered, automatic reversal of disputed payments, and a report screen for merchants.

He does not propose weekends. He reads 90 days of production logs. Disputes happen about 18 times a day, on fewer than 0.04 percent of payments. Merchants open the report on the first of the month and almost never otherwise.

He brings one page to product, legal and operations:

- **Kept in full:** the audit ledger, its tests and the rollback path. This is what the regulator checks.
- **Cut for now:** the merchant report screen. It ships next month.
- **Taken as debt:** disputes go into a queue, and two operations analysts approve them on a simple internal screen. It costs them ten minutes a day. The automatic worker is booked for the two sprints after launch.

The audit passes on the date. The automatic worker replaces the manual screen 18 days later, reading from the same queue, and nothing upstream changes.

## What moves when the date cannot

1. **The date is fixed.** Say why it cannot move and how large the gap is: three weeks left, six weeks of work.
2. **Kept in full.** Name what you refused to cut. Correct data, security checks, tests on the main path, a way to roll back. An interviewer listens for this list first.
3. **Cut for now.** Features that few people need on day one. Use data to choose them, and say when they ship.
4. **Written down as debt.** A shortcut that is safe at today's volume, kept in one place so it can be replaced, with an owner and a date.
5. **Paid back.** Interviewers nearly always ask whether you went back. Say when, and what it took.

| What is scored               | Strong signal                                                                    | Weak signal                                      |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| **What was protected**       | Data correctness, security and monitoring stayed whole                           | Tests, auth checks or rollback were skipped      |
| **How the cut was chosen**   | Usage data showed which parts few people needed                                  | The team worked 80-hour weeks to keep everything |
| **Where the shortcut lives** | Behind one queue or interface, so replacing it touches one place                 | Small hacks spread across many services          |
| **The case for payback**     | "This module adds four days to every experiment and causes a third of our pages" | "The code is a mess and needs a rewrite"         |

## A weak answer, then the fix

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time you convinced your team to spend time on technical debt."_

<Tabs title="One story, told twice">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"Our Android test suite was in a terrible state. Tests failed randomly and everyone hated it. I kept raising it in retrospectives and telling my manager we needed to stop and fix it properly. Eventually we got a sprint for cleanup, and things were a lot better after that."*

    **What the interviewer writes:** complained until given time. No cost to the business, no proposal, no number for "better".

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Situation and task.** *"I am an Android engineer on a team of six. One in four runs of our UI tests failed with no real bug, so nobody trusted them. Each release needed two days of manual checks by three people. Our product manager wanted to move from releases every two weeks to every week for the holiday experiments."*
    - **Action.** *"I stopped calling it cleanup and wrote half a page. Manual checks cost six engineer-days per release, about 150 days a year. Weekly releases would double that, which we could not staff. I proposed three weeks for two engineers: fix the 40 tests that caused most of the false failures, and add a way to set aside a flaky test automatically. I showed that the time would be paid back within the quarter."*
    - **Result.** *"She agreed to two weeks, not three. False failures fell from 25 percent to 4 percent, and manual checks went from two days to half a day. Weekly releases started six weeks later than she had hoped. Eleven tests are still set aside."*
    - **Learning.** *"I count the cost of a problem in days before I ask for time to fix it. A request with a payback date gets a yes much more often."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- The debt is priced: 150 engineer-days a year, and a product goal it was blocking.
- The request is specific: two people, three weeks, a named list of tests.
- The result is honest. The time was cut to two weeks, and eleven tests remain.

## Build your own

1. When did you last have a date that could not move? What was the gap between the time left and the work?
2. What did you refuse to cut, and why?
3. What shortcut did you take, where does it live, and has it been replaced?
4. What is the most expensive piece of debt in your current system? Price it in days per month.

## Red flags

- **"We worked 16 hours a day."** Effort with no choice in it. **Recover:** add what you cut and who agreed to it.
- **"We skipped the tests."** **Recover:** if it happened, say what it cost later and what you now keep fixed under pressure.
- **A shortcut that is still there.** **Recover:** say what volume or date will trigger the replacement, and who owns it.
- **A rewrite from nothing.** A six-month replacement with no deliveries on the way. **Recover:** describe how you would move one part at a time behind the old interface, with something shipped each month.
- **"The code was ugly."** **Recover:** give the cost in days or incidents.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="How do I answer 'tell me about a time you missed a deadline'?">
    Three parts. What you had not known when you made the estimate. How early you said so, in weeks before the date, and
    what options you offered. What shipped on or near the date, and what you changed about how you estimate. The
    estimate story on the page about production mistakes follows this shape.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="How do I stop a temporary shortcut from becoming permanent?">
    Give it three things on the day you take it: one place in the code, an owner, and a trigger. A trigger can be a date
    or a number, such as "when the manual queue passes 100 a day". In an interview, name all three.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Is it wrong to mention that I worked extra hours?">
    No, if it is one sentence of a story that is mostly about choices. "We also worked two late evenings in the last
    week" is honest. A story where hours are the only method is the problem.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
