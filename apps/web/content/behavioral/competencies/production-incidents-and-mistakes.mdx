## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  A failure story is scored on three things: you say plainly that **it was yours**, you **restored service before you
  looked for the cause**, and you left behind **a check that runs without you**, so the same kind of mistake cannot
  happen again.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

An engineer is on call for checkout the week her own database migration ships. At 10:42 an alert fires: checkout p99 is over 2.5 seconds and errors are burning through the monthly budget 14 times too fast.

She does not open the code. She first asks how bad it is: 18 percent of writes in the EU region are failing. Then she turns off the feature flag and rolls back the release. Ninety seconds later the error rate is back to 0.01 percent. The whole incident lasted six minutes.

Only then does she look for the cause. Her migration added a cascading delete on a column with no index, and under real traffic it locked a table. Staging never showed it because staging has a thousandth of the rows.

Her written review says so in the first line: "My migration caused this." It ends with two changes. The build now fails any migration without a lock timeout, and migrations run against a copy of production traffic before release.

## What the interviewer scores

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/behavioral/incident-ownership"
  stepper
  minWidth={700}
  caption="The incident from the story in four steps: size the damage, restore service, find the gap, and add a check that runs automatically."
/>

1. **Size the damage.** In the first minutes, say who is affected and how badly. That number decides everything after it.
2. **Restore before you debug.** A rollback, a flag or a traffic shift. Customers are back to normal while the cause is still unknown.
3. **Find the gap, without blame.** Ask why the mistake was possible: why staging missed it, why review missed it. "Someone made an error" is where the questions start.
4. **A check that runs without you.** A build rule, a release gate or an automatic rollback. A promise to be careful does not count.

| What is scored     | Strong signal                                                          | Weak signal                                      |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| **Ownership**      | "I caused it", with the effect on customers in numbers                 | A tiny non-failure, or a teammate or QA at fault |
| **Restoring**      | Service restored in minutes, by rollback or flag                       | Two hours of live debugging while requests fail  |
| **Telling people** | Short updates with times to support and leadership during the incident | Silence until it was over                        |
| **Cause**          | The gap in testing, tooling or design that let it through              | "A typo in a config file"                        |
| **Prevention**     | An automatic check, and what it has caught since                       | A new line in a wiki page                        |

The same incident, told so that it can be scored. It opens with a one-sentence headline, which stops a failure story from sounding evasive.

- **Headline.** "I dropped a database index that I believed was unused, and webhook delivery was slow for 12 percent of our large customers for 14 minutes."
- **Situation.** "I was cleaning up indexes on our notifications database. The usage statistics on the primary showed zero scans for one of them, so I dropped it. Four minutes later the primary was at 100 percent CPU and 180,000 deliveries were queued."
- **Action.** "First, I restored service. Rebuilding the index would have taken 40 minutes on a loaded primary, so I turned off the hourly analytics job that needed it and moved that job to a replica. Delivery times were normal at minute 14. Second, I posted the effect and the time window in the incident channel within ten minutes, and gave support the exact window for customer replies. Third, I asked why the statistics had been wrong. A weekly billing job ran only on the replica, and my audit script read only the primary."
- **Result.** "No deliveries were lost. All 180,000 went out once the queue drained."
- **Learning.** "I changed our migration check to read index usage from the primary and every replica over 35 days, and an index is now disabled for seven days before it can be dropped. That check found six more indexes of the same kind across four teams in the next year."

What changed:

- "I dropped it" comes first, with the size of the effect.
- Restoring service comes before the search for the cause, and each has its own time.
- The misleading statistics are now the cause that was found, and the ending is a check with a count of what it caught.

## A failure that is not an outage

The same question can be answered with a missed commitment. The shape is the same.

A tech lead promises a data export feature in six weeks, in time for a customer conference. In week three, her first full test shows that she underestimated the permission checks between customer accounts. She is four weeks short.

She tells her manager and product manager within a day, with the test numbers and two options: slip four weeks, or ship exports as time-limited download links on the date and add account-to-account access three weeks later. They take the second. The feature is shown at the conference, without the part customers had asked for most.

Her estimate had been made from a diagram of the easy path. She now spends two days testing the riskiest boundary before she gives any date. The scored parts are the same: she said it was her estimate, she limited the damage early, and she changed how she estimates.

## Build your own

1. What is the worst thing you have broken, or the commitment you missed by the most? Who noticed, and how?
2. How many minutes passed between the first sign and things being back to normal? What did you do in those minutes?
3. Why was the mistake possible? Ask "why" until you reach a test, a tool or a habit.
4. What runs today because of it, and what has it caught?

## Red flags

- **Someone else's fault.** "A junior engineer pushed a bad change." **Recover:** if you reviewed it or led the team, own the gap: "My review and our build both let a query with no index reach production."
- **A failure that is not one.** A typo caught in staging, or "I worked too hard". **Recover:** offer a real one. Interviewers will ask for it anyway.
- **Debugging before restoring.** **Recover:** say it as the learning: "I spent 40 minutes looking for the cause. Today I roll back in the first five."
- **"We will be more careful."** **Recover:** name the check you added, or the one you would add now.
- **A story with no feeling at all.** A real failure costs something. One honest sentence about how it felt makes the rest believable.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="I have never caused a big outage. What can I use?">
    Three things work. An incident you handled while on call, where you led the response and the review. A near miss: a
    bug of yours that a small rollout caught at 1 percent of traffic, and what you added afterwards. Or a mistake that
    was not an outage: a missed estimate, a design you had to undo, a requirement you misread.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="How bad should the failure be?">
    Bad enough that someone outside your team noticed, and small enough that you can explain it in two minutes. A
    student can use a group project that missed its deadline. What matters is that the mistake was real and yours.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Should I mention other people who contributed to the failure?">
    Describe the system and leave out the names. "The review did not catch it" is accurate and blames nobody. Then
    return to your own part.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
