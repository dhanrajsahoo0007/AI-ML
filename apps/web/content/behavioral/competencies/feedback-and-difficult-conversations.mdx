## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Feedback questions come in a pair. **Giving** it shows whether you will have an uncomfortable conversation in private,
  about what someone did and what it cost. **Receiving** it shows whether a real criticism changed **a habit you can
  name**.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A payments engineer notices a pattern in a teammate she likes working with. He is fast, and during his on-call weeks he merges quick fixes without tests. Two of them have broken something at the weekend. The second time, another engineer spent three hours on a Sunday night working out what the fix had changed.

She could mention it at stand-up, or tell their manager. She does neither. On Monday she asks him for 15 minutes, in private.

She starts with what happened: two hotfixes, no tests. Then what it cost: a three-hour page for someone else. Then she asks a question and stops talking: "What makes the tests hard to add?" His answer surprises her. The test harness takes 18 minutes to start on a laptop, so under pressure he skips it.

They spend two hours that week replacing the slow part of the harness with an in-memory version that starts in 12 seconds. He agrees to ship no hotfix without a test. She agrees to review his within the hour.

## What the interviewer scores

  spec="animated/behavioral/feedback-conversation"

1. **Observation.** What happened, as a camera would have recorded it. "Two hotfixes merged with no test." No "careless", no "always".
2. **Effect.** What it cost a person or the team. This is why the conversation is worth having.
3. **Question.** Ask what is behind it, then listen. Half the time the cause is something you can help remove.
4. **Agreement.** One change each, said out loud. It is easier to accept feedback from someone who also takes on work.
5. **Check back.** A month later she asked how it had gone. He had slipped once, in a deadline week, and raised it himself the same day. Feedback with no follow-up is a single conversation, and the interviewer wants to know whether the habit changed.

This order is close to the Situation, Behavior, Impact model that many managers are taught. The question and the agreement are the parts candidates most often leave out.

| What is scored  | Giving feedback                                       | Receiving feedback                                        |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Specific**    | An action and its effect, with no label on the person | A real gap in how you worked, in the words you were given |
| **Direct**      | In private, within a day or two                       | You listened and asked for examples before you explained  |
| **A change**    | Something you both did differently afterwards         | A habit, a checklist or a rule you can describe           |
| **Followed up** | You checked a month later                             | You asked the same person whether it had improved         |

## A weak answer, then the fix

**Question:** _"Tell me about a piece of feedback that was hard to hear."_

<Tabs title="One question, two answers">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"My manager once told me that I take on too much and care too much about quality. I can be a bit of a perfectionist. I took that on board, and I try to delegate more now and keep a better balance."*

    **What the interviewer writes:** a strength presented as a weakness, no moment where it cost anything, no change that can be checked. Most interviewers follow with: "Set that one aside. Tell me about feedback that stung."

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Situation.** *"I am an ML engineer. After my third experiment report last year, my manager passed on feedback from our product manager: she could not tell from my reports whether to ship. The decision was on page four, after the method."*
    - **My first reaction.** *"I was annoyed. I thought the detail was the point. Then I counted the questions she had asked on the last report. There were nine, and seven were answered in the report, just too far down."*
    - **Action.** *"I asked her to show me a report she found easy to use. I changed my template: the first line is the recommendation and the one number behind it, then the risk, then everything else as an appendix. After four reports I asked her directly whether it was better."*
    - **Result.** *"Her questions per report went from about nine to two. One launch was decided in the review meeting, where before it took another week of messages."*
    - **Where it stands.** *"My appendices are still too long. A teammate told me so last month, and I am working on it."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- The feedback is real and a little embarrassing, and it is quoted as it was given.
- The candidate admits the first reaction, then shows what they checked.
- The change is a template anyone could look at, and the person who gave the feedback was asked again later.

## Feedback to someone more senior

The hardest version is feedback upwards: to a tech lead, a manager or a product manager. The five moves are the same. Two things change.

- **Bring a count.** A senior person hears "the priorities keep changing" as a complaint. They hear "four of the last six sprints had a priority change after Wednesday, and those four produced nine of our eleven escaped bugs" as information.
- **Bring a proposal they can accept or adjust.** For example: changes after Wednesday go into a short queue that is reviewed on Friday, unless a customer is blocked.

A mobile engineer who said this to her product manager got part of what she asked for. The Friday queue was adopted. The exception for urgent requests was used so often in the first month that she had to raise it a second time, with a new count. That second conversation is worth as much in an interview as the first. It shows she cared whether the problem was fixed, and was not satisfied with having been heard once.

## Build your own

1. What is the last criticism that annoyed you when you heard it? Write down the words that were used.
2. What did you change because of it, and how would a colleague be able to see that change?
3. When did you last tell a peer something they did not want to hear? What did you say first?
4. Have you ever given feedback to your manager, a tech lead or a product manager? What happened next?

[Write it in the Story Builder](/behavioral/practice-lab/story-builder?for=feedback). Prepare one giving story and one receiving story. They are usually asked close together.

## Red flags

- **A label for a person.** "He was lazy." **Recover:** go back to the event: "In two sprints in a row, his changes were opened on Friday afternoon without tests, and sign-off slipped to the next week."
- **The disguised strength.** "I was told I care too much." **Recover:** offer a second, real one straight away, before you are asked.
- **Feedback given through a manager.** **Recover:** if that is what happened, say so and say what you would do now: speak to the person first.
- **Getting defensive in the interview.** An interviewer may challenge a choice in your story to see how you take it. **Recover:** pause, say what is fair in the challenge, then give your constraint. This is a live test of the same skill.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="Nobody gives me critical feedback. What do I say?">
    Go and get some before your interviews. "Do you have any feedback for me?" gets a polite no. Ask something narrow
    after a project: "What is one thing I could have done to make your team's side of this easier?" The answer, and what
    you did with it, is your story.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Can the feedback I received be technical?">
    Yes, if it changed how you work and not only one pull request. "My reviewer said my changes were too large to
    review, so I now split anything over 400 lines" is a good small story for a junior or mid-level role.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="What if the feedback conversation went badly?">
    It can still be a good story. Say what you did, how the person reacted, what you would change about how you raised
    it, and where things stand now. An honest partial result is more believable than a perfect one.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
