## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  An ambiguity story is about **what you wrote down and tested in the first week**. The interviewer wants to hear how a
  one-line goal became a goal with a number, a list of what you would not do, one tested unknown and a first small
  release.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A backend engineer is told by her director: "Enterprise customers are leaving because the dashboard is slow. Fix it this quarter." There is no spec and no target. Six teams own parts of the dashboard.

She does not start by tuning queries. She spends two days on one page.

First she joins load-time data to the list of accounts that left. Median load time has no link to leaving. What the lost accounts share is Monday mornings: admins with more than 10,000 seats waiting over eight seconds for the first load of the week.

Her page says three things. **Goal:** Monday first-load p99 for large accounts from 8.4 seconds to under 1.5. **Not doing:** no rewrite of the six teams' services this quarter. **Biggest unknown:** whether account totals can be built ahead of time without touching those services.

A two-day test says yes. In week two, one customer gets pre-built totals. By week five, the p99 is 920 ms. Of five accounts that had threatened to leave, three stay and two still go.

## From one line to a plan

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/behavioral/ambiguity-to-plan"

/>

1. **The one-line goal.** Say it as you received it. The interviewer needs to hear how little you were given.
2. **What you assumed, on one page.** A goal with a number, what you will not do, and the questions still open. The "not doing" list is often the most useful part, because people who disagree with it tell you so in week one.
3. **The biggest unknown, tested first.** Pick the assumption that would end the project if it were false, and spend a day or two on it before building the easy parts.
4. **A first milestone.** Something real, for one customer or one team, within two weeks. What they do with it shapes the rest.
5. **A check-in.** A set moment where the people who gave you the goal can say keep going, change direction or stop.

| What is scored                 | Strong signal                                                           | Weak signal                                                |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Setting the limits**         | A one-page plan within two days, with things you will not do            | Six weeks of building from a chat message                  |
| **Order of work**              | The riskiest unknown is tested in week one                              | Four weeks on screens and scaffolding before the hard part |
| **Room for change**            | The parts likely to change sit behind a setting, a flag or an interface | Business rules written deep into the database schema       |
| **Early contact with reality** | A thin working version in week two                                      | A reveal in week eight                                     |

## A weak answer, then the fix

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time the requirements changed while you were building."_

<Tabs title="One story, told twice">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"I was building a weekly active users report for the leadership team. The requirements kept changing. First they wanted logins, then they wanted any action, then finance wanted paying users only. I rewrote the pipeline three times and worked two weekends to get it done. It was chaotic, but I delivered it in the end."*

    **What the interviewer writes:** worked hard, complained about change, rebuilt the same thing three times. Nothing shows the candidate did anything to make the next change cheaper.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Situation and task.** *"I am a data engineer. Leadership asked for a weekly active users report in four weeks. Nobody had defined 'active'."*
    - **Action.** *"My first version counted logins, and a week later product wanted any action counted. I had built the definition into the pipeline, so that change cost me three days. I did two things so the next change would not. I moved the definition into one SQL view, separate from the pipeline. And I wrote a half-page with three candidate definitions and the number each one produced, and asked product and finance to choose in one meeting. They could not fully agree, so the report shows two numbers with labels."*
    - **Result.** *"When finance later asked for paying users only, the change took one afternoon. The report shipped a week after the first date. Four teams use it now."*
    - **Learning.** *"If nobody can define a term in the first meeting, I treat it as something that will change, and I keep it out of the pipeline."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- The first rewrite is still in the story, as the reason for the change of approach.
- The candidate made the open question visible, with numbers, and got a decision.
- The result is honest: one week late, and two numbers where one was wanted.

## Decisions that are hard to undo

Under ambiguity, speed comes from knowing which decisions can be changed later.

- **Hard to undo:** a public API, the key a database is partitioned by, a data model other teams build on, deleting data. Slow down for these. Get a second opinion before you commit.
- **Easy to undo:** a batch size, a screen behind a flag, a ranking rule held in a setting. Decide these quickly and move on.

In a story, one sentence is enough: "The ledger format was hard to change later, so I spent two days on it. The payment provider was behind an interface, so I picked one and moved on." It tells the interviewer that you spent your care where it mattered.

## Build your own

1. What is the vaguest instruction you have been given at work or on a course project? Write it in the words that were used.
2. What did you write down, measure or test in the first few days?
3. What did you decide not to do, and who agreed to that?
4. What changed halfway, and how many days did the change cost you?

## Red flags

- **Complaining about change.** "The requirements kept moving." **Recover:** say what the team was learning that caused the change, then what you did to make the next one cheap.
- **Rewrites as proof of effort.** Three rebuilds and two weekends show stamina. They also show the design could not take a change. **Recover:** say what you changed after the first rewrite.
- **Waiting for clarity.** "I was blocked until product finished the spec." **Recover:** name the questions you wrote down and who you took them to.
- **Planning without testing.** Four weeks on a 30-page design with no prototype and no data. **Recover:** name the one unknown you would now test in the first two days.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="How do I answer 'how do you know when you have enough information to start'?">
    Give a rule and an example. One workable rule: start when you know most of what you need and you have put a limit on
    the worst outcome, with a flag, a small rollout or a format you can migrate. Past that point you learn faster from a
    small release than from more discussion.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Is 'my manager did not give me enough detail' an ambiguity story?">
    Only if the story is about what you did next. Missing detail in a ticket is a small version of ambiguity, and it
    suits a junior or mid-level answer. For a senior answer, start from a symptom or a goal, with no solution given.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="What if the project was cancelled at the check-in?">
    That can be a strong story. Say what the first milestone showed, who decided to stop and what was saved by stopping
    in week three and not in week ten.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
