## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  A conflict question is scored on **how the disagreement ended**. The interviewer wants to hear that you understood the
  other person's goal, settled it with **a small test you both trusted**, and backed the decision afterwards, whoever
  turned out to be right.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A backend engineer on an orders team is designing how the service publishes an "order placed" event. She wants a transactional outbox: write the event to a table in the same database transaction as the order, and let a poller publish it. A senior teammate wants to write to the database and then to the message queue directly, because it is simpler and adds no delay.

The design document collects 23 comments in four days. Neither of them moves.

She stops replying in the thread and books 30 minutes with him. She opens by stating his case: a poller on his last team once fell 40 minutes behind during a sale. They agree on two numbers the design must meet: publish delay under 15 ms at p99, and zero lost events.

She spends four hours on a test that kills pods in the middle of a write. The direct write loses 0.3 percent of events on a restart. Her outbox loses none, but her poller adds 80 ms. He suggests waking the poller with a database notification, which brings the delay to 9 ms. The final design carries both names.

## What the interviewer scores

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/behavioral/conflict-resolution"

  caption="The story above as four steps: two proposals, the other side stated fairly, a short test, and one shared design."

1. **The deadlock.** Two proposals, each good for a different goal. A story where the other option was simply wrong has nothing to score.
2. **The other side, stated fairly.** She wrote down why direct writes made sense and what he was protecting. Then they agreed what the design had to achieve, in numbers.
3. **A short test.** Four hours, not four weeks. The test was chosen so that either result would change someone's mind.
4. **One design, shared.** His idea fixed the weak point in hers. The document names both of them.

| What is scored                       | Strong signal                                                               | Weak signal                                           |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **Understanding the other position** | You can explain why their proposal was reasonable                           | "It was bad practice"                                 |
| **Opinions to evidence**             | A test, a replay or a cost model that both sides accepted before it was run | Three weeks of comments, or a vote                    |
| **Talking directly**                 | You moved a stuck thread to a conversation with the person                  | You copied in a manager before speaking to them       |
| **After the decision**               | You took their best point, or backed their design fully when yours lost     | "I told them so" when a problem appeared months later |

The same disagreement, told so that it can be scored. In this version the candidate turns out to be wrong, which is a strong story to have.

- **Situation and task.** "Our rider app had to show the last 50 trips with no network, and we had one two-week release cycle. I wanted a flat file of JSON. A colleague wanted SQLite."
- **Action.** "First, I asked him what he was protecting against. He knew something I did not: search and date filters were planned for the next quarter, with 500 trips, not 50. Second, we agreed on two measures: added start-up time under 100 ms on a three-year-old phone, and a date filter that does not load every trip. Third, I measured both. My file added 40 ms and SQLite added 85 ms, but my file could only filter by loading everything. I changed my position in the pull request, and I added lazy opening so the database is not touched during start-up."
- **Result.** "Added start-up time ended at 30 ms. We shipped inside the two weeks. Search took three days the next quarter and needed no data migration."
- **Learning.** "Before I argue for the simpler option, I now ask what is planned for the next two quarters."

What changed:

- The colleague has a reason, and the candidate went and found it.
- The test had pass marks agreed before it ran, so its result ended the argument.
- The ending has numbers, and the candidate says plainly that they changed their mind.

## Build your own

1. Think of the last design document or pull request where the comments went back and forth more than three times. What was the other person trying to protect?
2. What could you have measured in a day that would have changed your mind or theirs?
3. Find one disagreement where you were wrong. What did the other person know that you did not?
4. What exists today because of that disagreement: a test, a rule, a section in a template?

[Write it in the Story Builder](/behavioral/practice-lab/story-builder?for=conflict). Prepare two versions: one where your evidence won, and one where theirs did. Interviewers often ask for the second straight after the first.

## Red flags

- **The villain.** The other person is stubborn, careless or political. **Recover:** stop and add one sentence that gives their reason: "To be fair to him, he had been paged for exactly this failure a year before."
- **The trivial conflict.** A ten-minute debate about naming where the reviewer said "good point". **Recover:** choose a disagreement where something measurable was at stake and it took more than one conversation.
- **Escalation as the fix.** "I took it to my manager and they agreed with me." **Recover:** say what you tried directly first. If you did escalate, say that you went together with a shared summary of both options.
- **Half of each.** A compromise that takes a piece of both designs to keep the peace. **Recover:** show the measure that decided which parts stayed.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="What if I ran the test, it supported me, and the other person still would not agree?">
    Say what you did next, in order. You asked what risk you were still missing, because there is often a worry that has
    not been said, such as on-call load. You offered a reversible trial on a small share of traffic with a rollback rule
    agreed in advance. If a decision was needed that day and it was hard to undo, you wrote a one-page summary of both
    options with them and took it to the decision maker together.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Can my conflict story be with a product manager or a designer?">
    Yes. Keep one story with another engineer and one with a different function. Some interviewers ask specifically for
    a technical disagreement with an engineer, and others ask about a trade-off with product or design.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Do I need a story where I lost the argument?">
    Yes. "Tell me about a time the other person was right" is a common second question. An answer that shows you
    changing your mind because of evidence is scored at least as well as one where you won.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
