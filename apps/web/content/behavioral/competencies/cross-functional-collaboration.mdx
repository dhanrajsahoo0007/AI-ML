## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  A cross-functional story shows that you can turn a technical limit into **a choice the other person can make**: what
  each option costs, what the user gets, and what you recommend. "That is not possible" and silent agreement both lose
  points.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A product manager and a designer bring a full-stack engineer a mock for a shared document editor. It shows, live, which paragraph every reader is looking at. Large customers have up to 5,000 readers in one document.

She estimates the work: seven weeks to rebuild how updates fan out, and the launch is in five. She does not say "too hard". She asks what problem the feature solves, and the three of them watch the research clips together. Authors were upset about one thing: two people editing the same section at once. Nobody had asked to see where readers were.

She comes back the next day with three options on one page:

- **A.** Live position for every reader. Seven weeks. Misses the launch.
- **B.** Nothing. No cost. The editing clashes continue.
- **C.** Live cursors only for people who are typing, plus a count of readers that refreshes every ten seconds. Six days.

They choose C in the meeting. It ships with the launch, and support tickets about editing clashes fall by about two thirds over the next quarter.

## What each partner needs from you

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/behavioral/cross-functional-handoffs"
  stepper
  minWidth={700}
  caption="You hold the cost, the limits and the risk. Each partner needs them in a different form."
/>

1. **You** are the only person in the room who knows what a request costs, where the system's limits are and what could go wrong. The others cannot decide well until you tell them.
2. **A product manager** owns what gets built and when. Give them options with a cost in days and a recommendation. A single estimate leaves them nothing to choose.
3. **A designer** owns how it feels to use. Give them the limits before the design is finished: what is slow, what can fail, how many items a screen can hold.
4. **Security and legal** own the rare, very expensive failures. Give them the data flow in writing: what is stored, where, who can read it and how it is deleted. Ask what must be true for them to approve.
5. **Support and customers** need plain words: what they will see, whether anything is lost, and a date.

| What is scored            | Strong signal                                                              | Weak signal                                                  |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Plain language**        | The trade-off stated as user effect, cost in days and risk                 | A lecture on partition rebalancing                           |
| **Early contact**         | Security and design saw a one-page plan in the first week                  | A privacy review requested two days before launch            |
| **A counter-offer**       | You found the one requirement causing most of the cost and proposed a swap | Six weeks spent building a spec you knew had a 3-day version |
| **Shared responsibility** | You agreed the success measure with product before building                | "My code worked. The product just did not convert."          |

## A weak answer, then the fix

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time a security, privacy or legal requirement got in the way of a launch."_

<Tabs title="One story, told twice">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"We were building a feature to summarise support tickets with a model, and I needed real tickets to test it. Legal blocked us for weeks because of privacy rules. It was a lot of red tape. I kept explaining that the data would stay internal, and eventually my manager escalated and we got an exception. The feature launched and worked well."*

    **What the interviewer writes:** sees legal as an obstacle, never learned what they needed, won by escalation and an exception. No numbers.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Situation and task.** *"I am an ML engineer. We were building ticket summaries for support agents, and I needed past tickets to measure quality. Our privacy counsel refused access to the ticket tables."*
    - **Action.** *"I asked for 45 minutes with her and our security architect, and I asked one question: what would have to be true for this to be acceptable? There were two things. No names, emails or card details could leave the production network. And if a customer asked to be deleted, their data had to be gone from my test set within 24 hours. I built a job inside production that replaces personal details with placeholders, and I connected the test set to the existing deletion queue. Then I showed them the audit log on real examples."*
    - **Result.** *"They approved it in four days. The first audit sample was not clean: 3 of 500 tickets still had an order number in free text. I added a second pattern check, which cost a week. We ended with a test set of 50,000 tickets and found two quality problems before launch that we would have missed."*
    - **Learning.** *"I now send a one-page data flow to privacy and security in the first week of any project that touches customer data."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- Legal has a reason, and the candidate went to find out what it was.
- The policy became two things that could be built and checked.
- The result includes a miss, the 3 in 500, and what it cost.

## Saying a technical limit in plain words

"Why does a one-line change take two weeks?" is a fair question from someone who cannot see the system. An answer that works has three parts.

1. **Start with their decision.** "You are choosing between showing the live total and shipping this month."
2. **Describe what happens to the user's data, without service names.** "Today those two numbers are kept in two places that are matched once a night. To show them together live, we either build a link between the two, which is two weeks, or show a 'pending' label until the nightly match, which is two days."
3. **Check by asking for the choice.** If they can choose, you explained it well enough.

## Build your own

1. When did a spec look far more expensive than the person asking realised? What was the one requirement causing most of the cost?
2. What was the user problem behind the request, and how did you find out?
3. When did a security, privacy or legal rule shape something you built? What did they need to be true?
4. When did you last explain a failure to support or to a customer? What did you say in the first sentence?

## Red flags

- **Blaming the partner.** "The product manager kept changing their mind." **Recover:** say what was changing in the market or the data, and what you did to make the next change cheaper.
- **"Legal blocked us."** **Recover:** name the rule and the risk behind it, then what you built to meet it.
- **A flat no.** "I told them it was not possible." **Recover:** add the options you offered next, even if there were only two.
- **Silent building.** You built the expensive version without saying a cheaper one existed. **Recover:** say what you would raise now, and at which meeting.
- **Unrequested rework.** Three sprints spent rewriting something that worked, without agreeing the timing with product. **Recover:** say how you now agree the timing of such work in advance.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="What if product wants to launch and I think the reliability risk is too high?">
    Put a number on the risk and offer a staged release. For example: "At full traffic our load test shows a 15 percent
    error rate above 5,000 requests a second. We can release to 2 percent today with a switch-off if errors pass 0.5
    percent, and to everyone next week." The product manager gets to learn from real users, and the risk has a limit.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="I have never worked with legal or security. What story can I use?">
    Use the partner you have worked with: a designer, a data analyst, a support lead, a teacher on a course project. The
    scoring is the same. They had a goal, you had a limit, and you gave them a choice.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Should I say what the partner got wrong?">
    Only if you also say what you did about it and what you got wrong yourself. A story in which every mistake belongs
    to another function reads as blame.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
