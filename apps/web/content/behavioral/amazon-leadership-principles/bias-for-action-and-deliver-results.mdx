## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  **Bias for Action** means you decide with part of the data when the decision can be undone, and you make it undoable
  when it cannot. **Deliver Results** means you still finish the thing that matters, at the right quality, when a
  dependency or a plan fails.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

Two teams have the same problem. Three weeks before a committed launch, the data platform team says the new event cluster they promised will be a month late.

Team A stops. They mark their goal "blocked by data platform" and wait four weeks.

Team B's tech lead spends two hours writing a small publisher interface with three methods. Behind it she puts a 150-line outbox table in the database the team already runs. It handles 500 events a second, which is enough for launch. The feature ships on the original date. When the cluster arrives a month later, swapping the code behind the interface takes one afternoon.

Both teams had the same missing dependency. Team B asked which part of the decision they could undo later. The choice of event system looked permanent. Behind an interface, it became a choice they could change in an afternoon.

An interviewer rarely learns much from a project that went to plan. The story worth telling is the one where the plan broke.

A common shorthand is the door. A **two-way door** is a decision you can walk back through. A **one-way door** is one you cannot.

1. **Ask what it costs to be wrong.** In the story, the team had perhaps two thirds of the facts and a date.
2. **A one-way door deserves the slow path.** Breaking a public API, dropping a column and a hard cutover cannot be undone.
3. **Add something that makes it reversible.** An adapter, a flag, a second write path or a shadow read.
4. **Then move fast.** A 1 percent rollout with a rollback you have tested is a two-way door.

### Bias for Action

In our words: speed matters, many decisions can be reversed and do not need long study, and a calculated risk is welcome. Amazon's own text makes the same point about reversible decisions (see Sources).

| A strong story shows                                               | What weakens it                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| You name what you did not know when you decided                    | "We just had to move fast"                      |
| You say what waiting would have cost: days, money, a missed date   | No cost of delay, so the speed looks like haste |
| You made the decision reversible, and you name the rollback signal | An irreversible change shipped without review   |
| The bet was sometimes wrong, and the rollback worked               | Every fast decision turned out right            |

### Deliver Results

In our words: focus on the few inputs that matter to the business, deliver them on time and at the right quality, and keep going after a setback.

| A strong story shows                                               | What weakens it                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| You cut scope and kept quality: fewer features, the same checks    | You kept the scope and cut the tests        |
| You went around a blocked dependency with something safe and small | "Blocked" as the end of the story           |
| A number for the result, and what did not ship                     | "It shipped and everyone was happy"         |
| The method would work again without overtime                       | "We worked nights and weekends" as the plan |

## A weak answer, then the fix

### Bias for Action

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time you made an important decision without all the data."_

<Tabs title="Bias for Action">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"We had to choose a partition key for a new audit log store, and we had no load data for the big tenants. I said we should just go with the tenant id because we had to move fast. It worked out fine in the end."*

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Situation and task.** *"Ten days before launch I had to choose the partition key for a new audit log store. I had traffic data for our small tenants and none for the five large tenants arriving next quarter."*
    - **Action.** *"Waiting for their data meant six weeks. A partition key cannot be changed later without moving all the data, so I looked for a way to make the choice reversible. I added a bucket number to the key and set it per tenant in config: 1 for a small tenant, up to 16 for a busy one. Reads merge the buckets. I also wrote down the signal for widening a tenant: more than 1 percent of their writes throttled for five minutes."*
    - **Result.** *"We launched on the date. In month two the largest tenant ran a backfill at 4,500 writes a second. I raised their bucket count from 1 to 16 in config and the throttling stopped within a minute. Their reads got about 30 ms slower, which we accepted."*
    - **Learning.** *"New multi-tenant tables on our team now start with the bucket in the key."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- The unknown is named: no load data for five tenants.
- The cost of waiting is named: six weeks.
- The decision was made reversible before it was made fast, and the answer admits the 30 ms cost.

### Deliver Results

The weak version of this answer is the exercise in the next section. Try it first, then come back to the fix.

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time a project you owned hit a roadblock. How did you deliver?"_

- **Situation and task.** _"Four weeks before a contract date, the GPU quota for our document classifier was frozen. The contract covered 11 document types."_
- **Action.** _"I profiled a week of traffic and found that 78 percent of documents matched 12 standard templates. Those did not need the large model. In six days I built a first stage on CPU: a small classifier that answers when its confidence is above 0.92 and passes everything else to the GPU pool we already had. I ran both paths side by side for three days and compared every answer."_
- **Result.** _"Nine of the 11 document types went live on the contract date with accuracy unchanged at 96 percent. Two types with handwriting failed the comparison, so I told the customer a week ahead and shipped them 12 days late. Average cost per document fell by 71 percent."_
- **Learning.** _"A CPU baseline is now required before anyone on the team asks for GPU quota."_

Why this one works: the candidate cut **scope** (two document types, with notice) and kept **quality** (the side-by-side check). The result is partial, and it is told plainly.

## Build your own

1. Think of a decision your team debated for more than two weeks. Write whether it could have been undone, and what a one-day test would have settled.
2. Think of the last time a dependency was late. Write what you built or cut so that your own date held, or what you would do now.
3. Find a fast decision of yours that was wrong. Write how long it took to reverse and what it cost.
4. Write your habit under a deadline: you cut quality, or you freeze and try to ship everything. Then find the story where you did neither.

## Red flags

- **"We worked nights and weekends."** Long hours do not repeat and say nothing about judgment. Recover by naming the decision that saved the date: the cut, the adapter or the simpler design.
- **A one-way door treated as a two-way door.** A public API change or an access-control change shipped without review is not a speed story. Recover by choosing a story where you made the change reversible first.
- **Speed with nothing unknown.** If you had all the data, the story is about execution. Recover by naming what was missing and what waiting would have cost.
- **The date held and quality did not.** Recover by saying which checks you kept and which features you moved out.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="What if my fast decision turned out to be wrong?">
    It can be one of your best stories if the reversal worked. Say what waiting for certainty would have cost, what
    small test you ran, how quickly it showed you were wrong, and that you rolled back with no harm to users. A two-day
    test that saves three weeks of building the wrong thing is a result.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="What are everyday examples of each kind of door?">
    One-way: a public API or webhook format that customers depend on, dropping a database column, a change to who can
    read whose data. Two-way: an internal call between two of your own services, a new nullable column, a cache
    lifetime, anything behind a flag.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="My project missed its date. Can I still use it?">
    Yes, if you can show what you did when you saw the slip coming. Say when you knew, who you told, what you cut and
    what shipped. A date missed by 12 days with a week of notice is a stronger story than a date held by luck.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
