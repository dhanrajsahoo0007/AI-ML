## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
**Bias for Action** is the skill of turning slow, high-risk "one-way doors" into fast, reversible "two-way doors" using feature flags, canaries, and adapters, while **Deliver Results** is how you route around broken dependencies without sacrificing core invariants.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

Two engineering teams face the same crisis: three weeks before a committed launch, the data-platform team announces that the new real-time Kafka cluster they promised won't be ready for another month.

Team A treats the external slip as an excuse: they halt development, mark their OKR "Blocked by Data Platform," and wait four weeks.
Team B practices **Bias for Action + Deliver Results**: their tech lead spends two hours defining a 3-method `EventPublisher` interface, backs it with a 150-line Postgres `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` outbox table using their existing database, and ships the product feature on the original deadline at 500 events/sec. When the Kafka cluster finally lands a month later, swapping the implementation behind `EventPublisher` takes a single afternoon.

Amazon interviewers don't want stories where everything went smoothly according to a Gantt chart; they want to know what you did when the Gantt chart caught fire.

| Principle | Core Engineering Mechanism | Bar Raiser Follow-Up Probe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bias for Action** | Classifying **One-Way vs. Two-Way Doors**; making decisions at ~70% information; bounding blast radius with canaries and kill switches | *"What was the cost of waiting another 2 weeks for 95% certainty, and how fast could you roll back if your 70% bet was wrong?"* |
| **Deliver Results** | Decoupling blocked dependencies via shims/interfaces; ruthless scope triage (must-have invariants vs. deferrable nice-to-haves) | *"What did you cut to hit that date, and how did you ensure the cut didn't leave behind silent operational debt?"* |

## Worked engineering example (STAR+L)

<Tabs title="Two-Way Door vs. Dependency Rescue">
  <Tab label="Bias for Action (Converting a 1-Way Door)">
    **Prompt:** *"Tell me about a time you made a critical decision without having all the data."*

    - **Situation & Task:** *"We needed to choose a partitioning key for a new multi-tenant audit-log store in DynamoDB 10 days before launch. We had traffic profiles for SMB tenants, but zero production traces for the 5 Fortune-500 tenants onboarding in Q3, giving us ~65% certainty on hot-partition risk."*
    - **Action:** *"Waiting 6 weeks for enterprise load traces would delay the launch, but picking a static `tenant_id` partition key was a **one-way door** if a large tenant spiked above 1,000 WCU/sec. I converted it into a **two-way door** by adding a write-sharding suffix (`tenant_id#bucket_0..N`) controlled by a dynamic per-tenant config map, defaulting N=1 for SMBs and allowing us to widen any noisy tenant to N=16 buckets live with zero data migration while an async scatter-gather reader merged queries."*
    - **Result:** *"We launched on schedule. When our largest enterprise tenant ran an unannounced 4,500 writes/sec backfill in month two, I bumped their shard factor from 1 to 16 via config in 30 seconds with zero throttling."*
    - **Learning:** *"We standardized configurable write-sharding suffixes across all new multi-tenant DynamoDB tables."*
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Deliver Results (Rescuing a Blocked Launch)">
    **Prompt:** *"Tell me about a time a project you owned hit a major roadblock and how you delivered anyway."*

    - **Situation & Task:** *"Four weeks before a contractual enterprise launch for our document-classification pipeline, the GPU cluster quota our inference service depended on was frozen by cloud capacity shortages."*
    - **Action:** *"Rather than slipping the contract date, I profiled our traffic and found 78% of documents matched 12 standard templates that didn't need a 7B parameter LLM. I built a two-tier cascade in 6 days: a fast CPU-based ONNX embedding + linear classifier handled high-confidence (over 0.92) standard docs in 18 ms on standard EC2 instances, routing only the ambiguous 22% tail to our existing small GPU pool."*
    - **Result:** *"We hit the contractual launch date on zero new GPUs, cut average inference cost by 71%, and reduced p50 latency from 640 ms to 24 ms while holding overall F1 score at 96.4%."*
    - **Learning:** *"We made CPU baseline cascades a prerequisite before any team requests new GPU quota."*
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Introspect your own work

<Quiz title="Audit your relationship with speed and uncertainty">
  <QuizItem
    question="1. Think of a decision your team debated in meetings for more than two weeks. Was it actually a one-way door, or a disguised two-way door?"
    hint="Could a 4-hour prototype or a feature flag have settled the debate in 2 days?"
  >
    Most internal service boundaries, caching layers, and internal schemas are two-way doors if wrapped behind a clean interface. Spotting when a team is treating a two-way door like a constitutional amendment is a core L5/L6 skill.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem
    question="2. When you are under a tight deadline, what is your personal tell: do you over-cut quality, or do you freeze trying to ship 100% of the original scope?"
    hint="Knowing your default failure mode helps you self-correct early."
  >
    The sweet spot for *Deliver Results* is **cutting surface area (fewer features/endpoints in v1) while holding quality invariants (idempotency, observability, security) at 100%**.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>

## Common red flags & how to recover

- **"We worked nights and weekends to hit the date":** Working 80-hour weeks is a weak *Deliver Results* signal because it doesn't scale and leads to burnout/bugs. Interviewers want to hear how you used **engineering judgment** (scope cuts, adapters, simpler architectures) to hit the date sustainably.
- **Treating one-way doors like two-way doors:** Never use a story where you shipped an irreversible public API change or security auth bypass without review.

  >
    | One-Way Doors (Slow down, review deeply) | Two-Way Doors (Move at 70% certainty) |
    | --- | --- |
    | External public API contracts & webhook schemas consumed by third parties | Internal microservice RPC methods behind a monorepo or versioned gateway |
    | Dropping or destructively mutating a production database column | Adding a nullable column or shadow index (`CONCURRENTLY`) |
    | Cryptographic key hierarchy & tenant data-residency boundaries | Caching TTLs, worker concurrency limits, or UI layout behind a feature flag |
  </InterviewQuestion>

  <InterviewQuestion
    question="What if I took a calculated risk (Bias for Action) and the hypothesis turned out to be wrong?"
    level="Senior"
    companies={["Amazon", "Meta"]}
  >
    That can still be an **outstanding** *Bias for Action* story, provided your reversibility mechanism worked! Show that:
    1. Waiting for 100% certainty would have cost 4 weeks.
    2. You shipped a guarded 2% canary in 2 days to get real production ground truth.
    3. The canary disproved the hypothesis within 3 hours, auto-rolled back with zero user harm, and saved the team 3.5 weeks of building the wrong thing.
  </InterviewQuestion>
</InterviewQuestions>
