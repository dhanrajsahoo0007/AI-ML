## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  **Insist on the Highest Standards** means you do not pass a defect down the line, and you fix a problem so that it
  stays fixed. In an interview it is tested against a deadline: a strong story **holds the standard and still ships**.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

Most teams have met two engineers who both say they have high standards.

The first leaves 42 comments on a pull request, most of them about names and spacing. Three days before a launch he asks for a two-week rewrite. He offers no code and no smaller option. After a few months, teammates send their reviews to someone else.

The second reads a new payment retry worker and sees that it has no <Term id="idempotent">idempotency</Term> key. If the network drops between the charge and the reply, a customer is charged twice. She does not stop at a blocking comment. She pairs with the author for two hours to add the key and a test that cuts the connection mid-request. The worker ships the same week. Then she adds a build check, so that no new worker can merge without declaring how it handles a retry.

Both engineers slowed one change down. Only the second one named a harm to a customer, helped to remove it, and left the team faster afterwards. That is the version of this principle an interviewer wants to hear.

## What the interviewer scores

In our words, the principle asks you to keep raising the quality bar, to stop defects before they reach the next person, and to fix causes so they do not return. The diagram follows one decision.

1. **The finding.** Three days before launch, a failover test shows stock oversold in 0.4 percent of reservations.
2. **Favour speed.** Ship on Monday and accept the defect. The date holds and the customer pays for it.
3. **Favour the standard.** Delay two weeks for a full rework. The defect is gone and so is the date.
4. **Split the decision.** Let speed decide the scope, and let the standard decide the things that must hold.
5. **Hold both.** Ship on Monday with a smaller scope and the defect closed.

The split in step 4 is the skill. Some things can be cut and some cannot:

| Hold the line                                                           | Open to cuts                                                          |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Correct data: money, stock, anything a customer would dispute           | Secondary filters, admin screens, export formats                      |
| Who can see whose data: access checks, tenant separation, personal data | Rare file formats on day one                                          |
| A tested rollback, and alerts on your <Term id="slo" />                 | Self-service setup, if the first five customers can be set up by hand |

A strong story names one item from the left column that was at risk, and one from the right that you gave up to protect it.

Keep the left column short. If everything is a standard, nothing can ship and the team stops listening. Three or four things that must be true of every release are enough, and each should be something a customer or an on-call engineer would notice.

## A weak answer, then the fix

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time you would not compromise on quality under pressure to ship."_

<Tabs title="One answer, twice">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"Three days before launch I noticed a small inventory drift in a failover test. The team wanted to ship, but I have very high standards, so I blocked the launch until it was fixed properly. It took two more weeks. Quality matters more than dates."*

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Situation and task.** *"Three days before we launched a stock reservation API, load tests had passed at 5,000 requests a second. Reading the failover test logs, I saw stock go negative in 0.4 percent of reservations when the cache leader changed."*
    - **Action.** *"The team was tired and said failovers are rare. I worked out the cost: at sale volume, one failover would oversell about 600 units, and each one is an order we cancel by hand. A full rework needed two weeks. I proposed a smaller change that fit before Monday. Every stock write carries a version, so a write from an old leader is rejected, and during a failover reservations use the database path, which is slower and correct. To make room, we moved bulk reservation out of the launch. I wrote the check and a failover test in 36 hours, and a teammate reviewed both."*
    - **Result.** *"We launched on Monday. Three weeks later a cache node was replaced in business hours and the oversell count was zero. Bulk reservation shipped five weeks later than first planned."*
    - **Learning.** *"The failover test now runs in the build before every release."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- The defect has a cost to customers: 600 oversold units per failover. In the weak answer it is "a small drift".
- The candidate built the fix and found a path that kept the date.
- Something was given up, and the answer says what: bulk reservation, five weeks late.
- The standard no longer depends on someone remembering it.

## Build your own

1. Name one annoyance your team treats as normal: a flaky test, a manual deploy step, a noisy alert. Write what a one-day check would do about it.
2. Think of the last time you pushed back in a review. Write the production scenario you were worried about, in one sentence, with a number.
3. Find a time you shipped with a known gap. Write what you held, what you cut, and whether you would make the same split again.
4. Find a defect that reached production on your watch. Write why the tests missed it and what catches it now.

## Red flags

- **Gold-plating.** Three weeks on a framework for a problem that happens once a year. Recover by tying the standard to a harm: who was hurt, how often and what it cost.
- **"I blocked the launch" as the ending.** Recover by saying what you built or cut so that the launch could go ahead.
- **Standards as taste.** Names, formatting, a preferred pattern. Recover by picking a defect that a customer or an on-call engineer would have felt.
- **A standard kept by memory.** "We agreed to be more careful." Recover by naming the check that now runs without you.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="How do I hold a standard when the deadline is tomorrow?">
    Split the decision the way the diagram does. Apply speed to the scope: drop a feature, launch in one region, cap the
    traffic. Apply the standard to what must hold: correct data, access checks, alerts and a rollback you have tested.
    You ship less, and what you ship is sound.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="A defect reached production on my watch. Does that rule the story out?">
    No. The principle is about defects that stay fixed. Tell it plainly: what broke, why your tests missed it, and what
    you added so that kind of defect is caught next time.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Is a refactoring story a good fit?">
    Only if you can name what the old code was costing: incidents, hours per release, bugs per month. A refactor told as
    "the code was ugly" sounds like taste.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
