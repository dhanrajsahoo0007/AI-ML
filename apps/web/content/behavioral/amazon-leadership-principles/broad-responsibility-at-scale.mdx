## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  **Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility** means that at scale a rare case happens every hour, and your system
  touches people who never chose to use it. You look for those **secondary effects** before they hurt someone, and you
  build the guard into the system.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

At a startup with 50 users, a case that happens in 0.01 percent of requests turns up about once a year. At a service that handles 100 million requests an hour, the same case happens 10,000 times an hour.

An ML engineer builds a tool that summarises customer support chats. To debug bad summaries, the design writes each raw prompt to an analytics table. At small scale this is ordinary debugging. At full scale the prompts will include card numbers, medical details and login tokens, in a table that 400 people can read. No customer agreed to that, and no customer would ever see it happen.

Launch is four days away, and nobody has asked for a change. She raises it in the design review anyway and builds the guard herself in two days. A scrubber replaces card numbers, tokens and health terms before anything is written. Read access drops from 400 people to the 12 who debug summaries. Rows are deleted after seven days.

The debugging still works. The team ships on the date.

In our words, the principle says that a large company's actions reach far beyond its customers, so its people should be humble and careful about secondary effects and leave things better than they found them.

1. **A useful feature.** The log has a good reason to exist.
2. **Scale changes the odds.** Multiply the rare case by your real traffic.
3. **Who else is affected.** Look past the user in front of you: other customers, partners, colleagues, the public.
4. **The pull to ship as it is.** The date is close, and the risk has not hurt anyone yet.
5. **Hold both.** Keep the feature and build the guard in, so it does not depend on anyone remembering.

You do not need a charity or climate story. For engineers the principle shows in ordinary production work:

| Area                            | A strong story shows                                                          | What weakens it                         |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| **Privacy and security**        | You caught personal data in a log, or one customer's data reaching another    | "Security reviews that sort of thing"   |
| **Fairness of a model**         | You measured quality per language, region or device and fixed the worst group | Only the average was measured           |
| **Accessibility**               | A key flow now works with a screen reader, a keyboard and a slow connection   | "Most users are fine"                   |
| **Partners and shared systems** | You limited what your traffic does to a smaller system when it is failing     | "Their server should have coped"        |
| **Waste**                       | You removed jobs, copies or retries that cost money and energy for nothing    | Nobody knew what the idle jobs were for |

The same story, told well:

- **Situation and task.** _"Our webhook service retried a failed delivery up to 20 times, 5 seconds apart. When a small merchant's server had a brief problem, our retries arrived as a wave of up to 50,000 requests and kept it down for hours. Nothing on our own dashboards was red."_
- **Action.** _"I went through 90 days of delivery logs and merchant support tickets. Our retries were involved in 40 percent of the outages that small merchants reported. I changed the policy to exponential backoff with random jitter and capped concurrent deliveries per destination. I added a <Term id="circuit-breaker" /> that pauses delivery to an endpoint after 60 seconds of errors and replays the events when it recovers. Our largest merchants wanted fast retries, so I made the cap a setting they can raise. I tested the change by replaying a real outage against a copy of a merchant's endpoint."_
- **Result.** _"Deliveries that succeed in the end rose from 74 to 98.6 percent. Our retry traffic fell by 62 percent, and tickets about webhook floods went from about 30 a month to 2. A late event can now arrive up to 15 minutes after it happened, and three merchants complained about that."_
- **Learning.** _"Our design review template now asks what our traffic does to the receiver when the receiver is failing."_

What changed:

- The merchant's outage is treated as something the candidate's system did.
- The harm is sized before the fix: 40 percent of reported outages.
- The fix is a design with a setting for those who need the old behavior, and the answer admits who was unhappy.
- The lesson went into a review template, so it reaches the next service.

## Build your own

1. Suppose someone sent 10,000 crafted requests to your service tonight. Write the worst thing that could happen to another customer's data or availability.
2. Check whether your product works as well on a slow phone connection, with a screen reader, or in a second language. Write down the group with the worst numbers.
3. Find something your system does to another team or a partner when that team is having a bad day. Write what limits it.
4. Find a risk you raised before anyone asked. Write what it cost to fix then, and what it would have cost later.

## Red flags

- **"That is legal's job."** Recover by telling what you found yourself, in a design or code review, and what you built.
- **A volunteering story.** A charity day says nothing about your engineering judgment. Recover with a production story about privacy, access, fairness or a partner.
- **Acting only when forced.** The fix came after the audit or the incident. Recover by choosing a story where you moved first, or say plainly what you missed and what you check now.
- **A guard that relies on memory.** "We agreed to be careful with that table." Recover by naming the check that enforces it.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="Will an engineer be asked about this principle?">
    Some prep guides say engineers are rarely asked about it by name. That is not confirmed, and Amazon says its
    principles apply to every role. One story is enough, and it will also serve for questions about ownership and
    standards.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="The harm I prevented never happened. How do I show a result?">
    Size what was exposed: how many records, customers or requests the risk covered. Then say what the guard has caught
    since. "The scrubber has redacted 40,000 card numbers in six months" is a result.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="What if raising the risk delayed the launch?">
    Say so, with the number of days and who agreed to it. Then say what you did to keep the delay short, such as
    shipping to a small group first. A delay you chose and explained is easier to defend than a risk you kept quiet.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
