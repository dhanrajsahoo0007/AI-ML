## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
**Dive Deep** means refusing to trust high-level averages when anecdotes or tail metrics disagree (peeling the onion down to raw logs, query plans, and flamegraphs) while **Are Right, A Lot** means actively hunting for disconfirming data to break your own initial hypothesis.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A checkout service's top-line Grafana dashboard is glowing bright green: **99.94% HTTP 200 OK**, average latency **48 ms**. Yet Customer Support reports three tweets from users in Brazil saying they can't complete purchases.

An engineer who doesn't *Dive Deep* glances at the green dashboard, replies *"Metrics look fine on our end, probably a client network issue,"* and closes the ticket.
An engineer who practices **Dive Deep + Are Right, A Lot** remembers Amazon's famous rule: **when the metrics and the anecdotes disagree, the anecdotes are usually right**. They slice the 200 OK metric by region and payment method, discovering that our API returns `HTTP 200 OK` with `{ "error": "PIX_TIMEOUT" }` inside the JSON body, so the top-line 5xx alert was completely blind! Within 30 minutes, they trace the timeout to an expired mTLS intermediate certificate on one regional proxy, fix the cert rotation, and replace the HTTP-status alert with a business-level checkout completion <Term id="sli" />.

## Worked engineering example (STAR+L)

<Tabs title="Dive Deep vs. Are Right, A Lot">
  <Tab label="Dive Deep (When Metrics & Anecdotes Disagree)">
    **Prompt:** *"Tell me about a time when your high-level metrics looked fine, but you dug deeper and found a critical problem."*

    - **Situation & Task:** *"After migrating our recommendation service to a new gRPC connection pool, overall <Term id="p99" /> latency improved from 95 ms to 62 ms and error rates sat at 0.01%. However, the Ads team mentioned a 1.8% dip in auction fill rate that started the same week."*
    - **Action:** *"Our dashboard looked healthy, so it was tempting to blame Ads changes. Instead, I joined our request logs with downstream auction IDs and sliced latency by payload size. I found that while 99% of users had under 50 history items, our top 0.5% power users (who generated 19% of ad revenue!) had 2,000+ items. For that 0.5% cohort, protobuf deserialization was triggering Go GC pauses that pushed p99.9 past the 80 ms auction deadline: silently dropping them to empty fallback ads without logging a 5xx error."*
    - **Result:** *"I capped history payloads to the 150 most recent high-signal events and pooled byte buffers, cutting p99.9 from 140 ms to 38 ms and recovering the full 1.8% auction fill rate (~\$1.4M/yr annualized)."*
    - **Learning:** *"I replaced our flat request-count SLO with a **revenue-weighted and cohort-sliced SLO** so whale-cohort regressions page on-call immediately."*
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Are Right, A Lot (Disconfirming Your Own Belief)">
    **Prompt:** *"Tell me about a time you strongly believed in a technical direction, sought out disconfirming evidence, and changed your mind."*

    - **Situation & Task:** *"When our Postgres events table hit 4 TB and write latency spiked, I was convinced we needed to migrate to Apache Cassandra, and I drafted an RFC for a 6-week migration."*
    - **Action:** *"Before presenting the RFC for approval, I asked our sharpest DBA (who was skeptical of introducing Cassandra operational toil) to help me design a benchmark that could **disprove** my migration plan. Profiling `pg_stat_io` together, we discovered 82% of our write IOPS wasn't new event inserts at all: it was B-tree index page splits on four unused secondary indexes plus synchronous `fsync` on non-critical telemetry rows."*
    - **Result:** *"I killed my own Cassandra RFC. Dropping the 4 unused indexes, partitioning by week, and setting `synchronous_commit = off` for telemetry transactions cut write IOPS by 76% and p99 write latency from 45 ms to 4 ms in 3 days, saving 6 weeks of migration work and \$90k/yr in new cluster costs."*
    - **Learning:** *"I added an index-usage audit query to our monthly operational review before any team is allowed to propose a new datastore."*
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Common red flags & how to recover

- **Stopping at "We restarted the server and it went away":** Restarting pods without capturing a heap dump, thread dump, or core trace destroys the evidence and guarantees the outage will return at a worse time.
- **Confusing *Are Right, A Lot* with stubbornness:** Claiming you are always right in every argument makes you look low-trust (*Earn Trust* failure). Showing how you actively try to falsify your own ideas proves true scientific rigor.

<InterviewQuestions>
  <InterviewQuestion
    question="Walk me through the hardest bug you ever diagnosed in production. How did you isolate the root cause?"
    level="Senior"
    companies={["Amazon", "Google", "Datadog"]}
  >
    Structure a debugging story like a **binary search over the system state**:
    1. **The symptom & blast radius:** Why it was non-obvious (e.g., intermittent 0.3% corrupted payloads only on Tuesday mornings).
    2. **Eliminating half the search space:** How you proved whether the corruption happened at the client, load balancer, app worker, or storage engine.
    3. **The smoking gun:** The exact packet capture, memory profile, or race-condition trace that reproduced the bug deterministically.
    4. **The systemic prevention:** The test or invariant check that prevents the entire bug class.
  </InterviewQuestion>

  <InterviewQuestion
    question="Tell me about a time you had to make a judgment call when two metrics pointed in opposite directions."
    level="Senior"
    companies={["Amazon", "Meta", "Netflix"]}
  >
    Classic example: an ML ranking or UX change increases short-term click-through rate (+3%) while slightly increasing latency (+60 ms) or session abandonment. Show how you dug into long-term cohort retention or downstream conversion to establish a **hierarchy of metrics** rather than picking whichever metric made your launch look good.
  </InterviewQuestion>
</InterviewQuestions>
