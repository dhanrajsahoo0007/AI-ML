## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  **Dive Deep** means that when a summary number and a customer's report disagree, you go down to the records until you
  can explain both. **Are Right, A Lot** means your judgment is good because you look for other views and try to prove
  your own belief wrong before you act on it.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A checkout team's dashboard is green: 99.94 percent of requests succeed, and the average response takes 48 ms. Support forwards three messages from customers in Brazil who cannot complete a purchase.

One engineer looks at the dashboard, replies that the service is healthy and that it is probably the customers' network, and closes the ticket.

A second engineer has the same first belief. She writes down what would be true if it were right: failures would be spread across every payment method. Then she checks. She splits the success number by region and payment method, and one slice stands out. Instant bank payments in Brazil fail 31 percent of the time. She reads a handful of raw responses. The API returns "200 OK" with an error code inside the body, so the error alert never saw them.

Thirty minutes later she has the cause: an expired certificate on one regional proxy. She renews it, then adds an alert that reads the error code in the body.

She went down two layers, and she dropped her own first explanation on the way.

1. **The summary and the report disagree.** A green dashboard and three customers who cannot pay. Both are facts, and a good explanation covers both.
2. **Dive Deep.** Split the number, then read the records behind the slice that looks wrong.
3. **Are Right, A Lot.** Say what your first belief predicts, and check whether that is what you see.
4. **Where they pull apart.** Each layer costs time, and a decision is waiting. Depth with no stopping point is its own failure.
5. **A strong answer holds both.** Go deep enough to explain both facts, then act.

### Dive Deep

In our words: stay close to the details at every level of seniority, check often, and be suspicious when the numbers and the stories from customers do not match.

| A strong story shows                                               | What weakens it                              |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| The exact steps: what you split, what you read, what you ruled out | "I looked into the logs"                     |
| You did the digging yourself                                       | "I asked someone on the team to investigate" |
| A cause you can name as a mechanism                                | "Some kind of memory issue"                  |
| A fix for the cause, and a way to see it next time                 | "We restarted it and it went away"           |

### Are Right, A Lot

In our words: have strong judgment, seek out people who see it differently, and work to disprove what you believe.

| A strong story shows                                                     | What weakens it                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| A test that could have shown you were wrong, chosen before you committed | "I had a gut feeling"                              |
| You went to the person who disagreed most                                | You asked only the people who agreed               |
| You changed your mind when the evidence did, and said so                 | You were right in every story you tell             |
| You stopped digging when one more layer could not change the decision    | Three weeks of analysis on a choice you could undo |

The same story with the depth in it:

- **Situation and task.** _"We moved our recommendation service to a new connection pool. <Term id="p99" /> improved from 95 ms to 62 ms and errors stayed at 0.01 percent. The same week, the ads team saw their fill rate drop by 1.8 percent."_
- **Action.** _"My dashboard gave me no reason to look, and their report did. I joined our request logs to their auction ids and split latency by the size of each user's history. 99 percent of users have under 50 items. The top 0.5 percent have over 2,000, and they bring in 19 percent of ad revenue. For them, decoding the history caused garbage collection pauses that pushed the slowest requests past the 80 ms auction deadline, so they were served an empty fallback. I confirmed it by replaying 500 of those requests in staging with pause logging switched on."_
- **Result.** _"I capped the history at the 150 most recent events and reused the buffers. The slowest 0.1 percent of requests went from 140 ms to 38 ms, and the fill rate came back within two days."_
- **Learning.** _"We now track latency for our heaviest users as its own target, so a regression for them pages us."_

### Are Right, A Lot

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time you changed your mind about a technical direction."_

<Tabs title="Are Right, A Lot">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"Our events table reached 4 TB and writes got slow. I knew from experience that we needed a different datastore, so I wrote the proposal and pushed it through. Some people doubted it, but I was confident, and I am usually right about these things."*

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Situation and task.** *"Our Postgres events table reached 4 TB and write latency rose. I believed we had to move to a different datastore, and I had drafted a six-week migration proposal."*
    - **Action.** *"Before I presented it, I went to the database engineer who doubted the plan most and asked her to help me design a test that could prove me wrong. We measured where the write load came from. 82 percent of it was not new events. It was upkeep on four indexes that no query used, plus a disk flush on every low-value telemetry row."*
    - **Result.** *"I withdrew my own proposal. We dropped the four indexes, partitioned the table by week and relaxed the flush for telemetry. p99 write latency went from 45 ms to 4 ms in three days, and we did not spend the six weeks. I had also expected our slow monthly report to speed up. It did not, and that is still open."*
    - **Learning.** *"We run an index usage report before anyone proposes a new datastore."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- The weak answer treats confidence as evidence. The fixed one names a test that could have gone against the candidate.
- The candidate went to the person who disagreed.
- It admits one prediction that was wrong.

## Build your own

1. Think of the last bug where your first explanation was wrong. Write how long you held on to it, and what finally changed your mind.
2. Name one number your team reports every week that could stay green while users have a bad time. Write how you would check it.
3. Find a decision where you asked someone who disagreed with you. Write what they told you and what you did with it.
4. Find an investigation you stopped. Write why one more day of digging would not have changed what you did.

## Red flags

- **"We restarted it and it went away."** Recover by saying what you captured before the restart, or what you added so the next occurrence leaves evidence.
- **Someone else did the digging.** Recover by choosing a story where you ran the queries. If you led the investigation, say which step you did yourself.
- **Always right.** A set of stories with no error in them reads as stubborn. Recover with one story where you changed your mind and say what changed it.
- **Digging without end.** Three weeks of analysis on a decision you could reverse in a day. Recover by saying where you stopped and why that was enough.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="How technical should the detail be?">
    Name the tool and the finding in one sentence each: "I split latency by payload size and the top 0.5 percent of
    users were ten times slower." The interviewer may not know your stack, so say what each step told you. Keep the
    deeper detail ready for follow-up questions.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Can I use a story where I was wrong?">
    Yes. Are Right, A Lot is about judgment over many decisions, and that includes noticing early when one is wrong. A
    story where you tested your own plan and dropped it is one of the clearest ways to show it.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="I am a manager. Do I still need a Dive Deep story?">
    Yes. Amazon's text for this principle says leaders work at every level of detail and that no task is beneath them.
    Use a story where you read the data yourself: an incident timeline, a cost report or a hiring funnel.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
