## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  **Customer Obsession** means you start from what the customer is living with and work backwards to the fix.
  **Ownership** means you act for the whole company and the long term, so a problem that sits between two teams is still
  yours to move.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

An API platform team ships a new pagination format. It cuts their database CPU by 25 percent, and the team meets its quarterly goal.

Two days later, a backend engineer on the team reads the gateway logs and sees something odd. About 3 percent of enterprise clients are requesting the same page again and again. The new format writes an empty cursor differently, and an older SDK reads it as "more pages to come". Those clients use up their rate limits, and their nightly data syncs fail without an error.

The SDK belongs to another team in another time zone. The release notes mentioned the cursor change in a footnote. An easy reply is available: the SDK team owns parsing, and the change was documented.

She does two things. Within 40 minutes she ships a 12-line server change that returns the old cursor shape to old SDK versions, and the failed syncs stop. Then she opens a pull request on the SDK repository with a fix and a contract test, and agrees a date 60 days out to remove the server patch.

The first move is Customer Obsession. The second is Ownership.

Amazon publishes 16 Leadership Principles and says each interviewer is assigned competencies based on them (see Sources). These two often appear in one story, and they can pull against each other.

1. **Customer Obsession** asks what the customer is living with today. In the story, 3 percent of clients were losing a nightly sync.
2. **Ownership** asks who will fix the cause and what the system looks like in a year. The cause sat in another team's SDK.
3. **Where they pull apart.** The fastest relief for the customer is a patch on your side. A patch with no end date becomes permanent, and that is a long-term cost you chose for the company.
4. **A strong answer holds both.** Relief today, the real fix in the right place, and a date to remove the patch.

### Customer Obsession

In our words: start with the customer and work backwards, and earn their trust by acting on what they experience, including problems they have not reported. The customer can be an end user, a merchant, or another engineering team that calls your API.

| A strong story shows                                                                   | What weakens it                                   |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| You found the pain yourself: in logs, in support tickets or by watching someone work   | The customer is "whatever was in the ticket"      |
| A number for the customer's cost: failed syncs, seconds of waiting, support contacts   | Only your own metric: CPU, build time, tickets    |
| You gave something up for the customer: time, scope or an internal shortcut            | The right thing for the customer was also easiest |
| You said no to one loud request to protect many users, and offered something in return | Saying yes to every request                       |

### Ownership

In our words: think like an owner of the whole company. Look further ahead than this quarter, act outside your own team when the problem is there, and never answer with "that is not my job".

| A strong story shows                                                                   | What weakens it                                         |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| You followed the problem past your team's boundary and brought the owners a tested fix | You stopped at "their service returned an error"        |
| You chose the option that would still work in a year, and you can say what it cost     | A quick fix that met this quarter's goal and broke next |
| You stayed until the result held: a check, an alert, a removal date                    | You shipped and moved on                                |
| The owning team reviewed and agreed to your change                                     | You changed another team's service without them         |

## A weak answer, then the fix

### Customer Obsession

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time you went further than expected for a customer."_

<Tabs title="Customer Obsession">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"Product engineers were unhappy with the feature flag SDK that my team owns. We got a ticket asking for faster tests, so we put it on the plan for the next quarter. We built a local mode and people liked it. Our customers are really important to us."*

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Situation and task.** *"I own the feature flag SDK that 40 product services use. Engineers told us their local integration tests took 45 seconds to start, because the SDK fetched flag rules over the network. A fix was not on our plan for the quarter."*
    - **Action.** *"I sat with three product engineers while they ran their tests. All three had replaced our SDK with hand-written fakes, and two production incidents that year came from fakes that had gone out of date. Over two days I built a local mode that reads flag rules from a file in the repository and checks their types, with no network call. I wrote a script that removed the fakes, and I ran it for the first five teams myself."*
    - **Result.** *"34 of the 40 services moved within three weeks. Test start time fell from 45 seconds to under 1 second. Six services stayed on the old path because they test against live flags, and I left them there."*
    - **Learning.** *"Our SDK design template now has a section on how the SDK behaves in a local test."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- The pain came from watching customers work. The weak answer waits for a ticket.
- The customer's cost has numbers: 45 seconds and two incidents.
- The result says who was not helped: six services.

### Ownership

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time you solved a problem that was outside your area."_

<Tabs title="Ownership">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"Our search service was missing its latency target every morning. I looked at our logs and the delay was in a call to the identity team's service. I filed a ticket with them and marked it urgent. They were busy with an audit, so it took six weeks. It was frustrating, but it was their service."*

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Situation and task.** *"Our search service missed its 120 ms <Term id="p99" /> target every day at 9 am. Our logs showed the time went on a call to the entitlements service, owned by the identity team. Their plan was frozen for a compliance audit."*
    - **Action.** *"I asked their on-call engineer if I could profile the call in their staging environment. A batch job at 9 am was using up the connection pool that live requests share. I wrote a 60-line change that gives batch reads their own pool, added a load test that showed p99 under 12 ms during a batch run, and walked their on-call engineer through it. They reviewed and merged it that afternoon."*
    - **Result.** *"Our 9 am p99 fell from 380 ms to 78 ms. The audit was not delayed."*
    - **Learning.** *"I also added a 40 ms timeout with a cached fallback on our side, so a slow dependency now degrades search and does not stop it."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- The weak answer ends at the team boundary. The fixed one crosses it, with the owning team's consent.
- The fix went where the cause was, and the owners reviewed it.
- The learning protects the candidate's own service the next time a dependency slows down.

## Build your own

Use these prompts to find a story in your own work.

1. Think of the last time you thought "that is another team's bug". Write what you did next, and what one step further would have looked like.
2. Name the person at the end of your system's request path. Write one way your system fails them without raising an alert: a retry, an abandoned upload, a support ticket.
3. Find one decision where you chose the slower option for the sake of next year. Write what it cost this quarter.
4. Find one request you turned down to protect other users. Write what you offered in its place.

## Red flags

- **The lone hero.** "Nobody else cared, so I worked 80 hours alone." It reads as poor teamwork and a single point of failure. Recover by saying what you left behind for the team: a runbook, a tool or a test.
- **Yes to everything.** Treating every request as Customer Obsession. Recover with a story where you said no to one loud request and offered something that met the real need.
- **Stopping at the boundary.** "Their service returned an error" as the end of the story. Recover by adding what you brought the owning team: a reproduction, a failing test or a draft fix.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="I work on an internal platform. Who is my customer?">
    The engineers who call your API or use your tool. Measure their cost the way you would for a paying user: minutes of
    waiting, failed builds, hours of manual work. Say who the customer is in your first sentence so the interviewer does
    not have to guess.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Can one story show both principles?">
    Yes, and the story on this page does. Amazon's interview pages give no rule about using a story twice. Prepare more
    than one all the same, so you can pick the story that fits the question you are asked.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="What if the other team rejected my fix?">
    It is still a usable story. Say what you did next: for example, you kept a guard on your own side, agreed an owner
    and a date for their fix, and checked on that date. A partial result told honestly is stronger than a story that
    stops at the ticket.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
