## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  **Invent and Simplify** means you look for a new way to do the job that leaves **fewer parts** than before. **Think
  Big** means you propose a direction larger than the request, and tie it to a first step small enough to ship.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

When a team gets a new requirement, the usual move is to add something: another service, another queue, another cache. Two years on, one click passes through 19 services, nobody can run the system on a laptop, and half of on-call time goes on mismatched data formats between them.

A senior engineer on the gateway team raises one thing in a design review. Three of those services pass JSON to each other, and all three read the same 40 MB configuration dataset, which changes once an hour. She asks why that needs three services.

She replaces the three services and their cache cluster with one snapshot file. A job writes it to object storage each hour, and the gateway loads it into memory. 14,000 lines of code are deleted. <Term id="p99" /> for that path falls from 45 ms to 3 ms, and pages for it go from six a month to none.

Then she looks sideways. Four other teams hold hourly reference data in the same heavy way. She writes up the snapshot as a shared pattern and helps one of those teams adopt it first.

1. **Invent and Simplify.** The invention is measured by what is gone afterwards: three services became one file.
2. **Think Big.** One deletion tool for eight teams, where the request was a workflow for one.
3. **Where they pull apart.** A big idea adds parts that someone must run. A simple design removes them.
4. **A strong answer holds both.** A large direction, and a first step that is small, simple and useful alone.

### Invent and Simplify

In our words: expect new ideas from yourself and your team, take good ideas from anywhere, keep looking for the simpler way, and accept that a new idea may be misunderstood for a while.

| A strong story shows                                                     | What weakens it                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| The system ended with fewer parts, or callers got a simpler interface    | A new framework, queue or cluster for a small problem   |
| You questioned a requirement and showed with data that it was not needed | Every requirement treated as fixed                      |
| You used an existing tool where one fitted, and said why where none did  | Your own database or message broker, built for interest |
| People doubted it, and you answered with a small proof on real data      | Nobody disagreed, so nothing was at stake               |

### Think Big

In our words: thinking small limits what you can achieve. Set a bold direction that gets people moving, and look for ways to serve customers that nobody has asked for yet.

| A strong story shows                                              | What weakens it                         |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| You saw the same problem in several teams and proposed one answer | You built your team's copy and moved on |
| A first milestone that shipped in weeks and was useful alone      | A vision document with no first step    |
| A number for the size of the gain: weeks saved, teams served      | "It would be transformative"            |
| You say who did not adopt it, and why                             | Every team adopted it                   |

## A weak answer, then the fix

### Invent and Simplify

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time you found a simple solution to a complex problem."_

<Tabs title="Invent and Simplify">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"Our fraud counters ran on a big streaming cluster that kept breaking. I redesigned it with a more modern architecture, using a new stream processor and a service mesh, and it was a lot cleaner. The team was impressed with the design."*

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Situation and task.** *"Our pipeline computed 15-minute fraud counters per merchant at 3,000 events a second. It ran on a 12-node streaming cluster with a separate store for removing duplicates. It cost \$11,000 a month and paged us twice a week when its saved state was corrupted."*
    - **Action.** *"In an incident review I asked why a 15-minute fraud signal needed exact duplicate removal. Nobody knew. It had been copied from the billing pipeline. I replayed 30 days of labelled fraud and showed that approximate counts in one-minute windows flagged 99.8 percent of the same merchants. In five days I wrote a 400-line consumer with no saved state, on tables we already ran. Two teammates were worried about losing exactness, so I ran both pipelines side by side for two weeks and posted the differences every day."*
    - **Result.** *"We turned the cluster off. Cost went from \$11,000 to \$650 a month, lag from 18 seconds to under 1 second, and we had no pages from it in nine months. About 11 merchants a month are flagged a few minutes later than before, which the fraud team accepted."*
    - **Learning.** *"Before I add a system that keeps state, I ask which requirement forces it, and I test that requirement on real data."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- The weak answer adds parts and calls it cleaner. The fixed one removes a cluster.
- A requirement was questioned and tested on 30 days of data.
- The doubters got a side-by-side run, and the answer states what got slightly worse.

### Think Big

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time you proposed something larger than what you were asked for."_

<Tabs title="Think Big">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    *"We were asked to build a data deletion workflow for the orders service. I told my manager we should really build a company-wide privacy platform that would handle every regulation for every team. I wrote a long vision document. It was not funded. I still think it was the right idea."*

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    - **Situation and task.** *"My team was asked to build a customer data deletion workflow for the orders service in six weeks, to meet a privacy regulation. While scoping it I found seven other teams with the same task on their plans, each about to build its own."*
    - **Action.** *"I wrote a four-page proposal for one deletion service. A team registers a webhook and a short file that says what to delete and when, and the service handles retries, the audit log and deadlines. To protect our own date, I built it with orders as the first user and nothing in it that orders did not need. In week five I showed it working to the seven team leads."*
    - **Result.** *"Orders met its date. Five of the seven teams joined, at about three days each in place of six weeks. Two teams with unusual storage built their own, so the saving was about 28 engineer-weeks against the 40 I had forecast."*
    - **Learning.** *"I fund the first step of a large idea from a deliverable my team already owes."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

What changed:

- The weak answer has a vision and no first step. The fixed one ships the first step inside an existing deadline.
- The size of the gain is a number, and it is lower than the forecast.
- Two teams said no, and the answer says so.

## Build your own

1. Suppose you had to remove 30 percent of the services, queues or tables in your system tomorrow. Write which ones you would merge, and what stops you today.
2. Look at the last feature you built. Write down which other teams are solving nearly the same problem.
3. Find a requirement you once questioned. Write how you tested whether it was needed.
4. Find an idea of yours that people doubted. Write what their worry was and what you showed them.

## Red flags

- **Design for the CV.** A container platform and a service mesh for an internal tool with 20 requests a second. Recover by starting from the problem's size and choosing the smallest thing that fits.
- **Not invented here.** Your own message broker where a managed one fitted. Recover by naming the specific limit that ruled out the standard option.
- **A vision with no first step.** Recover by adding the milestone that shipped, or by choosing another story.
- **Complexity moved to the callers.** Your service got simpler and every caller got harder. Recover by describing the change from the caller's side.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="People doubted my idea at first. Is that a bad sign for the story?">
    No. Amazon's text for Invent and Simplify says a new idea may be misunderstood for a long time. Say why the doubt
    was reasonable, what small proof you built on real data, and who changed their mind.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="I am junior. What does Think Big look like for me?">
    It looks like seeing one step past your task. You noticed that a second team had the same problem, or you built your
    part so that it would not need a rewrite next quarter. Say what you proposed and what happened to it, even if the
    answer is "not yet".
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="My big proposal was not funded. Can I still use it?">
    Only if something shipped. A first step that worked for one team is a result. A document that nobody acted on is a
    story about persuasion that failed, and it needs a clear lesson to be worth telling.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
