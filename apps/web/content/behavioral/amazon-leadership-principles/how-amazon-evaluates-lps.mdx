## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Amazon has no separate behavioral round. **Every interviewer** in the loop is assigned competencies based on the 16
  Leadership Principles and asks about your past work, and the hiring manager and a **Bar Raiser** from another team
  make the decision together.
</KeyIdea>

## The loop

What Amazon says about its own process (see Sources):

- **Stages.** An online application, assessments, phone screening, then the interview loop. The details differ by role.
- **Phone screen.** One or two calls of 30 to 45 minutes with a recruiter or the hiring manager. For SDE III, SDM and applied scientist roles, Amazon describes a 60-minute technical phone screen with a senior leader: half on Leadership Principles, half technical.
- **The loop.** You meet two to seven people for 45 to 60 minutes each: managers, future teammates, stakeholders and a Bar Raiser from a different team.
- **Behavioral questions in every interview.** Each interviewer is assigned different competencies based on the principles and typically asks two or three behavioral questions.
- **The result.** Amazon says you hear within two business days of a phone screen and within five business days of the loop.

| Role              | Before the loop                                                                                                                      | The loop                      |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- |
| SDE II            | Online assessment: two coding questions in 90 minutes, about 20 minutes of system design scenarios and an 8-minute work style survey | Four interviews of 55 minutes |
| SDE III           | A 60-minute technical phone screen                                                                                                   | Five interviews of 55 minutes |
| SDM               | A 60-minute phone screen, and a writing assessment sent about two days before the loop                                               | Five interviews of 55 minutes |
| Applied scientist | A 60-minute technical phone screen                                                                                                   | Four interviews of 55 minutes |

Amazon does not publish how many principles each interviewer covers, or how many minutes of an interview they take. Candidates commonly report 15 to 30 minutes of behavioral questions inside each technical interview, and a Bar Raiser interview that is mostly behavioral. Amazon confirms neither.

## What they score

A principle is easy to show alone. The harder test is a story where two principles pull against each other: you moved fast and still had to protect quality, or you disagreed and still had to support the decision. Prepare each story with both sides in it.

The table below is our reading of what makes an answer easy or hard to score against a principle.

| What is listened for    | A strong answer                                                  | A weak answer                                |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Your own part**       | "I" with a decision and the reason for it                        | "We" in every sentence                       |
| **Evidence**            | The number before and after                                      | "It went well"                               |
| **The other principle** | You say what you protected while you moved fast, or what it cost | One principle, told as if nothing was traded |
| **What lasted**         | A check, an alarm or a rule that works without you               | "We were more careful afterwards"            |
| **Mistakes**            | The mistake stated first, then what you changed                  | Only successes                               |

Amazon's role pages recommend the STAR structure. Its interview loop page calls STAR one way to structure an answer and says it is not the only way.

## How the decision is made

Amazon describes the decision in its article on the Bar Raiser programme:

1. After the interviews, each interviewer shares feedback with the Bar Raiser and the hiring manager.
2. The Bar Raiser runs the debrief. Their job there is to keep the discussion on evidence, to stop the most senior voice from dominating, and to move the group towards agreement.
3. The **hiring manager and the Bar Raiser make the final decision together**.

**Who the Bar Raiser is.** A volunteer from another team, in any job family. Amazon says there are more than 3,600 of them. They train for three months to a year by shadowing. They can help design the loop and choose which principles it covers.

**The bar.** Amazon states it as growth potential and skills better than 50 percent of people in similar roles. It also says candidates are not expected to score highly on every principle.

**What Amazon does not publish.** Its pages do not mention a veto. Former interviewers and prep guides often say the Bar Raiser can block a hire, so treat that as unconfirmed. Amazon also does not publish a voting scale or the number of votes an offer needs.

## The 16 principles in a story

Amazon says all 16 principles apply to every role. The pages in this section cover them in pairs. The right-hand column is our summary.

| Principles                                                    | Where they show in a story                                                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Customer Obsession, Ownership                                 | You found a customer's pain yourself and followed the cause past your team's boundary |
| Bias for Action, Deliver Results                              | You decided with part of the data, limited the risk and finished                      |
| Insist on the Highest Standards                               | You held a quality line and still shipped                                             |
| Dive Deep, Are Right, A Lot                                   | You checked the detail behind a summary, and changed your mind when the evidence did  |
| Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit, Earn Trust                | You disagreed openly with evidence, then backed the decision fully                    |
| Invent and Simplify, Think Big                                | You removed complexity, or proposed a direction larger than the task                  |
| Frugality, Learn and Be Curious                               | You did more with less, or learned something outside your area and used it            |
| Hire and Develop the Best, Strive to be Earth's Best Employer | You raised someone else's level and made the team a better place to work              |
| Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility                  | You weighed the effect of your work on people beyond the customer in front of you     |

## Worked answers

<Tabs title="Two answers that fit an Amazon loop">
  <Tab label="Speed with a guard">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time you took a calculated risk to move fast."*

    - **Situation and task.** *"Two weeks before our peak-season code freeze, the address lookup in checkout was timing out for 6 percent of rural postcodes. We estimated about \$18,000 a day in abandoned carts. A full index rebuild would take four weeks."*
    - **Action.** *"I asked first whether the fix could be undone. A change to the main index could not, so I left the index alone. In 48 hours I built a read-only fallback that held the 50,000 most used rural postcodes. I put it behind a <Term id="circuit-breaker" /> that used the fallback only when the main lookup took more than 150 ms, and I logged every case where the two disagreed."*
    - **Result.** *"We shipped in three days. Timeouts fell from 6 percent to 0.08 percent. The log showed 212 addresses where the fallback and the index disagreed, and we corrected them before a label was printed."*
    - **Learning.** *"I turned the breaker and the fallback into a small library. Four other checkout dependencies use it now."*

    **Why it fits:** it shows Bias for Action, and it says what protected Insist on the Highest Standards.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="A mistake, and what lasted">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time you made a mistake."*

    - **Headline.** *"I shipped an Android release that crashed on start for 2 percent of users. It is why our releases now go out in stages."*
    - **Situation and task.** *"I was the release owner for our Android app, which has about 400,000 daily users. I had merged a change to how we read saved settings."*
    - **Action.** *"Crash reports rose within 20 minutes. I paused the rollout at 30 percent and told the team channel what I had done and what I did not know yet. I reproduced the crash on a device with settings saved by a two-year-old version, wrote the fix and a migration test, and shipped a patch the next morning."*
    - **Result.** *"About 8,000 users saw the crash for up to 18 hours, and I could not undo that. The patch brought the crash rate back to its usual 0.1 percent."*
    - **Learning.** *"Every release now starts at 1 percent for a day, and the build loads settings saved by each of our last ten versions."*

    **Why it fits:** it shows Ownership and Earn Trust. The mistake comes first, the cost is not hidden, and the ending is a check that works without the candidate.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Red flags

- **Saying the principle in place of showing it.** "I am very customer obsessed" earns nothing. Recover by giving the action and the number.
- **A one-sided story.** Speed with no guard, or quality with no deadline. Recover by adding the sentence about what you protected, or what it cost.
- **"We tried harder" as the ending.** Recover by naming the check you added.
- **One flagship story for every question.** Interviewers share their feedback, so a repeated story gives the panel less evidence. Recover by noting which story you told to whom, and choose a different project when one fits.

## Common questions about this loop

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="How can I tell which interviewer is the Bar Raiser?">
    Amazon says the Bar Raiser comes from a different team from the one you are applying to. You may not be told who it
    is. Every interviewer's feedback is read in the debrief, so prepare for each interview in the same way.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Are there behavioral questions in the coding and design interviews?">
    Yes. Amazon says each interviewer is assigned competencies based on the principles and typically asks two or three
    behavioral questions. Candidates commonly report 15 to 30 minutes of them in a technical interview, which Amazon
    does not confirm.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Can I use the same story twice?">
    Amazon publishes no rule about it. You will hear fixed limits from other candidates, and none of them is confirmed.
    A safe plan is to prepare several projects, and to repeat one only when it is clearly the best fit for the question.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
