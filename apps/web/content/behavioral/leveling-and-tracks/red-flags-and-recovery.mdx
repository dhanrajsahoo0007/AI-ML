## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Every candidate has an answer go wrong: too much background, a sentence that blames someone, a blank. You recover in
  three moves: **notice it, name it in one calm sentence, and restart from the first thing you did**.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A backend engineer is asked about a difficult migration. He starts with the history: the service was written in 2018, the original team left, the documentation was thin, there had been two failed attempts before his. He glances at the clock on his screen. Ninety seconds have passed and he has not yet said anything he did. The interviewer has stopped typing.

He has two choices. He can keep going and squeeze his own work into the last twenty seconds. Or he can stop.

He stops. "Let me get to what I did. The problem was 180 million rows to move with no downtime. I did three things." He lists them: a dual-write period, a checker that compared old and new rows every hour, and a cutover rehearsed twice in staging. He gives the result, one thing he would change, and stops talking at about three minutes.

The interviewer's notes for that question contain the three actions and the result. The ninety seconds of history are not in them, because there was nothing in the history to write down.

Walk through the steps:

1. **The drift.** The answer has gone wrong and you are still talking.
2. **Notice.** Use a sign you can check while speaking. The simplest is whether you have said "I" followed by a verb.
3. **Name it.** One sentence that tells the interviewer you are changing course. No apology and no explanation.
4. **Restart from the action.** The first thing you did, and why.
5. **Land it.** The result, what you learned, and then stop.

Six things go wrong most often. Each has a sentence that gets you out.

| What went wrong                 | How it sounds                                              | Say this                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Too much background**         | Past a minute, and still on history                        | "Let me get to what I did. The problem was X, and I did three things."                                                    |
| **Blame**                       | "The product manager kept changing everything"             | "Let me put that more fairly. She was under pressure to hit the launch. My part was..."                                   |
| **"We" for everything**         | "We designed, we migrated, we decided"                     | "To be specific about my part: the team did the rollout, and I wrote the design and built the checker."                   |
| **A blank**                     | Silence, or a rushed start on the first story you think of | "Let me take a few seconds to pick the right example."                                                                    |
| **The wrong story**             | Thirty seconds in, you see it does not fit the question    | "This one is not a good fit for your question. I have a better example. May I switch?"                                    |
| **Defensive under a follow-up** | Arguing when asked "why did you not use X?"                | "That is a fair challenge. X would have been simpler. Here is the constraint that ruled it out, and what I would do now." |

Meta's interview guide says its interviewers will steer a general answer back to a concrete example, and that talking about failed projects or conflicts will not count against you (see Sources). A redirection from the interviewer is help. Take it.

The same answer, recovered after the second sentence:

- **The slip.** "Honestly the product manager had done no user research and changed the designs every Tuesday..."
- **Name it.** "...let me stop and say my own part in that."
- **Restart from the action.** "The designs were changing weekly, and I treated each change as a new ticket. I never asked to see the sign-up data. In week three I could have proposed putting the new flow behind a flag for 5 percent of users. I did not. We rebuilt the screens four times and launched in March."
- **Land it.** "Sign-up completion fell from 61 to 58 percent. We rolled back within a week. On my next project I asked for the funnel numbers on day one and we tested the first screen on 5 percent of users before building the rest."

**What changed:** the blame stops mid-sentence, the candidate's own decisions appear, the bad number stays, and there is an ending.

## Two more recoveries

<Tabs title="Recoveries in full">
  <Tab label="No exact story for the question">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time you had to let someone go."* The candidate is a tech lead with no formal reports.

    - **Left alone:** *"I have never been a manager, so I do not have an example."*
    - **Recovered:** *"I have not let anyone go. That decision sits with my manager. The closest I have is a contractor on my project who missed three deadlines. I gave him the feedback directly, set out what I needed in two weeks, and when it did not change I recommended to my manager that we end the contract. May I walk you through that?"*

    **Why it works:** it is honest about the gap, offers the nearest real case, and asks before spending three minutes on it.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="A data scientist loses the thread">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time you explained something technical to a non-technical audience."*

    - **The slip:** *"...so the model uses gradient boosting with about 400 trees, and the interesting part is how we handled the categorical features, which, sorry, where was I..."*
    - **Recovered:** *"Let me go back to your question. The audience was our finance team. I had lost them in the first meeting with a slide of model details, which is what I just did to you. In the second meeting I showed three customers the model got right and one it got wrong, and what each mistake would cost. They approved the pilot that week."*

    **Why it works:** the candidate uses the slip as the example. It is a small moment and it shows more self-awareness than a polished answer would.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Build your own

1. **Record one answer on your phone.** Note the second at which you first say "I" followed by something you did.
2. **Find your blame sentence.** In your failure and conflict stories, underline any sentence about what someone else did wrong. Write your own part next to it.
3. **Write your three lines.** One for too much background, one for the wrong story, one for a challenge. Use your own words, and say them aloud ten times.
4. **Pick your closest stories.** For three questions you have no story for, write down the nearest one you do have.

## Red flags

These are the ways a recovery itself goes wrong.

- **Apologising at length.** "I am so sorry, I am terrible at this" makes the interviewer look after you. Say the one-line pivot and continue.
- **Switching stories twice.** One switch is fine. A second one suggests you have not prepared. Finish the second story whatever happens.
- **Explaining the slip.** "I got nervous because..." uses time. The interviewer does not need the reason.
- **Trailing off.** "So, yeah, that is it." End with the result and one learning, then offer: "I can go deeper on the rollout if useful."

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="Is it acceptable to ask for time to think?">
    Yes. A few seconds of silence followed by a structured answer is better than an instant start on the wrong story.
    Say that you are thinking, so the silence has a label.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Can I keep notes beside me in a video interview?">
    Practice differs between companies, so ask your recruiter if you are unsure. If you do, keep a few words for each
    story and its two key numbers. Reading full sentences from a page sounds flat and is easy to spot.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="I gave a weak answer in one round. Can I make up for it in the next?">
    You cannot change the earlier feedback. Where interviewers compare notes after the loop, as a first-hand account
    describes at Meta, a strong story on the same theme in a later round gives them a second data point. Do not force it
    into a question it does not fit.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
