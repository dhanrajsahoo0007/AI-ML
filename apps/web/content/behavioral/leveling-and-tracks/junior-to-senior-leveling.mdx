## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Junior, mid-level and senior candidates get the same behavioral questions. The level you are graded at comes from four
  things in your story: **who found the problem**, **how unclear it was**, **who you had to persuade**, and **what you
  left behind for other people**.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

An engineer with seven years of experience interviews for a senior role. Asked about a hard project, she says: "My manager asked me to move our payment webhooks onto a queue because they timed out in sales. I built the producer and the consumer, wrote the tests and shipped it in three sprints. We had no timeouts on the big sale day."

Every word is true, and the work was good. She gets an offer one level lower than she applied for.

The interviewer could only write down what she said: a manager found the problem and chose the fix, nobody outside her team was involved, and no number changed. That is a solid mid-level story.

The real project was bigger. She had read three old incident reviews and spotted the pattern herself. She had argued against her manager's first idea, which was more servers, with a cost model. She had got the merchant team to change how they read receipts. She left all of that out because it did not feel like "the work".

No company publishes one shared ladder. Former interviewers describe the same pattern, though: a junior story changes the candidate's own work, a senior story changes the whole team, and a staff story changes several teams (see Sources). The level labels you see online, such as L3 to L5 or E3 to E5, are third-party shorthand, so this page uses words.

| What is listened for        | Junior                                          | Mid level                                              | Senior                                                                     |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Who found the problem**   | Someone gave you a defined task                 | Someone gave you a problem and you designed the answer | You found the problem, or turned a vague goal into a plan                  |
| **How unclear it was**      | You asked good questions about an assigned task | You settled the open questions for one feature         | You wrote down what was in and out of scope, and tested the unknowns first |
| **Who you had to persuade** | You took review feedback well                   | You settled a disagreement with a peer, one to one     | You got another team or function to agree, without your manager            |
| **What you left behind**    | Tests and notes for the next person             | A healthier component and a better runbook             | Something the whole team uses: a library, a check, a person you trained    |

A senior story does not need all four at full strength. It needs the first one, and at least one of the last two.

## A weak answer, then the fix

The same project from the story above, told twice. Nothing is added that did not happen.

<Tabs title="One project, two tellings">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a challenging project you delivered recently."*

    *"Last quarter my manager asked me to migrate our payment webhook processor to a queue because it was timing out during flash sales. I designed the producer and consumer, set up a dead-letter queue, wrote integration tests and deployed it over three sprints. It worked well and we had no timeouts on the sale day."*

    **How it is heard:** the manager found the problem and picked the solution. No options were compared. No other team appears. There is no number before or after.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a challenging project you delivered recently."*

    *"Before our peak season I read the reviews of our last three flash-sale incidents. In each one, sending webhooks to merchants had used up the checkout worker pool at about 3,500 requests a second. My manager's first idea was to double the workers. I built a small cost model that showed more workers would not survive a slow bank, and he agreed. I wrote a two-page comparison of three designs and chose a queue with an outbox table. The merchant portal team had to change from waiting for a receipt to polling for it, so I met their lead and we agreed the new contract. On the sale day we peaked at 9,200 requests a second and checkout p99 stayed at 45 ms. Two of the five merchant integrations were not ready in time and stayed on the old path, which I would plan for earlier next time. One other team now uses the outbox code."*

    **What changed:**

    - She says who found the problem: she did, from the incident reviews.
    - She shows a decision, with the option she argued against.
    - Another team appears, and she dealt with them herself.
    - There are numbers, something left behind, and one thing that fell short.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Build your own

Take your three best projects and ask of each:

1. **Who noticed the problem first?** If it was you, say how. If it was your manager, say what you found when you looked into it.
2. **What did you decide, and what was the other option?** One sentence on the road you did not take.
3. **Who outside your team had to change something?** Name the team and how you got their agreement.
4. **What do other people use now because of it?** If the answer is "nothing", that is fine for a mid-level role.

## Red flags

- **Starting with "my manager asked me to".** It may be true, but it hides your part. Recover by adding what you found when you investigated, and what you changed about the plan.
- **Claiming more than you did.** Saying you designed a system you only worked on falls apart at the second follow-up question. Claim the part you owned and go deep on that.
- **A senior story with no other people.** If nobody had to be persuaded, the story is about skill and says nothing about scope. Add the reviewer, the other team or the product manager.
- **Dressing up a junior story.** For a junior role, nobody expects a cross-team project. A defined task done carefully, with a question asked at the right time, is the right size.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="My best project really was assigned to me. Is it useless for a senior interview?">
    No. Most work is assigned. What matters is what you did after the assignment: what you found, what you changed about
    the plan, and who you had to convince. Start the story there.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="How is the level decided when the role is open to two levels?">
    No company publishes this. A former interviewer who writes about it says that hire or no-hire and level are separate
    decisions that need different evidence, and Meta's behavioral feedback is reported to include a level. The safe
    reading: every story you tell is also evidence for your level.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="I am interviewing for a junior role. What should my stories show?">
    That you finish defined work carefully, ask for help at a sensible point with what you have already tried, take
    review feedback without arguing, and are curious about how your part fits the system.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
