## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  A staff interview often asks the same questions as a senior one and listens for **wider reach**: a senior story
  changes one team, a staff story changes **two or more teams**, and a principal story changes **how a large part of the
  organisation works**. A hard problem solved alone does not show that reach, however hard it was.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A senior engineer interviews for a staff role. Asked for her most significant project, she describes a replication bug that had corrupted about 0.2 percent of writes for months. She found it in six weeks, mostly alone, by building her own fault-injection tool. Four engineers before her had failed. It is the best technical work of her career.

The feedback comes back as "strong senior".

Nothing in the story reached past her own team. Nobody else changed what they did. The tool lived on her laptop. No other team learned how to find the next such bug.

The same six weeks contained a staff story that she did not tell. Three teams ran the same replication library. She could have described how she got those teams to adopt her tool in their release checks, who objected and why, and the engineer she trained to own it. That version has less debugging in it and more of what a staff loop asks about.

Walk through the steps:

1. **Senior.** Former interviewers describe senior scope as the whole team, about three or more people, and starting to reach one other team.
2. **Staff.** Two or more teams change what they do because of your work.
3. **Principal.** Many teams, or how a large part of the organisation works.
4. **Deciding across teams.** The most reported staff prompt asks for a major technical decision you drove across several teams without authority over them. A second asks for a project you stopped, or a system you retired that people were attached to.
5. **People above and below.** A time you pushed back on leadership, and a time you helped a senior engineer grow into a staff-level contributor or a lead the team follows.

Loops differ. One widely read guide for staff-plus candidates says some staff loops mirror the senior loop and some look like a manager loop with a coding question added. Prep guides written by former interviewers put the behavioral round at 45 minutes for staff roles at Meta and Google, with an optional 45-minute project retrospective at Meta. None of this is from an official company page.

The same project, told at staff level:

- **Situation and task.** "Four teams in our commerce group each ran their own event pipeline: search, ads, recommendations and fraud. Adding one merchant field took four weeks. No team had the fix in its goals, because they reported to three different directors."
- **Action.** "First I measured it with our finance analyst: about 40 percent of the streaming bill was duplicate work. I took that one-page summary to the three directors and asked for one engineer from each team for a quarter. Second, the fraud team objected that a shared pipeline would slow their daily rule changes. They were right, so I changed the design: a shared core for ingestion, and a section each team could deploy to on its own. Third, I did not build it myself. I split it into three pieces and an engineer from each team led one. I reviewed designs and wrote the migration checker."
- **Result.** "Three of the four pipelines were retired in two quarters. Adding a field went from four weeks to about a day. Ads is still on its own pipeline, because their latency needs were tighter than I had allowed for."
- **Learning.** "The design only got adopted after I changed it for the team that objected most. I now look for that team in the first week."

**What changed:** the other teams appear as people with reasons, the candidate gave work away, there are numbers, and one team that did not move is named.

## A second worked answer: stopping a project

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time you stopped a project, or retired a system people were attached to."_

- **Situation and task.** "Leadership had provisionally approved five engineers for two quarters to build automatic failover between two cloud providers. Sales wanted it for enterprise deals."
- **Action.** "Before the team started, I spent three days on our last 14 major outages. Every one came from our own configuration changes, expired certificates or one overloaded database zone. None would have been prevented by a second cloud. I also priced the data transfer for 400 TB of state. I took a two-page note to the engineering vice president and the sales lead. It proposed four weeks of work on isolating zones and automatic rollback of bad configuration, plus a quarterly restore drill in the second cloud for the customers who asked."
- **Result.** "The larger project was cancelled. Outage minutes fell by about 60 percent over the next year. Two enterprise customers still wanted live failover and one of them did not renew, so the decision had a real cost."
- **Learning.** "I had the sales lead in the room for the decision and not for the analysis. I would involve them from the first day next time."

## Build your own

1. **Which of your projects changed what another team does?** List the teams and what each one changed.
2. **What did you stop?** A project, a system, a meeting, a plan. Say what evidence you used and what it cost.
3. **Who leads something now because you gave it to them?** Name the work, and what you did to help them succeed.
4. **When did a leader hear "no" from you?** Say what you proposed in its place and what happened.

## Red flags

- **The solo hero.** A hard problem solved alone. Recover by adding who else adopted the result and who owns it now.
- **The architect who left.** "I wrote the design and the teams got the implementation wrong." Stay in the story until the result, and say what you did when it went off course.
- **Complaining about politics.** "The directors had competing goals" is the starting condition of most staff work. Say what each wanted and what you proposed that both could accept.
- **Keeping the best work.** If every interesting piece in your stories was yours, there is no evidence that you grow people. Name what you gave away.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="My company is small and has no staff title. Can I still interview for staff?">
    Interviewers grade the reach of the work, and your title is weak evidence either way. At a 40-person company, "two
    or more teams" may be most of engineering. Say how many people and teams changed what they did, and be exact about
    the size.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Does the behavioral round count for more at staff level?">
    Unofficial study guides say so. We found no interviewer or company source that gives a weight. One former hiring
    committee chair at Meta says he read the behavioral feedback first on staff packets. Treat the round as one you
    cannot afford to be vague in.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Will the questions be different from a senior loop?">
    Often they are the same questions. Several former interviewers say staff loops reuse senior prompts and score them
    on wider scope. Prepare staff-sized stories for ordinary questions about conflict, failure and feedback.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
