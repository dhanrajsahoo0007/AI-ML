## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
A Senior (L5) engineer is graded on solving the hardest problems on their team's roadmap; a **Staff/Principal (L6/L7+) engineer** is graded on **changing what goes onto the roadmap across multiple teams**, **killing low-ROI/doomed initiatives early**, and **creating architectural and cultural leverage across 20 to 200+ engineers without direct authority**.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

When a strong Senior (L5) engineer interviews for a Staff (L6 / E6) or Principal (L7 / E7) role and gets down-leveled back to L5, they are often bewildered: *"I told them about a super complex distributed system I built almost entirely by myself in 6 weeks!"*

That sentence is the exact reason they were graded L5. At L6+, **heroic solo coding on a 6-week problem is an anti-signal** if nobody else was multiplied along the way. Staff+ hiring committees (which always include existing L6/L7 engineers and Directors) look for Will Larson's four Staff archetypes (**Tech Lead, Architect, Solver, Right Hand**) and ask:
- *"Did this person align 3+ teams or resolve a multi-quarter organizational deadlock?"*
- *"Did they spot an existential scaling, reliability, or business risk 6–12 months before it paged anyone?"*
- *"Did they grow other L4s into L5s and L5s into L6s?"*

## What interviewers are really testing

<AnimatedDiagram spec="animated/behavioral/leveling-ladder" stepper caption="Compare L5 (Team Execution) vs. L6 (Multi-Team Strategy & Multiplication) and L7 (Org/Company Direction) on the Leveling Ladder." />

Staff and Principal behavioral loops probe **five distinct L6/L7+ signals**:

| Staff+ Signal | What L6 (Staff) / L7 (Principal) Looks Like | What Caps You at L5 (Senior) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Horizon & Problem Selection** | Operating on a 6-to-24 month horizon; identifying unowned cross-org bottlenecks and translating executive business goals into technical strategy | Operating sprint-to-sprint on problems already triaged by your EM or PM |
| **2. Killing Bad Bets ("Strategic Subtraction")** | Proactively canceling or de-scoping a multi-engineer project (including your own!) when a 3-day spike or FinOps model proves negative ROI | Shipping a complex 6-month system faithfully even though the business hypothesis was broken |
| **3. Breaking Org-Level Stalemates** | Aligning competing teams/Directors who have opposing OKRs using paved roads, shared benchmarks, and architectural principles | Escalating cross-org friction to VPs without proposing a technical synthesis |
| **4. Force Multiplication** | Designing paved-road platforms, golden templates, and RFC review cultures that make 30–100 engineers 2x faster and safer | Being the single point of failure who has to review or write every critical PR personally |
| **5. Sponsorship & Bench Building** | Intentionally carving out high-visibility L5/L6-scope problems for rising engineers and co-signing their RFCs | Hoarding the most interesting architectural work for yourself |

## Worked engineering example (STAR+L)

<Tabs title="Staff+ Signature Stories: Multi-Org Alignment & Killing a Doomed Bet">
  <Tab label="Unifying 4 Fragmented Org Pipelines (Conway's Law + Paved Road)">
    **Prompt:** *"Tell me about a time you drove a multi-team technical strategy across organizational boundaries."*

    - **Situation & Task:** *"Across our commerce division (65 engineers across 6 squads), four different teams had built their own bespoke event-enrichment pipelines for search, ads, recommendations, and fraud. Cloud Kafka/Flink spend had ballooned to \$1.8M/yr, schema drift between the four pipelines caused 11 Sev-2 incidents in two quarters, and adding a new merchant attribute took 4 weeks of coordinated changes across all 4 orgs."*
    - **Action:** *"Nobody had 'unify the 4 pipelines' in their OKRs because each team reported to a different Director. As Staff Engineer, I drove a 3-stage alignment:
      1. **Quantifying the Fragmentation Tax:** I co-authored a 3-page 'State of Event Enrichment' brief with our FinOps analyst showing the \$950k/yr duplicate compute cost and the 4-week attribute lead-time tax, getting all 3 Engineering Directors to sponsor a unified architecture.
      2. **Designing for Autonomy (Solving Conway's Law):** The 4 teams resisted a monolithic pipeline because they feared a central bottleneck on their feature velocity. So I designed a **Declarative Feature-View Mesh**: a shared core ingestion/join engine owned by Platform, paired with self-serve isolated WASM/SQL UDF sandboxes where each product squad could deploy new enrichment attributes in 15 minutes without touching other teams.
      3. **Sponsorship & Execution:** Instead of writing all the code myself, I carved out 3 Senior-scope sub-RFCs (backfill engine, UDF sandbox, shadow parity verifier) for three high-potential L4/L5 engineers across the partner teams and coached them through execution."*
    - **Result:** *"Decommissioned all 4 legacy pipelines within 2 quarters, cut annual streaming compute spend by \$1.05M (58%), reduced new attribute rollout time from 4 weeks to 2 hours, and 2 of the engineers I sponsored earned promotion to Senior/Staff in the next cycle."*
    - **Learning:** *"Cross-org architectures fail when they force teams into a single release train; they succeed when you centralize the undifferentiated heavy lifting and decentralize the product iteration seam."*
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Killing a Doomed Multi-Quarter Project ('Strategic Subtraction')">
    **Prompt:** *"Tell me about a time you pushed back on leadership strategy or convinced an organization NOT to build something."*

    - **Situation & Task:** *"Leadership had tentatively approved a 5-engineer, 2-quarter initiative to build a custom multi-cloud Kubernetes failover control plane so our SaaS platform could dynamically shift live traffic between AWS and GCP during cloud provider outages."*
    - **Action:** *"Before the team started sprint 1, I spent 3 days running a **Failure-Mode & Economics Audit** of our last 3 years of outages and our stateful data gravity (400 TB of transactional Postgres + Redis state). I discovered two critical truths:
      1. **Historical Reality:** 100% of our prior 14 major outages were caused by our own bad config deploys, certificate expirations, or single-AZ database saturation—zero would have been prevented by AWS-to-GCP failover.
      2. **Physics & FinOps:** Continuously replicating 400 TB cross-cloud would cost \$640k/yr in egress fees alone, and split-brain state reconciliation during a live cutover carried a higher probability of corrupting customer data than a regional AWS outage itself.
      I presented a **Counter-Proposal Memo** to our VP of Engineering and Enterprise Sales Lead: cancel the 2-quarter multi-cloud project, and invest 4 weeks of 2 engineers into **Multi-AZ Cell Isolation + Automated Config Canary Rollbacks** inside AWS, plus an immutable cross-cloud cold-backup restore drill every quarter to satisfy enterprise procurement checkboxes."*
    - **Result:** *"The VP canceled the multi-cloud build—saving 10 engineer-quarters and \$640k/yr in cloud egress—while our 4-week cell-isolation + config canary work cut customer-impacting incident minutes by 84% over the next 12 months and closed 100% of enterprise security reviews."*
    - **Learning:** *"At the Staff+ level, the highest-ROI code you ever write is often the 3-page analysis that saves 5 engineers from spending 6 months solving the wrong problem."*
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Common red flags & how to recover

- **The "Ivory-Tower Architect" failure mode:** Saying *"I wrote the 30-page vision doc and handed it off to the teams to implement, and they messed it up."* Staff+ engineers stay close to the metal—writing the prototype, pair-debugging the gnarliest production edge case, and owning the outcome through GA.
- **Treating politics/alignment as beneath you:** Complaining that *"Directors had competing goals"* instead of showing how you synthesized a win-win technical roadmap.
