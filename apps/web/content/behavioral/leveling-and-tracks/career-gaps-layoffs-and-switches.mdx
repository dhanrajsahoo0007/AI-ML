## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  A gap, a layoff or a switch needs **three plain sentences: what happened, what you did with the time, and why you are
  ready now**. A short, calm answer closes the topic; a long one opens it.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

Two engineers were laid off in the same reduction. Both are asked, "I see you left in March. What happened?"

The first talks for two minutes: the reorganisation, how unfair the selection was, how his project was the profitable one, how he has "been keeping busy". The interviewer's notes say "still upset, unclear what he has done since".

The second says: "My team was cut in the March reduction, about 200 people. Since then I have rebuilt a side project on a new stack and contributed two fixes to an open-source queue library. I am looking for a backend role with real scale, which is why I applied here." It takes 20 seconds. The interviewer moves on to her work.

## The three sentences

1. **What happened**, as a fact. No apology and no blame.
2. **What you did with the time.** Something concrete: a project, a course you finished, open-source work, caring for someone, recovering from an illness. You do not owe medical or family details.
3. **Why you are ready now**, tied to this role.
4. **Then stop.** If they want more, they will ask.

## Each situation

| Situation                      | A three-sentence answer                                                                                                                                                          | What makes it worse                                                             |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Layoff**                     | "My team was cut in the March reduction. Since then I have built X and contributed to Y. I am looking for a role with Z."                                                        | Explaining why it should not have been you                                      |
| **A gap of a year or more**    | "I took 14 months to care for a parent. In the last four I have been building again: here is what. I am ready for full-time work."                                               | "Personal reasons" with nothing after it                                        |
| **A short stay**               | "The role became on-call support two months in, which was not what we had agreed. I left after trying to change it. I now ask to meet the team before I accept."                 | Blaming the company, or three short stays in a row with three different excuses |
| **Fired for performance**      | "It was not a good match: the role needed deep frontend work and I am a backend engineer. I should have seen that sooner. This role is squarely what I do well."                 | Hiding it. References and dates usually reveal it                               |
| **Switching fields**           | "I spent six years in mechanical engineering. For two years I have written the simulation code for my team, and I have shipped these two projects. I want to do that full time." | Asking them to ignore your past                                                 |
| **Contract work or many jobs** | "I contracted for three years by choice, for range. The longest was 14 months at X. I now want to own one system for several years."                                             | Sounding as if you were never asked to stay                                     |

Be truthful. Dates and reasons can be checked through references, and an invented story is far more damaging than the gap.

## Choosing stories when your work does not match

If you are switching fields or returning after a gap, your recent stories may not be about the role. Three rules:

- **Behavioral questions are about how you work.** A story about resolving a conflict in a hospital lab or a factory is a valid conflict story. Tell it with the same structure and numbers.
- **Lead with the closest work.** Side projects, coursework and open-source contributions count for technical questions. Give them the same detail as paid work.
- **Translate the terms.** Explain your old field's words once, briefly. "A tolerance stack-up, which is how small errors add up across parts."

For returning after a gap, prepare one fresh story from the gap itself, so that not every example is two years old.

## Build your own

1. Write your three sentences. Read them aloud. If it takes more than 30 seconds, cut.
2. Remove any word that blames a person or a company.
3. Make sure sentence two names something a stranger could look at or ask about.
4. Practise saying it in the same tone you use for your current job. The tone matters as much as the words.

Then put your strongest recent project into the [Story Builder](/behavioral/practice-lab/story-builder?for=growth), so the next question lands on your work.

## Red flags

- **Over-explaining.** Length reads as worry. **Recover:** finish the sentence you are in and stop.
- **Bitterness about a former employer.** **Recover:** "It was the right decision for them. Here is what I did next."
- **Hiding a gap by stretching dates.** Background checks compare dates. Use real ones.
- **Apologising for a different background.** Say what it gives you: a field you know that most engineers do not.
