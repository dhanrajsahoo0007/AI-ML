## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  In a manager interview the questions are about **what you did for and with people**: the engineer who was below the
  bar, the one you grew, the hire that failed, the decision you had to pass on. An answer about the code you wrote
  yourself does not answer them.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A tech lead interviews for her first engineering manager role. She leads six engineers and their last three launches were on time.

The interviewer asks: "Tell me about a time your project was behind schedule." She answers with confidence. The payments integration was three weeks late, so she took the hardest module herself, worked two weekends and shipped it. The date held.

The interviewer writes: "Solved a delivery problem by doing the work herself. No evidence of why the team was late or what changed."

Then: "Tell me about a time you managed an underperforming engineer." She describes someone who was slow, says she gave him simpler tickets, and that he left after a few months. The interviewer writes: "No diagnosis. No direct feedback given. Outcome happened to her."

Both stories were true, and both described an engineer covering for a team. A manager's version of the first story would say why the estimate was wrong, what she changed in planning, and who on the team now owns that module.

No company publishes a manager rubric in the sources we read. Two official pages say what is expected of managers. Google's guide to structured interviewing says a people manager should show they can **empower a team, set a long-term vision and develop their reports**. Microsoft lists its manager expectations as **model, coach, care**. The themes below come from the prompts candidates report.

| Theme                          | A strong story shows                                                                             | What weakens it                                         |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Underperformance**           | What you observed, what you found when you asked, the clear expectation you set, and the outcome | A vague "he was not a fit", or a problem you waited out |
| **Growing and keeping people** | One named piece of work you gave to someone so they could grow, and what happened                | "I mentor everyone on my team"                          |
| **Hiring**                     | What you look for, a role that was hard to fill, a hire that went wrong and why                  | Hiring described as the recruiter's job                 |
| **Disagreeing upward**         | What you said to your own manager, with evidence, and how you carried the decision afterwards    | Blaming leadership to your team                         |
| **Conflict**                   | Each person's position stated fairly, and what you did beyond "I talked to them"                 | Taking a side, or leaving it to settle by itself        |
| **Delivery and change**        | The cause of the slip or the reorg's effect, and what you changed in how the team works          | You did the late work yourself                          |

Managing an underperforming engineer is the most reported manager prompt in our research, followed by disagreeing with your own manager and letting someone go.

How many rounds this takes varies. A former chair of a hiring committee at Meta says manager candidates usually get several behavioral rounds, on topics such as managing people, cross-functional partners and a project retrospective. One candidate for a manager role at Stripe reports a people-management round, a management behavioral round, a role-play and a written document presentation. Neither is an official description.

The same situation, handled and told as a manager:

- **Situation.** "A senior engineer on my team of nine, a strong performer the year before, missed two sprint commitments in a row. Three of his reviews were blocking other people."
- **Diagnosis.** "I raised it in our next one-to-one, the same week. I described the two misses and the blocked reviews and asked what was going on. Since a reorg four months earlier he had been the only person who knew the old billing system, and he was answering about 15 questions a day. He had not told me because he thought it was his job."
- **Action.** "I did two things. I set up a rota so that whoever was on call took those questions first, with his notes as the starting point. And we wrote down together what I expected in the next six weeks: the migration design by week two, reviews answered within a day. We checked it every Tuesday."
- **Result.** "He delivered the migration on time and trained two engineers on the old system. The rota was unpopular for the first month, and one engineer asked to come off it."
- **Learning.** "I had missed it for two sprints because I only looked at output. I now ask each person every month what is taking their time that I cannot see."

**What changed:** feedback in the same week, a cause found by asking, a written expectation, and an outcome the manager chose.

## A second worked answer: a decision you disagreed with

**Question:** _"Tell me about a time you had to pass on a decision to your team that you did not agree with."_

- **Situation.** "My director decided that all teams would return to a fixed two-week release train. My team of seven had released daily for a year and our incident rate was the lowest in the group."
- **Action.** "I disagreed with him in private first. I brought our numbers: 210 releases and 2 incidents in a year. He listened and kept the decision, because four other teams had caused most of the incidents and he wanted one rule. I asked for one exception, hotfixes within a day, and got it. Then I told my team myself. I said what the decision was, why he had made it, that I had argued against it and lost, and that we would follow it properly."
- **Result.** "Two engineers were openly unhappy, and one raised it again at every retrospective for a quarter. Our lead time went from one day to nine. Group incidents fell by about a third, which was his goal."
- **Learning.** "I think I was right for my team and he was right for the group. I would tell the team about my disagreement again, and I would still never pass a decision on as 'leadership says'."

## Build your own

1. **Who on your team was below the bar, and when did you first tell them?** Write the date you noticed and the date you spoke.
2. **Who grew because of work you gave them?** Name the work and what you had to stop doing yourself.
3. **When did your team hear a decision from you that you disliked?** Say what you told them, word for word.
4. **What did a late project teach you about how your team plans?** Name the change you made.

## Red flags

- **The engineer who covers for the team.** You fixed the slip by coding at the weekend. Recover by saying what caused the slip and what you changed.
- **"I" for the team's technical work.** Say "I" for what you did about people, planning and decisions. Give the design to the engineer who wrote it.
- **Speaking badly of someone you managed.** Even about a person you let go, describe behavior and the standard, and what you did to help.
- **"Leadership decided."** Passing a decision down with a shrug tells the interviewer your team hears your complaints about your boss.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="I am a tech lead with no direct reports. What do I use for people questions?">
    Use what you have done without the title: an engineer you onboarded, a peer you gave hard feedback to, a junior
    engineer you coached from stuck to independent, interviews you ran. Say clearly that you were not their manager. Do
    not invent authority you did not have.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="I have never let anyone go. What do I say if asked?">
    Say so plainly, then give your closest case: a performance conversation that got serious, or someone who left after
    one. Then say how you would handle it: early feedback, a written expectation, regular check-ins and a decision by a
    date.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="How technical do I need to be?">
    It varies by company and no source gives one answer. Be ready to explain your team's architecture and one technical
    decision you questioned or overrode, and why. Overriding a team's technical decision is a reported prompt.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
