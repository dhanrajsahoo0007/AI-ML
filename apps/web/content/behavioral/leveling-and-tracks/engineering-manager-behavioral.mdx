## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
In Engineering Manager (EM) and Tech Lead Manager (TLM) behavioral loops, your unit of output is no longer the code you write personally: it is **the health, velocity, and technical bar of your system of people**, proven through how you **coach underperformers with candor and care**, **retain and sponsor top performers**, and **build repeatable operational mechanisms**.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

Many first-time Engineering Managers and Senior Tech Leads fail EM behavioral interviews at Meta, Google, Amazon, Stripe, and Uber because they answer people-management questions like a super-Senior IC:
- When asked *"How did you handle a slipping roadmap?"*, they say *"I jumped in and coded the hardest module over the weekend."* (That's an IC hero anti-pattern that leaves the team dependent on you.)
- When asked *"Tell me about an underperformer you managed,"* they either describe someone they fired immediately without coaching, or someone they let coast for 12 months out of conflict avoidance.

EM behavioral loops usually split into **two dedicated 45-minute interviews**:
1. **People Management & Coaching:** Hiring, onboarding, performance turnarounds (PIPs), growing Senior/Staff leaders, diversity & psychological safety.
2. **Execution, Delivery & Cross-Functional Leadership:** Roadmap planning with PM, shielding the team from thrash, operational excellence (SLOs/on-call health), and upward executive management.

## What interviewers are really testing

EM scorecards evaluate **four core pillars**:

| EM Pillar | Strong Hire Signal (M1 / M2+) | Red-Flag / Down-Level Signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Managing Underperformance** | Diagnosing root cause early (skill vs. will vs. role fit vs. personal blocker), delivering clear written + verbal expectations within weeks, providing weekly coaching scaffolds, and making a decisive call if the bar isn't met | Waiting until annual review to surprise someone with a low rating, or immediately firing without coaching |
| **2. Retaining & Growing Top Talent** | Proactively mapping career-stretching projects to each engineer's next level; building succession depth so the team thrives when you are on vacation | Relying on 1 "hero" engineer for all critical work while junior engineers stagnate on bug tickets |
| **3. Execution & Operational Mechanisms** | Fixing recurring delivery slips or on-call burnout via structural mechanisms (20% reliability budgets, RFC templates, triage rotation), never via micromanagement | Micromanaging daily tasks OR being completely hands-off while deadlines slip |
| **4. Product & Upward Partnership** | Co-owning business outcomes with the PM; pushing back on executive thrash with clear capacity/ROI trade-off data | Acting as a passive order-taker for Product, or blaming upper management to your reports |

## Worked engineering example (STAR+L)

<Tabs title="EM Signature Prompts: Underperformance Turnaround & Inheriting a Burned-Out Team">
  <Tab label="Coaching an Underperformer (Diagnosis → Scaffold → Turnaround)">
    **Prompt:** *"Tell me about a time you managed an underperforming engineer. How did you diagnose the issue and what was the outcome?"*

    - **Situation & Task:** *"When I took over a 9-person core platform squad, a Senior Engineer (L5) who had been a strong contributor the prior year had missed two consecutive sprint commitments, was writing terse 1-line PR reviews, and was silently holding up a critical auth migration."*
    - **Action:** *"Instead of jumping straight to a formal PIP or ignoring it, I followed a **3-step diagnostic and coaching loop**:
      1. **Root-Cause Diagnosis (Skill vs. Will vs. Context):** In a candid, empathetic 1:1, I shared specific observations (the two missed milestones and blocked PRs) and asked what was going on. I uncovered that after a re-org 4 months earlier, they had become the sole undocumented point of contact for 15 legacy services (getting interrupted 12+ times a day on Slack) while feeling their new project had vague success criteria.
      2. **Structural Unblocking + Explicit Expectations:** First, I removed the environmental blocker by instituting a rotating 'Interrupt Shield' (on-call engineer handles all Slack questions) that cut their daily interrupts by 80%. Second, we co-wrote a clear **4-Week Expectations & Milestones Doc** defining what L5 ownership looked like on the auth migration: publishing the 2-page rollout RFC by Friday, delegating two sub-tasks to an L4 peer, and posting weekly risk updates.
      3. **Weekly Calibration:** For 6 weeks, we spent the first 15 minutes of our Tuesday 1:1 reviewing progress against that shared doc with zero surprises."*
    - **Result:** *"With the interrupt tax removed and clear expectations set, the engineer delivered the auth migration 1 week early, mentored two L4s onto the legacy rotation, and earned an 'Exceeds Expectations' rating two quarters later."*
    - **Learning:** *"Before assuming an underperformer lacks motivation or skill, audit their environment for hidden interrupt taxes and ambiguous expectations, then pair empathy with crystal-clear written milestones."*
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Turning Around a Low-Morale, High-Burnout Squad">
    **Prompt:** *"Tell me about a time you improved the health, velocity, or operational excellence of a struggling team."*

    - **Situation & Task:** *"I inherited an 8-engineer checkout infrastructure squad with a 42% favorable engagement score, 28 pages per week per on-call engineer, and only 45% sprint predictability, causing Product to lose trust in our roadmap estimates."*
    - **Action:** *"I spent my first two weeks conducting listening 1:1s with all 8 engineers and our PM partner, plus auditing 60 days of PagerDuty alerts. I found the team was trapped in a **reactive doom loop**: 60% of engineering time was eaten by noisy alerts and unplanned urgent requests, leaving no time to fix the root causes. I negotiated a **3-Part Turnaround Contract** with our Director and PM:
      1. **Alert Hygiene Blitz (Weeks 1–2):** We deleted or downgraded 19 non-actionable alerts (keeping only user-symptom SLO pages) and dedicated 25% of sprint capacity strictly to eliminating the top 3 recurring pager sources.
      2. **Capacity-Based Sprint Planning:** Instead of planning assuming 100% feature capacity (when on-call + interrupts ate 30%), we explicitly budgeted 70% roadmap / 20% reliability / 10% on-call buffer.
      3. **Career Ownership Alignment:** I paired each engineer with a named domain subsystem (e.g., Idempotency, Webhook Delivery, FinOps) so everyone had a clear multi-quarter technical identity rather than feeling like interchangeable ticket processors."*
    - **Result:** *"Within two quarters, weekly pages dropped from 28 to **2.5 per week (-91%)**, sprint commitment predictability rose from 45% to **92%**, team engagement jumped from 42% to **89%**, and we experienced zero regrettable attrition over the next 18 months."*
    - **Learning:** *"You cannot fix low morale with team happy hours when the root cause is operational toil; giving engineers uninterrupted focus time and predictable systems does more for morale than any perk."*
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Introspect your own work

<Quiz title="Audit your EM & Tech Lead stories">
  <QuizItem
    question="1. In your management stories, how often do you say 'I decided the architecture' vs. 'I set the constraints and coached my Tech Lead / Senior Engineer to drive the RFC'?"
    hint="EMs who compete with their own Senior Engineers for technical credit fail the people-leadership rubric."
  >
    Frame your technical involvement as **setting context, asking probing questions on failure modes/SLOs, and clearing organizational roadblocks** while your engineers shine as the authors.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem
    question="2. How do you answer: 'How do you stay technical as an Engineering Manager without micromanaging?'"
    hint="Every EM loop asks this question."
  >
    Share a concrete 3-part routine: (1) reading RFCs and postmortems deeply to ask second-order trade-off questions, (2) participating in shadow on-call or bug bashes to feel developer friction firsthand, and (3) reviewing metrics/dashboards weekly, while leaving implementation choices to the ICs.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>

## Common red flags & how to recover

- **Taking credit for your team's technical breakthroughs as "I":** While ICs should say "I" for technical implementation, EMs must say **"I"** for *people, process, coaching, and org-alignment actions*, and **"My engineer [Name] / the team"** for the code they shipped.
- **Badmouthing an underperformer:** Even when describing someone you had to manage out, speak about them with respect and focus on objective role-fit and performance criteria.
