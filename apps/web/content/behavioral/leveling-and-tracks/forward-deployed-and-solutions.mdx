## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  A story from a customer-facing engineering role is scored twice: on **what the customer got and how fast**, and on
  **what your own product kept afterwards**. The strongest stories show you watching the real work, shipping something
  small on real data, and coming back with something reusable or a clear "no".
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A solutions engineer is two weeks from a contract decision at an insurer. The pilot sorts incoming claim documents. The customer's vice president of claims has the dashboard open: 62 percent of claims are routed correctly. She says she will cancel on Friday.

On a video call, his team argues about the number. He does something else. He travels to the claims office and sits behind three adjusters for two hours.

He sees two things nobody on his side knew. About 40 percent of scans are faxes that bundle several patients into one file, so the system reads five claims as one. And the adjusters paste results into an old terminal that cuts text at 80 characters.

That night he writes a splitter that cuts a bundle wherever the patient number changes. On Thursday he processes 150 live claims next to the adjusters, with the vice president watching. Routing is correct on 88 percent.

She signs for one region, not the three that were planned. He takes the splitter back to his product team, and it ships to every customer the next quarter.

## What the interviewer scores

Walk through the steps:

1. **The ask.** What the customer said they wanted, in their words.
2. **Watch the work.** What you saw when you sat with the people doing the job. This is usually where the story turns.
3. **A small first version.** Something working in days, on their real data, with its limits stated.
4. **Something breaks.** A demo fails or a number is bad. Say what you told the customer and how soon.
5. **Result and product.** The customer's number, and what your own product kept: a feature, an adapter, or a request you declined.

This is our own rubric for forward-deployed, solutions and applied engineering roles. It does not describe one company's process.

| Signal                                     | A strong story shows                                                        | What weakens it                                          |
| ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| **You find the real problem**              | A gap between the request and what the users needed, found by watching them | Building the requirements document as written            |
| **You stay calm when it breaks**           | What you said to the customer in the first hour, and the fix                | Blaming their IT team, their data or your own colleagues |
| **You protect the product**                | A customer need met without a private copy of the code                      | One custom version per customer                          |
| **You measure what the customer measures** | Their number: claims routed, hours saved, a contract signed or lost         | Lines of code, or a demo that never went live            |

## A weak answer, then the fix

<Tabs title="A customer demands a custom feature">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time an important customer demanded something that would have created a lot of technical debt."*

    *"A big banking customer needed their own 14-step approval process built into our deployment pipeline before their go-live. It was a large deal, so we made it happen. I worked very hard for three weeks and we delivered on time. The customer was happy. Later two other banks wanted their own versions and it got a bit messy, but that is the nature of enterprise software."*

    **How it is heard:** the candidate said yes without asking why. There is no sign they understood the need. The cost is waved away, and there are now three versions of the product.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time an important customer demanded something that would have created a lot of technical debt."*

    - **Situation and task.** *"A bank asked us to build their 14-step compliance approval into our core deployment pipeline before their go-live in the third quarter. Two other banks were asking for different flows of their own."*
    - **Action.** *"I did not say yes or no on the call. I asked to meet their head of security to understand what the 14 steps protected. Her real requirement was narrower: every deployment needed a signed record that her own system had approved it. So I proposed one hook in our pipeline that calls the customer's approval service and waits for a signed answer. Their 14 steps stayed in their system. I wrote a one-page design, got our product lead to agree it was general, and built it with one engineer from the core team."*
    - **Result.** *"The bank passed its audit with no private version of our code. Their go-live slipped two weeks because their approval service was not ready, and their project manager was not pleased with me. One of the other two banks adopted the same hook in three days. The third still wants its flow built in, and we have said no."*
    - **Learning.** *"A customer's request is a solution they have already designed. I now ask what it protects before I discuss how to build it."*

    **What changed:** a question before an answer, the real need found, a general design, and an honest account of who was unhappy.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Build your own

1. **What did the customer ask for, and what did they need?** Write both, in one sentence each.
2. **Who did you watch or talk to below the sponsor?** Name the role and what you saw.
3. **What broke in front of them?** Write the first thing you said.
4. **What did your product keep?** A feature, an adapter, a document, or a "no".

## Red flags

- **"Their data was a mess."** Messy data is the job. Recover by saying what the mess was and how you handled it.
- **Yes to everything.** A story where every request was built suggests a product with a version per customer. Say what you declined or redirected.
- **No to everything.** "It did not match our API, so they had to fix their side" shows no effort to meet them. Say what you adapted.
- **The sponsor only.** If the only customer in your story is an executive, add the person who used the thing every day.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="I have only worked on internal systems. What can I use?">
    Internal users are customers for this purpose. Use a time another team asked for one thing and needed another, or
    when you sat with support or operations staff to see how your tool was used. Say plainly that the customer was
    internal.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Can I name the customer?">
    Not unless the work is public. Describe them by industry and size: "a regional insurer with about 2,000 staff".
    Leave out contract values if they are confidential, and use proportions.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="What if the pilot failed and the customer left?">
    It can still be a strong story. Say what you would do differently in the first two weeks, and what your product team
    learned. A lost deal with a clear lesson is better than a vague win.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
