## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  A strong machine learning story has four parts: **the offline result, the online result, the gap between them, and
  what you changed**. Interviewers for ML, AI and data roles listen for how you handled the gap, because a model that
  only ever worked in a notebook tells them little.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

An ML engineer on a search team trains a new ranking model. Offline, the ranking score rises 4.8 percent, the team's biggest gain that year. She presents it on a Friday and everyone is pleased.

The A/B test runs for two weeks on 5 percent of traffic. Purchases per search do not move. On mobile they fall slightly.

There are two ways to tell this in an interview. In the first, she says the test was noisy, the team retrained with more data, and a later version did well. In the second, she says what she checked and in what order. The live features matched the training features, so that was not it. The model added 185 ms at p95, and on phones, slower results meant more abandoned searches. The offline labels came from clicks on the old top results, so they favoured the old model's taste.

The second telling is the same project. It is the one that shows how she works when the number she wanted turns out to be wrong.

Walk through the steps:

1. **Offline result.** One number, the metric and the baseline it beat.
2. **Online result.** What happened with real users, including when it was nothing.
3. **The gap.** The causes you checked, in order, and which one the evidence pointed to.
4. **What you changed.** A fix that follows from the cause, not a second guess.
5. **What stayed.** The second result, stated honestly, and the rule or check you added.

Four things are listened for across ML, AI and data roles. This is our own rubric, not a company's.

| Signal                            | A strong story shows                                                    | What weakens it                                           |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| **You investigate the gap**       | A list of causes checked, with what ruled each one in or out            | "The test was noisy" or "we retrained"                    |
| **You look at the data**          | You read 50 or 100 raw examples yourself and found something            | The dataset treated as a box you never opened             |
| **You try the simple thing**      | The baseline you measured first, and the cost of the complex option     | A large model chosen because it was interesting           |
| **You can defend the evaluation** | How the test set was built, what it cannot tell you, and who checked it | Five examples in a notebook, or a score nobody questioned |

## A weak answer, then the fix

<Tabs title="The same project, told twice">
  <Tab label="Weak answer">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time a model looked good offline and did not deliver online."*

    *"I trained a transformer reranker for our search team that improved our offline ranking metric by nearly 5 percent. When we tested it online the results were flat, which was disappointing, but A/B tests can be noisy. We iterated on the architecture, tried a few different configurations and distilled it, and the next version performed really well. It taught me that offline metrics are not everything."*

    **How it is heard:** no cause was found. "We iterated" hides what was done and by whom. The second result has no number. The learning is a slogan.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Fixed">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time a model looked good offline and did not deliver online."*

    - **Offline.** *"I trained a reranker for product search. Our offline ranking score rose 4.8 percent against the production model."*
    - **Online.** *"A two-week A/B test on 5 percent of traffic showed no change in purchases per search, and a small drop on mobile."*
    - **The gap.** *"I checked three causes. First, feature mismatch: I logged live features for 10,000 queries and compared them with training. They matched. Second, latency: my model added 185 ms at p95, and when I split the test by connection speed, the loss was all on slow connections. Third, labels: our test set was built from clicks on the old top three results, so it rewarded agreeing with the old model."*
    - **What I changed.** *"I distilled the model into a smaller one and reranked 25 candidates in place of 100. That brought the added latency to 18 ms. I also asked our analyst to build a second test set from randomised results."*
    - **Result.** *"The second A/B test showed purchases per search up 1.1 percent. That is real, and it is much less than the 4.8 percent I had presented."*
    - **Learning.** *"Every offline evaluation on our team now reports latency next to quality, and fails if p95 goes over budget."*

    **What changed:** three named causes with evidence, a fix tied to the cause, a modest honest number, and a check that stays.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Build your own

1. **Which of your results did not hold up?** Write the offline number and the real one next to each other.
2. **What did you check, in order?** List the causes, and what ruled each one in or out.
3. **What did the fix cost?** Time, accuracy, compute, or a launch date.
4. **What check exists now because of it?** If none does, say what you would add.

## Red flags

- **"The test was noisy."** It may have been, but say how you know: the sample size, the interval, a rerun. Without that it sounds like an excuse.
- **Only architecture.** A story made of layer counts and hyperparameters has no decision an interviewer can score. Add what the data looked like and what users did.
- **A number you cannot explain.** If you quote 93 percent, expect "on what test set, built how?" Prepare that answer, or quote a number you can defend.
- **Complexity for its own sake.** If asked why you did not use a simpler model, "we wanted to try the new one" is weak. Say what the baseline scored.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="I have never run an A/B test. Can I still tell this kind of story?">
    Yes. The pattern is any result that looked good in evaluation and worse in the real world: a model that failed on a
    new customer's data, a pipeline that passed tests and broke on production volumes, a demo that worked and a pilot
    that did not. Keep the four parts.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="My LLM project has no clean metric. What do I say for the result?">
    Say how you measured at all: the set of real questions you built, how many there were, who graded them and how often
    two graders agreed. A small evaluation you can describe exactly is stronger than a large score you cannot.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="I am a data engineer and I do not train models. Does this page apply?">
    The same four parts fit a pipeline: the check that passed, what broke downstream, the gap, and what you changed. For
    example, a row count that matched while a join silently dropped one region's records.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
