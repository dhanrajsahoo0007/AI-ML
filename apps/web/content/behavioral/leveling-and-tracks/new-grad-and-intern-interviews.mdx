## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  At entry level nobody expects large results. Interviewers look for **how you work with others, how you learn, and how
  you react when something goes wrong**, and a course project or an internship is enough to show all three.
</KeyIdea>

## The story

A final-year student is asked about a time she disagreed with a teammate. She thinks: "I have never had a job, so I have nothing."

Then she remembers her database course. Two of four teammates wanted to skip writing tests to make the deadline. She did not argue. She timed how long their last bug had taken to find, which was six hours, wrote tests for the two riskiest parts in one evening, and showed that the next bug took twenty minutes. The team kept tests for those two parts and dropped the rest.

That is a complete answer: a real disagreement, something she did herself, a result with numbers, and a compromise. It came from a course.

## Where your stories come from

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/behavioral/new-grad-story-sources"

1. **Team course projects.** The best source for teamwork and conflict, because you did not choose your teammates and there was a deadline.
2. **Internships.** The best source for feedback and mistakes: someone reviewed your code, and something you wrote probably broke.
3. **Your own projects.** Anything nobody assigned shows initiative and how you learn: a tool you built, an open-source fix, a hackathon.
4. **Jobs, clubs and teaching.** Tutoring, a part-time job or running a student society show responsibility for other people. They count, even when they are not technical.

Prepare **five stories**, not eight: one from each source and one spare.

## What is scored at this level

| Scored                                                 | Not expected                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------- |
| You can say exactly what **you** did in a group        | Leading a team                      |
| You ask for help at the right time, not too late       | Solving everything alone            |
| You took feedback and changed something                | Never having been wrong             |
| You can say how you learned a new tool, step by step   | Already knowing the company's stack |
| You finished things, and can say what "finished" meant | Business results in money           |
| You are curious about the work, with a specific reason | A five-year plan                    |

Results at this level are small and that is fine: "the page loaded in 2 seconds, down from 5", "we got 87 percent", "my mentor merged it with two comments".

## The questions reported most

These are the entry-level prompts that came up most often in the reports we collected.

- **Walk me through the project you are most proud of.** Expect "what was your part?" and "what would you change?".
- **Tell me about a conflict or disagreement with a classmate or teammate.**
- **Tell me about a time you failed or made a mistake.** Expect "what did you do differently afterwards?".
- **Tell me about feedback you received and how you responded.**
- **Tell me about a time you learned something new quickly.**
- **Tell me about your most recent internship or project: what you built, and the hardest part.**
- **Why this company, and what do you want to do after you graduate?**
- **How do you use AI tools when you program?** Say what you use them for, how you check the output, and one time the output was wrong.

Practise them in [Interview Practice](/interviews) with practice mode on.

## Build your own

1. List every team project, internship, own project and other role from the last three years.
2. For each, write the hardest moment in one sentence. That moment is the story.
3. Write what **you** did in that moment, starting each sentence with "I".
4. Add what happened, with any number you have, and what you do differently now.

Write your best three in the [Story Builder](/behavioral/practice-lab/story-builder?for=growth). Its checks will tell you if a story is mostly background or says "we" where it should say "I".

## Red flags

- **"We" for everything.** In a group project the interviewer cannot tell what you did. **Recover:** "My part was".
- **Blaming a teammate.** Even a teammate who did nothing. **Recover:** say what you did about it.
- **A failure that is really a success.** "I worked too hard on it." **Recover:** pick a real one: a bug you shipped, a deadline you missed, a design you had to throw away.
- **Apologising for having no experience.** Do not say "I have only done school projects". Tell the school project well.
- **Inflating an internship.** "I led the migration" will not survive two follow-up questions. Say what you were given and what you did with it.

## Common questions

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="Can I use the same project for several questions?">
    Yes, with five stories you will reuse some. Use a different moment from the project each time, and tell the
    interviewer: "This is from the same project I mentioned."
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Does a non-technical job count?">
    Yes for teamwork, responsibility and dealing with difficult people. A story about handling an angry customer in a
    shop is a good conflict story. Use technical work for questions about projects and learning.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="My grades are average. Will that come up?">
    Rarely in a behavioral round. If it does, say it plainly, say what you spent the time on, and move to what you
    built.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
