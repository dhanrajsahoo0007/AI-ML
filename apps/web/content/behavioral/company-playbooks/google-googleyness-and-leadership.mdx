## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Google runs **structured interviews**: every candidate for a role is scored against the same rubrics, and many
  questions are either "tell me about a time when" or "how would you approach this situation". Candidates call the
  behavioral part **Googleyness and Leadership**. Google's own careers pages do not use that name.
</KeyIdea>

## The loop

What Google says about its own process (see Sources):

- **Assessments come first for many roles**: the Google Hiring Assessment, which is about work style, and role assessments such as a coding exercise.
- Then **one or two short recruiter conversations**, and for some roles a small project.
- Then **a panel of interviews with several Googlers**, by video or in person. In-person interviews are back for some roles.
- Interviews are **structured**. Questions are open-ended and tied to the role, every candidate is scored against the same rubrics, and there are no brainteasers. AI tools are not allowed.
- The whole process **typically takes about 6 to 8 weeks**.

Google's pages give no number of rounds and no round length. The rest comes from candidates who interviewed between 2024 and 2026, and Google has not confirmed it:

- The panel usually has one behavioral round, which recruiters and candidates call "Googleyness and Leadership" or G&L. The other rounds are coding and, for more senior roles, system design.
- Reported lengths run from 20 to 45 minutes, with about 4 to 7 questions. New graduates report about 15 minutes of it added to a 45-minute coding round.
- The questions mix past behavior with hypotheticals, and an engineering manager sometimes runs the round.

## What they score

Google's interview prep page names three areas to prepare. Its re:Work guide to structured interviewing names role-related knowledge, problem solving and leadership as the things an interview measures. Neither page defines "Googleyness". The trait lists you find online come from prep sites and one book, so this table uses Google's three areas.

| Area on Google's prep page                     | A strong story shows                                                                 | What weakens it                                          |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Handling complexity and ambiguity**          | You gave a vague problem a shape: what was unknown, the small test you ran, the call | Waiting for someone to write the requirements            |
| **Working with people and teams**              | A conflict where you can state the other person's case, and what you both changed    | The other person was "difficult" and you were right      |
| **Leadership style**                           | You got people moving toward a goal when you were not the formal leader              | A story with no other people in it, or one about rank    |
| All three, because the interview is structured | Concrete actions, reasons and a result the interviewer can write down                | General statements about how you "usually" handle things |

## How the decision is made

Google's pages say this much:

1. Interviewers must give **structured feedback**.
2. After the interviews, your application and the feedback are **brought together and reviewed**. The decision draws on "a number of perspectives" and goes through "various internal reviews".
3. **Your recruiter** delivers the offer. An outcome can take several weeks.

The pages we read do not mention a hiring committee or team matching. Candidates from 2024 to 2026 still report both: a committee that approves a level, and calls with hiring managers to find a team. The order they report varies. Treat the details (who sits on a committee, how it votes) as hearsay.

What you can rely on: your interviewer writes your answers down against a rubric, and people who were not in the room read that feedback. An answer with a clear action, a reason and a number survives being written down.

## The level bar

Google's pages do not name levels. Candidates and third-party sites commonly write them as L3 (entry) to L6 (staff); treat those labels as unofficial.

Google's re:Work guide says leadership expectations change with level. An individual contributor should show **emergent leadership**: stepping in when a team needs it, without a title. A people manager should show they can empower a team, set a long-term vision and develop their reports.

## Google's ten things in the interview

Google publishes a list called "Ten things we know to be true". Interviewers do not ask about it by name. Five of the ten show up most easily in engineering stories.

| From Google's list                                 | Where it shows in a story                                                |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Focus on the user and all else will follow.**    | You chose the user's outcome over a team metric that already looked fine |
| **It's best to do one thing really, really well.** | You cut scope so that one thing worked properly                          |
| **Fast is better than slow.**                      | You measured latency or a slow feedback loop and shortened it            |
| **There's always more information out there.**     | You went and got the data before arguing your side                       |
| **Great just isn't good enough.**                  | You hit the target and then said what you would still improve            |

## Past questions and hypothetical questions

Google's prep page says many questions take one of two forms, and each needs a different answer.

- **"Tell me about a time when..."** asks for one real story. Google recommends the STAR structure: the situation, your task, your actions, the result. Add what you learned.
- **"How would you approach this situation?"** asks for your reasoning. Do not answer with a story. State one assumption or ask one clarifying question, name what you would find out first, give two options with their trade-offs, choose one, and say what would change your mind. You can add a 20-second real example at the end.

The full method is on [Hypothetical and Situational Questions](/behavioral/foundations/hypothetical-and-situational-questions).

## Worked answers

<Tabs title="One past question, one hypothetical">
  <Tab label="Past: your team disagreed">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time you proposed an idea and your team disagreed."*

    - **Situation and task.** *"I was the data engineer designing an alert for unusual charges in our cloud billing pipeline. I proposed a sliding-window model that flags any account more than three standard deviations from its recent average."*
    - **Action.** *"In the design review, an engineer six months into the job asked in a doc comment whether invoices on the first of the month would set it off. Two senior reviewers agreed with her. I thought the window would absorb it, but I had not tested that. We replayed 90 days of billing data together that afternoon. My model fired 340 false alerts, nearly all on month boundaries. I rewrote the design to remove the monthly pattern first, listed her as co-author, and asked her to present the replay at our team demo."*
    - **Result.** *"False alerts fell from 28 percent of all alerts in shadow mode to 4 percent at launch. The redesign cost three weeks, so we missed the quarter-end date I had promised finance."*
    - **Learning.** *"I now replay real data before a design review, not after it. And I ask the newest person in the review to comment first."*

    **Why it works here:** a colleague's input changed the plan, the credit went to her, and the cost is stated.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Hypothetical: your tech lead disagrees">
    **Question:** *"Your tech lead disagrees with your design and you are sure they are wrong. Walk me through what you do."*

    - **Assumption first.** *"I will assume this is a design we can still change, and that the launch is weeks away, not tomorrow."*
    - **Find out.** *"Being sure is not evidence. I would first write down their objection in my own words and check it with them. Often we disagree about a fact, such as the read pattern, or about a priority, such as launch date against future changes."*
    - **Options.** *"If it is a fact, I would propose a two-day test on real traffic shapes and agree beforehand on the number that settles it. If it is a priority, a test will not help. I would write a one-page comparison and ask the lead to decide, because priorities are their call."*
    - **Choose, and say what changes it.** *"I would start with the test. If the number goes against me, I drop it and say so in the doc. If the lead still chooses their design after seeing my case, I build their design properly and note the metric that would make us revisit it."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Red flags

- **The lone expert.** "Nobody else understood the system, so I rewrote it myself" gives no evidence of working with people. Recover by naming who reviewed it and what you changed because of them.
- **A story for a hypothetical.** If the question is "how would you", a three-minute story does not answer it. Recover with "Let me answer the question as asked", give your approach, then offer the story in one line.
- **Only your opinion.** "I knew it was the wrong design" has no data in it. Say what you measured or who you asked.

## Common questions about this round

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="Is 'Googleyness' an official set of criteria I can study?">
    No official page we read defines it. Google's prep page names three areas: complexity and ambiguity, working with
    people and teams, and leadership style. Prepare stories for those and you cover what the trait lists online are
    guessing at.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Does every candidate go to a hiring committee?">
    Google's careers pages do not say so. They mention several perspectives and internal reviews. Candidates still
    report a committee step, but treat it as commonly reported and not confirmed. It changes nothing about how you
    should answer.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Can I skip a question if I have no matching experience?">
    One candidate reports an interviewer offering exactly that. A better move is to say you have not faced that exact
    situation, name the closest one you have, and ask whether it would be useful to hear.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
