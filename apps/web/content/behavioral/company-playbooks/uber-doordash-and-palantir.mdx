## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Uber and DoorDash are both reported to spend **about an hour on one project of yours**: Uber in its bar raiser round,
  DoorDash in its hiring manager screen. Prepare one project you can defend three questions deep, **including the part
  that went wrong**. We could not open an official process page for either company, and we have no sourced research on
  Palantir.
</KeyIdea>

## The project defense round

Walk through the five steps:

1. **Where it happens.** At Uber, the bar raiser round is reported as one hour with an interviewer from outside the team, who picks the focus and usually goes deep on one past project. At DoorDash, the hiring manager screen is reported as a one-hour deep dive on a past project, before the onsite.
2. **The overview.** You have about two minutes to say what the project was, why it mattered and what your role was. Anything longer uses up the time for the questions that are scored.
3. **The first probes.** What you did yourself, and why you chose that design over the alternatives.
4. **The hard probe.** What went wrong. Reported prompts at both companies ask for a project that failed and for the part that was yours.
5. **What it decides.** Outside sources say Uber's bar raiser round and DoorDash's behavioral round are both used for levelling.

## What they score

Neither company publishes a rubric. This table is built from the prompts reported for the two companies, and the signals are our reading.

| Reported theme                          | A strong answer shows                                           | What weakens it                                         |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **One project, in depth**               | You can answer "why" three times about a decision, with numbers | You know the summary and not the detail                 |
| **Your own part**                       | A clear line between what you did and what the team did         | "We" for every action                                   |
| **How it was delivered**                | The plan, what slipped, and what you cut                        | A launch date and nothing about how you got there       |
| **A project that failed**               | Your share of the cause, stated first                           | The failure was the market, the manager or another team |
| **Ownership outside your team**         | Something falling between teams that you picked up              | "That was not ours"                                     |
| **Getting close to the real operation** | You watched the work being done, and it changed what you built  | Only dashboards                                         |
| **Shipping now or polishing more**      | The rule you used to decide, and one time it was wrong          | "It depends" with no example                            |

## The loop at each company

### Uber

**Official.** The Uber careers page we could open does not describe the loop or list the company's values.

**Reported.** interviewing.io, from conversations with current and former Uber engineers, describes an onsite of two one-hour coding rounds, one hour of system design, and two rounds that concern this page:

- **Collaboration and Leadership**, 75 minutes, run by the hiring manager or a hiring manager from the same organisation. It covers how you delivered a past project, or how you would deliver a hypothetical one.
- **Bar Raiser**, one hour, run by an interviewer from outside the team.

A former Uber engineering manager writes that Uber adopted bar raisers around 2015, that they came from a different director's reporting line, and that they had a short training programme.

**The decision, as reported.** The full panel meets and aims for a unanimous decision. One strong no-hire is enough to reject. The former manager's account says the bar raiser was the final decider in theory, and that hiring managers often escalated in practice. That account covers roughly 2015 to 2018, so do not assume a veto exists today. Uber has not confirmed any of this.

### DoorDash

**Official.** Every official DoorDash page we tried failed to open, so nothing here is confirmed by DoorDash.

**Reported.** interviewing.io, from conversations with current and former DoorDash engineers, describes a 30-minute recruiter call, the one-hour hiring manager screen, a one-hour technical phone screen, then an onsite of two coding rounds, system design and a behavioral round of one hour each. The behavioral round is usually run by the team's hiring manager, who also uses it to tell you about the role.

**The decision, as reported.** Interviewers write feedback, then a roundtable decides hire or no-hire and the level, usually by consensus. The hiring manager has the final say when there is none.

### Why this page prints no values

Prep sites list values for both companies. We could not check any of those lists against an official page, and the lists disagree with each other, so we do not print them. Ask your recruiter for the current list, read it on the company's own site, and match your stories to it yourself.

### Palantir

Palantir is in this page's title, but we have no sourced research on its interviews: no official page and no first-hand report. We removed the earlier description of its rounds for that reason. If you are interviewing there, ask your recruiter which rounds you will have and what each one assesses. One piece of general advice holds for any round that asks you to break down an open problem: say your assumptions aloud, and start from what the user needs before you describe a system.

## Worked answers

<Tabs title="Two answers that survive an hour of probing">
  <Tab label="A project that failed">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a project you drove that failed. What part was yours?"*

    - **Situation and task.** *"I led a four-person project to batch grocery orders so that one driver could carry two. The target was a 10 percent drop in cost per delivery."*
    - **My part in the failure.** *"I set the batching rule from six weeks of historical data and did not test it in a live market first. That was my call. I wanted to hit the quarter."*
    - **What happened.** *"In the first city, cost per delivery fell 7 percent, but late deliveries went from 6 percent to 15 percent, because the history did not include how long drivers wait at a second store. We turned it off after nine days."*
    - **Action.** *"I wrote the review myself and named my decision as the cause. Then I ran the test I should have run first: one zone, two weeks, with store wait times measured. It showed batching only worked when the stores were under 800 metres apart."*
    - **Result.** *"We relaunched with that limit. The saving was 3 percent, not 10, and lateness went back to 6 percent. The project missed its goal."*
    - **Learning.** *"Historical data told me what orders looked like, not what the work was like. I now test in one small area before I commit to a number."*

    **Why it works:** the candidate's own decision comes first, and the modest final result is not dressed up.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Getting close to the real work">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about getting close to the real operation or customer to understand a problem."*

    - **Situation and task.** *"I am a data engineer for a parcel company. Our dashboard said 98 percent of parcels were scanned at the depot within an hour of arrival. Drivers kept complaining that parcels were missing from their routes."*
    - **Action.** *"The numbers and the complaints could not both be right, so I spent a night shift at the busiest depot. Staff were scanning a whole pallet label when the truck arrived and sorting the parcels up to three hours later. The dashboard counted the pallet scan. I changed the metric to count the scan at the sorting belt, and added a count of parcels that were on a pallet but never reached the belt."*
    - **Result.** *"The real figure was 81 percent. Nobody was pleased with me that week. Over the next two months the depot changed its shift pattern and the figure reached 93 percent. Missing-parcel complaints fell by about half."*
    - **Learning.** *"A metric measures what people do to satisfy it. I now watch a process once before I trust a number about it."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Red flags

- **Summary without depth.** You quote a result and cannot explain the cause when asked. Recover by saying which part you know first-hand and which part a teammate owned.
- **A ten-minute overview.** The interviewer has an hour and many questions. Stop at two minutes and ask where they want to go deeper.
- **A failure with no fault of yours.** If asked what part was yours, "none" is the wrong answer. Name a decision or something you did not check.
- **Only dashboards.** A story about an operational problem with nobody from the operation in it. Say who you talked to or watched.
