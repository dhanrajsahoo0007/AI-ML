## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Apple has **no single interview loop**. Each team runs its own, staffed by the people you would work with, and Apple's
  own tips say only that interviews use **open-ended questions about your past performance**. So you prepare for **one
  team**: why that team, the work you are proudest of, and the hardest project you got through.
</KeyIdea>

What Apple says about its own process (see Sources):

- There is **no fixed format**. Interviews are virtual or onsite.
- Interviewers ask **open-ended questions about past performance**. Apple's own examples are working with a team toward a goal, handling deadline pressure, and the areas you need to develop.
- Some roles add a **presentation, case study, technical assessment or coding exercise**.
- **Your recruiter** is the contact for every question about the process.

What candidates commonly report (not confirmed by Apple):

- A recruiter screen, then **one to three phone or video screens**, often one of them with the hiring manager.
- An **onsite of four to eight rounds of about 45 to 60 minutes**, each with one or two interviewers who are future teammates. Reported loops range from 4 rounds for a mid-level role to 7 rounds for a senior one.
- Each team runs its own loop and the questions are not standardised.
- The behavioral round is usually run by the **hiring manager or a senior manager**. Behavioral questions also turn up inside technical rounds.

## What they score

Apple publishes no rubric. This table is built from Apple's three example topics and from the prompts candidates report most often. The signals are our reading.

| Topic                                        | A strong answer shows                                                          | What weakens it                                       |
| -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **Teamwork toward a goal** (Apple's example) | Your part, the other people's parts, and one thing you changed because of them | "We" all the way through, or nobody else in the story |
| **Deadline pressure** (Apple's example)      | What you cut, what you refused to cut, and why                                 | "We worked weekends and shipped"                      |
| **Areas to develop** (Apple's example)       | A real gap, with what you are doing about it this quarter                      | A strength described as a weakness                    |
| **Why Apple, and why this team**             | A reason tied to this team's product, and work of yours that connects to it    | Praise for the brand that would fit any team          |
| **Work you are proud of**                    | Detail: the measurement, the trade-off, the part users never see               | A project summary with no decision of yours in it     |
| **A disagreement with a peer or manager**    | Their view stated fairly, what you did, and the outcome even if you lost       | A story where you were simply right                   |

## How the decision is made

Apple's page says only that the hiring team's process may continue after your interview. The best outside account is from interviewing.io, which cites Apple interviewers:

1. Teams often decide in **a live discussion after the loop**, in place of written feedback.
2. **The hiring manager for the role has the most say.** That is one source's description. Apple states nothing about who decides.
3. An onsite may be cut short if the first half goes badly.

Candidates on Blind report waits of several weeks after the onsite, and say the team has full control over its questions.

The practical point: you are being assessed by the people who would sit next to you, and they will talk about you in a room. Each of them should leave with one clear story about your work.

## Apple's published values in the interview

Apple publishes seven shared values: **Accessibility, Education, Environment, Inclusion and Diversity, Privacy, Racial Equity and Justice, and Supply Chain Innovation**. No official page ties them to interview scoring. Candidates do report the question "How do you line up with Apple's values, and where have you shown them in your career?", so have one honest example. Four of the seven are the easiest for an engineer to show.

| Value                       | Where it shows in an engineering story                                             |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Accessibility**           | You tested with a screen reader or large text and fixed what you found             |
| **Privacy**                 | You collected less data than you were allowed to, or kept processing on the device |
| **Inclusion and Diversity** | You changed how a review or meeting ran so that more people's views were heard     |
| **Environment**             | You cut compute, battery use or waste, and you measured it                         |

Lists that name "innovation", "simplicity" or "craftsmanship" as Apple's core values come from prep sites. Use Apple's own seven.

## Secrecy and craft: what is not confirmed

The title of this page mentions secrecy and craft because they are what people expect from Apple. We found **no first-hand report** of a behavioral question about secrecy or about working with limited information. Explicit attention-to-detail questions also appear only in prep articles.

Two things still hold as general advice:

- **Do not reveal confidential details of your current employer's work.** Explain the constraint, the options and the numbers, and leave out the product name and the customer.
- **Know your project in detail.** "What work moment are you most proud of?" and "What was your most difficult project?" are among the most reported prompts, and the follow-ups go into detail.

## Worked answers

<Tabs title="Two answers that fit a team loop">
  <Tab label="Work you are proud of">
    **Question:** *"What work moment are you most proud of?"*

    - **Situation and task.** *"I am a mobile engineer on a document scanning app. Our scans met the speed target for 95 percent of users. On older phones with 3 GB of memory, about 4 percent of long exports crashed after page 15 and the user lost the whole scan."*
    - **Action.** *"The feature had passed its target and nobody had asked me to look. I thought losing a 15-page scan was worse than a slow one. I profiled memory and found that we kept every decoded page in memory until the export finished. Over three days I changed the export to write each page to disk as it was compressed, and I added a recovery step so that a crash kept the pages already done."*
    - **Result.** *"Peak memory fell from 1.1 GB to about 180 MB. Crashes on those phones fell from 4 percent to 0.3 percent. I did not get them to zero: very large pages still fail, and that is on our list."*
    - **Learning.** *"We now run every media feature on a low-memory test device before release. I am proud of it because users will never know it happened."*

    **Why it works:** it has detail an interviewer can dig into, and it admits what is still broken.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Disagreeing with your manager">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a manager's decision."*

    - **Situation and task.** *"I test firmware for a wearable device. Two weeks before a release, my manager decided to skip the 72-hour battery soak test to hold the date. I thought that was a mistake, because the previous release had a drain bug that only showed after 40 hours."*
    - **Action.** *"I asked for 20 minutes with her. I started with her constraint: a retail partner had a fixed date. Then I showed the earlier bug and proposed a shorter option: a 24-hour soak on the three builds that touched power management. She said no, because even 24 hours put the date at risk, and she took responsibility for the call. I wrote my concern in the release notes, as she suggested, and then set up monitoring so we would see drain reports within a day of launch."*
    - **Result.** *"We shipped on time. There was no drain bug. She was right about that release."*
    - **Learning.** *"I had argued from one past failure. She was weighing a cost I could not see. I still think a soak test should be the default, and we agreed to start it a week earlier on the next release, which we did."*

    **Why it works:** the candidate lost the argument, says so, and shows how they supported the decision.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Red flags

- **A generic "why Apple".** If the answer would fit any team, it fails the "why this team" follow-up. Recover by naming the team's product and one piece of your work that connects.
- **Leaking your employer's secrets.** Product names, customer names or unreleased plans. Recover by restating the story as constraints and numbers.
- **A strength as your area to develop.** Apple lists this question as an example, so expect it. Name a real gap and what you are doing about it.
- **No detail.** A proud moment told as a summary. Add one measurement and one decision.

## Common questions about this round

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="How do I talk about past work that is under an NDA?">
    Describe it at the level of constraints and trade-offs: the request rate, the latency target, the two options and
    why you chose one. Leave out product names, customers and unreleased plans. Say once that you are leaving those out,
    then carry on.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Will I be asked about secrecy?">
    We found no first-hand report of such a question. Prepare for the prompts that are reported: why Apple, why this
    team, the work you are proudest of, your hardest project, a disagreement and your areas to develop.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Is the loop the same for every team?">
    No. Apple gives no fixed format, and candidates report loops of four to eight rounds with questions each team
    chooses. Ask your recruiter how many rounds there are and who runs them.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
