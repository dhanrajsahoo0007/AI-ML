## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Netflix publishes a **culture memo** and no description of its interview steps. Candidates report that questions about
  the memo start in the recruiter screen and return in several onsite rounds, so you need to have **read the current
  memo** and to have **one real story for each value you claim**.
</KeyIdea>

What Netflix says: its careers page describes no interview steps. It says the company aims for a meaningful interview experience and names a recruiting partner as your contact.

What candidates commonly report (not confirmed by Netflix, and it varies by team):

- **A recruiter screen of about 30 minutes** that already includes questions about the culture memo.
- **A hiring manager screen of 30 to 45 minutes**, mostly a discussion of one past project.
- **A technical screen or a take-home exercise.**
- **An onsite of roughly four to eight interviews**, sometimes split over two days.
- The culture and behavioral rounds are run by the **hiring manager**, by a **director or senior manager**, and by an **HR business partner**. One 2025 report for a mid-level role had a hiring manager round, a "workstyle" round with two interviewers, and a final round with a senior manager or director.

Candidates, and one commenter on Blind tagged as a Netflix employee, say each team designs its own process. Ask your recruiting partner for the exact list of rounds.

## What they score

Netflix publishes no rubric. The memo lists the values it wants in people, and the most reported prompt is some form of "pick one value from the memo and tell me about a time you showed it". This table covers five of the memo's values. The signals are our reading.

| Value named in the memo | A strong story shows                                                            | What weakens it                                                   |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Judgment**            | A decision you made yourself, the options you weighed and what you did not know | A decision that a committee or your manager made for you          |
| **Candor**              | Feedback you gave to someone's face, in their words, and feedback you acted on  | "I gave feedback and it went well", or a weakness that is a boast |
| **Courage**             | You said the unpopular thing to the person who could act on it                  | You disagreed in private and went along in the meeting            |
| **Selflessness**        | You gave up time, credit or your own project for the team's result              | Every story ends with your own win                                |
| **Resilience**          | A project that went badly, your part in it, and what you did the next week      | A failure that was somebody else's fault                          |

## How the decision is made

Netflix has made no official statement about this. The best account is from interviewing.io, which cites Netflix interviewers:

1. **Each interviewer gives a pass or a fail.** There is no company-wide rating scale.
2. The decision is mostly made in **a live discussion after the onsite** that aims for consensus.
3. One or two directors usually sit on a loop, and one of them is often from a partner organisation.

Several claims you will read elsewhere have no source we could find: that culture is "40 to 50 percent" of the evaluation, that one interviewer can veto, that a weak culture round overrides strong technical rounds, or that the hiring manager decides alone. Do not plan around them.

## The level bar

Netflix does not publish a level map. A salary site lists levels commonly written as L3 (entry) to L7, with L5 as senior engineer, and candidate reports mention L4 and L5. One reported prompt asks the candidate to argue which of two levels fits them, so know the scope of your own work.

The claim that Netflix hires only senior engineers is out of date. New graduate, intern and mid-level loops are all reported.

## The culture memo in the interview

The current memo has four parts: **The Dream Team**, **People Over Process**, **Uncomfortably Exciting** and **Great and Always Better**. It names these values: selflessness, judgment, candor, creativity, courage, inclusion, curiosity and resilience.

Three things to get right:

- **Read the current page.** Press coverage dates the latest rewrite to June 2024. The title of this page uses "Freedom and Responsibility", which was a heading in older versions; press coverage says the rewrite folded it into People Over Process. If you quote the memo, quote today's wording.
- **Have an opinion.** "What did you make of the culture memo?" is reported as often as any other question. A summary is a weak answer. Say what attracts you, what you would find hard, and give a story for each.
- **The keeper test is not an interview question.** The memo describes it as something a leader asks about a person already on their team: would I fight to keep them, and would I hire them again. We found no first-hand report of a candidate being asked about it. You should still know what it means for you: the bar stays high after you join.

## Worked answers

<Tabs title="Two answers that fit these rounds">
  <Tab label="Your view of the memo">
    **Question:** *"What did you make of the culture memo?"*

    - **What attracts me.** *"The part on candor. On my current mobile team, feedback arrives twice a year in a review. Last spring a peer told me in the moment that my pull requests were too large to review properly. It stung, and he was right. I now keep them under 400 lines, and my median review time went from two days to five hours. I want more of that."*
    - **What I would find hard.** *"People Over Process. I like a checklist. After a bad release I added a manual sign-off step for my team, and within a month people were approving without reading. I replaced it with an automated check on crash rate. I know my first instinct is the checklist, and I would want colleagues to call it out."*
    - **A question back.** *"The memo says the bar stays high after you join. How does your team tell someone early that they are below it?"*

    **Why it works:** it gives an opinion, a real weakness and a story for each, and it ends with a question that shows the candidate read past the headings.

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Critical feedback you received">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about recent critical feedback you received: how you reacted and what you changed."*

    - **Situation.** *"I am a site reliability engineer. After an incident review last year, a backend engineer told me my reviews made her team feel accused. She pointed to one sentence: 'The deploy was pushed without checking the dashboard.'"*
    - **Reaction.** *"I was defensive at first. I said the sentence was accurate. That evening I reread my last five reviews and counted 11 sentences that named a team as the cause and 2 that named a missing safeguard."*
    - **Action.** *"I apologised to her the next morning. I rewrote my template so that each finding names the safeguard that was missing, and I asked her to review my next two write-ups before I published them."*
    - **Result.** *"Attendance at my incident reviews went from about 6 people to about 15. One team still sends nobody, so I have not won everyone back."*
    - **Learning.** *"Accurate is not the same as useful. I now read every write-up once as the team it is about."*

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Red flags

- **Summarising the memo back.** The interviewer has read it. Recover by naming one part you agree with and one you would find hard, each with a story.
- **A weakness that is a boast.** "I hold too high a bar" tells the interviewer you avoided the question. Give a real piece of feedback and what you changed.
- **Agreeing with everything.** A candidate with no doubts about the memo sounds like one who has not thought about it. Honest reservations, stated calmly, are fine.
- **Blame in a failure story.** If the project went badly because of other people, the story shows nothing about you. Say your part first.

## Common questions about this round

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="Will the interviewer apply the keeper test to me?">
    The memo describes the keeper test as a leader's question about people already on the team. Only prep sites say it
    is used on candidates, and we found no first-hand report of anyone being asked about it. Prepare for questions about
    feedback and judgment, which are well reported.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Can I say I disagree with part of the memo?">
    Yes, if you have a reason and an example. Candidates report being asked which part they disagree with or find
    unclear. Name a real reservation and say how you would work with it.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Is every Netflix loop the same?">
    No. Candidates and a Netflix-tagged commenter say each team designs its own process. The number of rounds, who runs
    the culture round and whether there is a take-home all vary. Ask your recruiting partner.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
