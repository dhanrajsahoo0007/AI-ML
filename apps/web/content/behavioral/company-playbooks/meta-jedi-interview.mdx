## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Meta's behavioral interview is one 45-minute round in the full loop. It asks about your past work, and the
  interviewer's feedback carries a **level** as well as a hire or no-hire call, so the **size of your stories** matters
  as much as how well you tell them.
</KeyIdea>

## The loop

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/behavioral/meta-loop"
  stepper
  caption="Meta's software engineer loop. The behavioral round is one 45-minute slot in the full loop."
/>

What Meta says about its own process (see Sources):

- **Recruiter call first**, then a **technical screen**: 45 minutes with an engineer, mostly coding.
- **The full loop** has three kinds of round, each 45 minutes: **coding**, **design** (systems or product, depending on your background) and **behavioral**.
- The behavioral round covers **your previous work, your motivations and how you work with people**. Meta describes its purpose as finding out whether you would do well in a fast-moving engineering organisation.
- Many of Meta's technical interviews now include an AI assistant inside the coding tool. That applies to the coding and design rounds, not to this one.

Candidates and prep guides often call this the "Jedi" round. Meta's own guides call it the behavioral interview, and that is the name your recruiter will use.

## Inside the 45 minutes

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/behavioral/meta-behavioral-round"
  stepper
  caption="The 45 minutes and the eight focus areas, as described by a former Meta interviewer."
/>

A former Meta engineer and manager who ran more than 150 of these interviews describes the round like this: about 5 minutes of introductions, 35 minutes of questions about your past work, and 5 minutes for your questions. Expect **five or six questions**, each followed by more questions on the same story.

He lists eight focus areas. Prep guides often group them into five (conflict, growth, ambiguity, results, communication); the eight are more useful for checking your stories.

| Focus area            | A strong story shows                                                         | What weakens it                                             |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Motivation**        | What you care about, and that it is users or impact, not only the technology | "It was an interesting problem" and nothing else            |
| **Proactive**         | You started something hard without being asked                               | Every project was handed to you with a spec                 |
| **Unstructured work** | You took a problem nobody owned and gave it a shape                          | Waiting for a manager or product manager to define the work |
| **Perseverance**      | Several attempts at a real blocker, including the ones that failed           | A blocker that went away by itself                          |
| **Conflict**          | You dealt with the person directly, with evidence                            | Going to a manager first, or avoiding the person            |
| **Empathy**           | You can state the other person's position as they would                      | The other person is "difficult"                             |
| **Growth**            | A real weakness and a change other people noticed                            | "I work too hard"                                           |
| **Communication**     | Short, ordered answers that a non-specialist can follow                      | A four-minute answer with no clear action of yours          |

## How the decision is made

According to the same first-hand account:

1. After the round, the interviewer writes feedback with a **hire or no-hire recommendation**, a **level**, and how confident they are.
2. The recommendation is built from the signals across the eight areas. A hire at any level needs positive signals in nearly all of them.
3. No single interviewer decides. After all the feedback is in, the interviewers discuss strengths and concerns in a **debrief**.

Meta does not publish who makes the final call, so treat any detailed claim about committees as hearsay. What you can rely on: your behavioral feedback is read next to your coding and design feedback, and it includes a level.

The practical point: if you are interviewing for senior and every story is something you did alone inside your own area, the stories are graded as mid level however well you tell them.

## Meta's six values in the interview

Meta publishes six values. Interviewers do not ask about them by name, but your stories are heard against them.

| Value                                     | Where it shows in a story                                                          |
| ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Move fast**                             | You tested an idea with a small experiment before building the whole thing         |
| **Build awesome things**                  | You cared about the quality of what users got, not only that it shipped            |
| **Be direct and respect your colleagues** | You told someone a hard thing to their face, and you kept the working relationship |
| **Focus on long-term impact**             | You chose the problem because of its effect, and you can say what that effect was  |
| **Live in the future**                    | You adopted a new tool or approach early and can say what it changed               |
| **Meta, Metamates, me**                   | You did something for the team or the company that was not your job                |

## Worked answers

<Tabs title="Two answers that fit this round">
  <Tab label="Direct feedback to a peer">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time you gave difficult feedback to a teammate."*

    - **Situation and task.** *"On our ads ranking team, a senior peer was merging urgent fixes straight to the release branch during experiments. It broke the match between our offline and online feature logs twice in one month."*
    - **Action.** *"I did not go to our manager. I asked him for 15 minutes the same day. I started with why he was doing it: our build took 55 minutes and blocked urgent experiment changes. Then I showed him the effect: two weeks of corrupted training labels that had cost three teammates their experiments. We built a four-minute check together for changes that only touch configuration, so nobody needed to skip the build."*
    - **Result.** *"Logging incidents went to zero, config changes went from 55 minutes to 4, and we still work together well."*
    - **Learning.** *"When a good engineer goes around a process, I now fix the slow process and keep the rule it was protecting."*

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Starting something nobody asked for">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a time you found an opportunity nobody had asked you to work on."*

    - **Situation and task.** *"While debugging a feed latency spike, I saw that our service fetched a 40-field user profile on every request. Most feed rankings used 4 of those fields."*
    - **Action.** *"Nobody owned the contract between the two services. I spent one afternoon on a prototype that fetched only the needed fields, on 1 percent of traffic, to get real numbers. With those I wrote a one-page proposal, built a small tool that updated the three calling teams' code, and moved the main path over two weeks."*
    - **Result.** *"Network traffic between the two services fell by 78 percent and feed <Term id="p99" /> improved by 19 ms."*
    - **Learning.** *"A one-day test on real traffic persuaded the other teams faster than a slide deck would have."*

    **Why it reads as senior:** three other teams changed their code because of it.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Red flags

- **Waiting for permission.** "I waited for my manager to choose" reads as a lower level in every focus area.
- **A solo hero.** A story with no product manager, no designer and no other engineer in it suggests you work alone. Say who you worked with.
- **Only successes.** Meta's guide says that talking about failed projects and conflicts will not count against you. Having none sounds like you are hiding them.
- **Answers in general terms.** Meta's guide says interviewers will steer a general answer back to a concrete example. Start with the example.

## Common questions about this round

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="Does this round include coding?">
    Meta's guide describes it as a conversation about your past work and motivations. Coding is in the coding rounds. Be
    ready to explain technical decisions from your stories in detail, because the follow-up questions go there.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Is it safe to talk about a project that failed?">
    Yes. Meta's guide says to be honest and that unsuccessful projects or conflicts with peers will not count against
    you. What is listened for is what you learned and what you changed.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="What should I ask in my five minutes at the end?">
    Ask about how the interviewer's team works, not about perks. Two examples: how a recent disagreement about
    priorities was settled, and what a new engineer usually ships in the first month.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
