## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  Stripe and Airbnb each **publish a short list**, six operating principles and four core values, and candidates report
  a round at each company where your stories are heard against that list. Neither company publishes how the round is
  run, so learn the lists from the official pages and treat round counts and lengths as reported.
</KeyIdea>

Walk through the four steps:

1. **The round.** At Stripe, a one-hour behavioral round is reported, run by a hiring manager or by a "Leveler" who interviews across levels. At Airbnb, one or two core values interviews are reported, and the sources disagree on the number and the length.
2. **The questions.** Stripe is reported to ask engineers about past experience and to give manager candidates scenarios. Airbnb's reported questions start with why you want to work there and which part of the mission matters to you.
3. **The yardstick.** This is the one official part: Stripe's operating principles and Airbnb's core values, both on their careers pages.
4. **Afterwards.** Both decisions are described only by outside sources. See the company sections below.

## What they score

Neither company publishes a rubric, and no official page ties a question to a principle. The table gives each published item with our reading of where it shows in an engineering story.

| Published item                           | Where it shows in a story                                                        | What weakens it                                           |
| ---------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Stripe: Users first**                  | You argued for the user when an internal option was easier                       | "That was the product team's job"                         |
| **Stripe: Create with craft and beauty** | You went back and improved something that already worked                         | "It passed review, so we moved on"                        |
| **Stripe: Move with urgency and focus**  | What you cut to hit a date, and what you refused to cut                          | Speed with no mention of what it risked                   |
| **Stripe: Collaborate egolessly**        | You changed your mind in front of others, or gave away the credit                | A disagreement you simply won                             |
| **Stripe: Stay curious**                 | You dug into something outside your area until you understood it                 | Curiosity as a claim with no example                      |
| **Stripe: Obsess over talent**           | Someone you hired, taught or raised the bar for                                  | Only your own output                                      |
| **Airbnb: Champion the Mission**         | A reason for joining that is about the mission, and a cause you kept pushing for | A reason that would fit any travel or marketplace company |
| **Airbnb: Be a Host**                    | You went beyond what was required for a customer or colleague                    | Help that was part of your job anyway                     |
| **Airbnb: Embrace the Adventure**        | A real risk you took, and how it turned out                                      | A "risk" with no downside                                 |
| **Airbnb: Be a Cereal Entrepreneur**     | You delivered something with almost no time, people or clarity                   | A plan that needed a bigger team before it could start    |

## The loop at each company

### Stripe

**Official.** We could not open any Stripe page that describes the interview loop. Stripe's culture page lists the six operating principles: **Users first. Create with craft and beauty. Move with urgency and focus. Collaborate egolessly. Stay curious. Obsess over talent.**

**Reported.** interviewing.io, from conversations with current and former Stripe engineers, describes a five-hour onsite: coding, system design, a bug bash, an integration round (below staff) or a presentation (staff and above), and the one-hour behavioral round. It says answers are judged against the operating principles. A first-hand report from an engineering manager candidate in 2025 lists a people-management round, a management behavioral round, a role-play round and a written document presentation.

**The decision, as reported.** The panel submits written feedback and then meets. Most decisions are by consensus, and the hiring manager has the final say when there is none. Stripe has not confirmed this.

Stripe states nothing about a "bar raiser". One prep site uses the term; interviewing.io calls the role a Leveler.

### Airbnb

**Official.** We could not open any Airbnb page that describes the interview loop. The careers site names four core values: **Champion the Mission. Be a Host. Embrace the Adventure. Be a Cereal Entrepreneur.** "Belong Anywhere" is the mission phrase and is not one of the four.

**Reported.** interviewing.io, from conversations with current and former Airbnb interviewers, describes an onsite of coding (one to three hours), system design (one hour), behavioral (one hour) and two core values interviews run by non-engineers, then team matching with hiring managers. Other sources say one core values interview, or "one or two". One candidate in January 2026 reports a single core values round of about 20 minutes, held after the onsite had been passed.

**The decision, as reported.** A hiring committee decides the level and the hiring manager decides whether to make the offer. Airbnb has not confirmed this. The claim that core values interviewers have a veto comes from outside sources only.

**What this means for you.** Your core values interviewer may not be an engineer. Tell each story so that someone from design or operations can follow it: the person affected, what you did, what changed. Leave the architecture for the technical rounds.

## Worked answers

<Tabs title="One answer for each company">
  <Tab label="Stripe: a mistake that affected users">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a mistake that affected users."*

    - **Situation and task.** *"I am a backend engineer on a payouts service. I changed how we retried a failed call to a bank. I tested timeouts and missed one case: the bank accepting the payout and then timing out on the reply."*
    - **What happened.** *"For about three hours, that case created a second hold on the merchant's balance. 212 merchants saw money held twice. No money left the system, but to a merchant it looked like it had."*
    - **Action.** *"I found it from a support ticket, not from an alert, which was a second failing of mine. I rolled back within 20 minutes of the ticket, wrote a script to release the 212 duplicate holds, and had support email each merchant the same day with what had happened. Then I added a unique key to every payout request so a retry could never create a second hold, and a check that compares holds to payouts every hour."*
    - **Result.** *"All holds were released within five hours. Eleven merchants had already contacted support. The hourly check has caught two unrelated problems since."*
    - **Learning.** *"I had tested the failures I could imagine. I now list every point where a remote system can succeed without telling us, and test each one."*

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Airbnb: almost no resources">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about delivering something with almost no resources, time or clarity."*

    Told for an interviewer who is not an engineer:

    - **Situation and task.** *"On a home rental marketplace, last-minute cancellations by hosts rose 18 percent before summer. Guests were arriving with nowhere to stay. A proper prediction system was six months away and I had three weeks and no extra people."*
    - **Action.** *"I spent two mornings listening to support calls with hosts who had just cancelled. Most of them were not careless. They had taken a booking on another site and forgotten to update their calendar with us. So the fix was a reminder. I used the messaging tool we already had to ask hosts with a booking in the next 14 days to confirm their calendar, and to make it one tap."*
    - **Result.** *"Last-minute cancellations fell by about a quarter over the peak weeks. I had hoped for half. Hosts who never open messages were not helped at all."*
    - **Learning.** *"I would have built the wrong thing if I had started from the data. The calls told me why."*

    **Why it works:** no jargon, a guest and a host in the story, and a result that is honest about its limit.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Red flags

- **"That was product's job."** A wall between your code and the user fails Users first and Be a Host alike. Recover by saying what you learned from users yourself.
- **Talking down to a non-engineer.** If the interviewer asks a basic question, answer it plainly. Recover from jargon by restating the point in one sentence.
- **A mistake with no user in it.** "We had a brief incident" hides the effect. Say who was affected, how many and for how long.
- **Reciting the list.** Naming a value mid-answer ("this shows I am a host") sounds rehearsed. Tell the story and let the interviewer make the link.
