## In one sentence

<KeyIdea>
  NVIDIA publishes its hiring steps and names **no behavioral round**. Behavioral questions are reported to come up in
  the **hiring-manager conversation**, mixed with technical questions about your past work, so each story you bring has
  to hold up when the questions go into the engineering detail.
</KeyIdea>

What NVIDIA says on its "How we hire" page (see Sources):

- **Phone interviews first.** After that you meet the **hiring manager, team members and people from other groups**, by video or in person.
- Interviews are **one-on-one, in a small group or as a panel**, and each usually takes **30 to 60 minutes**.
- **One interview on site and in person is required** before an offer.
- An optional 15-minute **Insider Chat** with a member of one of NVIDIA's community resource groups is there for your questions. NVIDIA says it has no effect on the hiring decision.

What is reported and not confirmed by NVIDIA: interviewing.io, writing from conversations with NVIDIA engineers, describes an optional 30-minute call with the hiring manager early on, and a one-hour hiring-manager round in the final interviews. It describes that round as a conversation that moves between your experience and technical questions. We could not open a first-hand candidate report of NVIDIA's behavioral questions, so every prompt you see listed for NVIDIA, here or elsewhere, is commonly reported and no more than that.

## What they score

NVIDIA publishes no scoring guide for behavioral answers. The table below is ours. It groups the themes in the prompts that candidates commonly report, and says what a strong and a weak story look like for each.

| Theme in commonly reported prompts | A strong story shows                                                        | What weakens it                                           |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Motivation**                     | A reason for this team that follows from work you have already done         | "AI is the future", or the company's size                 |
| **Depth in past work**             | You can explain each design choice and what you measured                    | You describe the system and cannot say why it is that way |
| **Taking criticism**               | The exact criticism, and the change you made afterwards                     | A defense of the original work                            |
| **Your own quality bar**           | What you would not ship, and what you did when you shipped below it         | "It passed review"                                        |
| **Persistence**                    | A problem that took weeks, with the attempts that failed                    | A hard problem that was solved on the first try           |
| **Acting without authority**       | You fixed or changed something outside your area by persuading its owner    | Waiting for a manager to assign it                        |
| **Candor**                         | You told someone an unwelcome fact about their work, with evidence, to them | Telling everyone except that person                       |

## How the decision is made

What NVIDIA publishes is short:

1. A decision **typically comes within a few weeks** of your interviews.
2. Your **feedback may be shared across teams**. In practice a team you did not apply to may read what your interviewers wrote.

What is reported and not confirmed: interviewing.io says each interviewer files a scorecard on a 1 to 5 scale and that the decision weighs all of them together. One source also says a hiring committee debates each candidate. Nothing NVIDIA publishes supports that, so do not plan around it.

NVIDIA does not publish who makes the final call or how much weight a behavioral signal carries.

## The level bar

NVIDIA does not publish how the bar changes with level. The one report we have, again from interviewing.io, is that junior candidates are judged mostly on coding, and that at principal level and above the system design and behavioral signals count for the most.

## NVIDIA's values in the interview

NVIDIA's Code of Conduct lists the company's values, including **innovation**, **intellectual honesty**, **speed and agility**, **excellence and determination**, and **one team**. We read those names in a search result for the document and could not open the document itself, so check the wording in the Code of Conduct before you quote a value back to an interviewer.

NVIDIA does not say that interviewers score against these values. Use them as a check on your stories:

| Value, as listed above           | Where it could show in a story                                           |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Innovation**                   | You tried an approach your team had not used, and measured it            |
| **Intellectual honesty**         | You reported a result that made your own work look worse                 |
| **Speed and agility**            | You changed the plan in days when a measurement showed it was wrong      |
| **Excellence and determination** | You kept at a problem for weeks and can list what you ruled out          |
| **One team**                     | You fixed something for another group because it was blocking the result |

## Worked answers

Both answers fit a conversation that goes technical. Each one names the measurement, so a follow-up question has something to hold on to.

<Tabs title="Two answers for a hiring-manager conversation">
  <Tab label="A problem that took weeks">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about a problem you worked on for a long time before it paid off."*

    - **Situation and task.** *"I work on training infrastructure. About 1 run in 40 of one vision model ended with a loss of NaN after six to nine hours. Nobody owned it, because rerunning usually worked."*
    - **Action.** *"I spent parts of seven weeks on it. I first suspected the learning rate schedule and was wrong. Then I suspected mixed precision and was wrong again. What worked was saving the exact batch that came before each failure. Three failures in, I saw that the batches held repeated samples. Two data loader workers were writing to the same shared memory buffer after a worker restarted."*
    - **Result.** *"After the fix we had no NaN failures in the next 600 runs. That saved about 90 GPU hours a week. It is a small number for our cluster, and the bigger gain was that people stopped rerunning failures without looking."*
    - **Learning.** *"I now record the input to a rare failure before I form a theory. My first two theories cost me three weeks."*

  </Tab>
  <Tab label="An unwelcome truth">
    **Question:** *"Tell me about telling someone an unwelcome truth about their work."*

    - **Situation and task.** *"I test firmware for a storage controller. A senior engineer reported a 22 percent throughput gain from his new scheduler. When I reran his benchmark, I saw the new build had run with a warm cache and the old build had run cold."*
    - **Action.** *"I reran both builds cold and warm, three times each, before I said anything. The gain was 6 percent. I sent him the numbers privately that afternoon and offered to go through my setup in case I had made the mistake. He checked and agreed."*
    - **Result.** *"He corrected the internal report the next day. I was too slow for one thing: the 22 percent figure had already gone to a partner team in a slide two days earlier, and they had to be told. The scheduler still shipped, with the 6 percent figure."*
    - **Learning.** *"I now rerun a benchmark the day the result is posted, before it gets copied into anything."*

    **Why it works:** the evidence came first, the person heard it first, and the answer admits the part that went wrong.

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Red flags

- **A story with no technical layer.** If the round mixes behavioral and technical questions, "we improved performance" invites a question you cannot answer. Recover by naming the measurement and the one design choice that was yours.
- **Defending the work when asked about criticism.** Recover by stating the criticism in the critic's words, then the change you made.
- **Quoting values you have not checked.** Value lists for NVIDIA differ between third-party sites. Recover by describing what you did and leaving the label out.
- **"Why NVIDIA" answered with the market.** Recover with one sentence that connects a problem you have worked on to what this team builds.

## Common questions about this round

<Quiz title="Common questions">
  <QuizItem question="Is there a separate behavioral round at NVIDIA?">
    NVIDIA's hiring page does not name one. It says you meet the hiring manager, team members and people from other
    groups. Reports place most behavioral questions with the hiring manager. Prepare stories for every conversation,
    because any interviewer may ask about your past work.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="Does the Insider Chat count towards the decision?">
    NVIDIA says it does not. It is an optional 15 minutes with a member of a community resource group. Use it to ask
    what working there is like.
  </QuizItem>
  <QuizItem question="How technical should a behavioral story be?">
    Tell it in plain terms first, in about two minutes. Then be ready to go down two or three levels: what you measured,
    what you ruled out, and why you chose the design you did. Reports describe the hiring-manager round as moving
    between the two.
  </QuizItem>
</Quiz>
