"Which metric would you use?" follows almost every modeling answer. Interviewers check that you know the definitions, but more importantly that you can choose metrics that match the business cost of errors, handle imbalance, and explain why offline metrics may not match online results.

<InterviewQuestions>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Explain the confusion matrix." category="Classification" level="Junior">
    A table of predictions versus actual classes. For binary problems it has true positives, false positives (predicted positive but actually negative), false negatives (missed positives) and true negatives. Most classification metrics are computed from these four counts.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Why is accuracy misleading for imbalanced data?" category="Classification" level="Junior">
    If 99% of transactions are legitimate, a model that always predicts "legitimate" is 99% accurate yet catches no fraud. Accuracy hides performance on the rare class, so use precision, recall, F1, PR-AUC or cost-based metrics instead.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Define precision and recall, and when you prioritize each." category="Classification" level="Junior">
    Precision is true positives over predicted positives: how many flagged items were right. Recall is true positives over actual positives: how many real positives were found. Prioritize precision when false alarms are costly, such as spam filters removing real email; prioritize recall when misses are costly, such as cancer screening or fraud.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is the F1 score and when is it appropriate?" category="Classification" level="Junior">
    The harmonic mean of precision and recall, which is high only when both are high. It is useful as a single number for imbalanced problems when both errors matter similarly. When one error is more costly, use F-beta to weight recall or precision more, or optimize a cost-based metric directly.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What does ROC-AUC measure?" category="Classification" level="Mid">
    The area under the curve of true positive rate versus false positive rate across all thresholds. It equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative, so it measures ranking quality independent of threshold. 0.5 is random and 1.0 is perfect.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC: which should you use for imbalanced problems?" category="Classification" level="Mid">
    PR-AUC, the area under the precision-recall curve, is usually more informative when positives are rare, because ROC-AUC's false positive rate stays small even with many false positives when negatives are abundant, making weak models look good. PR-AUC's baseline equals the positive rate, so it reflects how hard the problem is.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you choose a classification threshold?" category="Thresholds" level="Mid">
    Base it on the business trade-off, not the default 0.5. Estimate the cost of false positives and false negatives and pick the threshold minimizing expected cost, or meet a constraint such as at least 95% recall or a fixed review capacity per day. Choose it on validation data and monitor it, since score distributions shift.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is log loss and why use it?" category="Classification" level="Mid">
    Log loss, or cross-entropy, is the average negative log of the probability assigned to the true class. It rewards well-calibrated, confident correct predictions and heavily penalizes confident mistakes. It evaluates probabilities themselves, which matters when downstream decisions use scores directly.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is calibration and how do you check and fix it?" category="Calibration" level="Senior">
    A model is calibrated if, among predictions with probability 0.7, about 70% are positive. Check with reliability diagrams and metrics such as expected calibration error or the Brier score. Fix with Platt scaling or isotonic regression fitted on a held-out set. Calibration matters for pricing, risk and any decision that uses probabilities.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Macro vs micro vs weighted averaging for multi-class metrics?" category="Classification" level="Mid">
    Macro averaging computes the metric per class and averages them equally, giving rare classes equal weight. Micro averaging pools all predictions, so frequent classes dominate; for single-label problems micro F1 equals accuracy. Weighted averaging weights classes by their frequency. Report macro when performance on rare classes matters.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="MAE vs MSE vs RMSE: when do you use each?" category="Regression" level="Junior">
    MAE averages absolute errors, is in the target's units and is robust to outliers. MSE averages squared errors and penalizes large errors heavily; RMSE is its square root, back in target units. Use RMSE when large errors are especially bad, MAE when all errors matter proportionally or outliers are noise.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What are the problems with MAPE?" category="Regression" level="Mid">
    Mean absolute percentage error is undefined or explodes when actual values are zero or near zero, penalizes over-forecasts and under-forecasts asymmetrically, and favors models that under-predict. Alternatives include weighted MAPE (WAPE), symmetric MAPE, or scaled errors such as MASE.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What does R-squared tell you about a regression model?" category="Regression" level="Junior">
    The share of variance in the target explained by the model compared with predicting the mean. It can be negative on test data if the model is worse than the mean. It does not tell you whether errors are acceptable in business terms, so pair it with error metrics in target units.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you evaluate a forecasting model?" category="Forecasting" level="Mid">
    Use time-based backtesting with rolling or expanding windows that mimic production, compare against naive baselines such as last value or seasonal naive, and use scale-appropriate metrics such as MAE, WAPE or MASE. For probabilistic forecasts, evaluate prediction interval coverage or quantile loss.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What metrics are used for ranking and recommendation?" category="Ranking" level="Mid">
    Precision at k and recall at k, MRR (mean reciprocal rank of the first relevant item), MAP (mean average precision) and nDCG, which uses graded relevance and discounts lower positions. Coverage, diversity and novelty complement accuracy, and online metrics such as engagement and retention decide final success.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Explain nDCG." category="Ranking" level="Senior">
    Discounted cumulative gain sums the relevance of results with a logarithmic discount by position, so relevant items near the top count more. Normalizing by the ideal ordering's DCG gives nDCG between 0 and 1, comparable across queries. It handles graded relevance such as perfect, good and fair.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you evaluate a clustering model without labels?" category="Clustering" level="Mid">
    Internal metrics such as the silhouette score, Davies-Bouldin index and within-cluster variance measure compactness and separation. But the most important test is usefulness: whether clusters are stable across samples, interpretable and actionable for the business, which often needs domain review.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you evaluate an anomaly detection model?" category="Classification" level="Senior">
    If some labeled anomalies exist, use precision at k, recall at a fixed alert volume and PR-AUC. Without labels, review top-ranked anomalies with experts, inject synthetic anomalies to measure detection, and track alert precision in production. Alert volume must fit the team's capacity.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you connect model metrics to business metrics?" category="Business Alignment" level="Mid">
    Map errors to outcomes: each false positive and false negative has a cost, and each correct action has a value. Estimate expected business value at different thresholds, report model performance in those terms, and validate the relationship with experiments. Stakeholders care about money, time saved or risk avoided, not AUC.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you get a confidence interval for a model's metric?" category="Calibration" level="Mid">
    Bootstrap the test set: resample it with replacement many times, compute the metric on each resample, and take percentiles. This shows whether a difference between two models is meaningful or within noise, especially with small test sets.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you evaluate model fairness?" category="Business Alignment" level="Senior">
    Compute metrics separately for relevant groups and compare them, using criteria such as demographic parity (similar positive rates), equal opportunity (similar true positive rates) or equalized odds (similar true and false positive rates). These criteria can conflict, so choose based on the application's harms and legal context, and examine data and labels for bias.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you evaluate LLM outputs?" category="LLM Evaluation" level="Mid">
    Use task-specific checks where possible, such as exact match, schema validity, unit tests for code or retrieval metrics. For open-ended outputs use rubric-based LLM-as-judge calibrated against human ratings, pairwise comparisons and human review samples. Track faithfulness, safety, latency and cost alongside quality.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What are BLEU and ROUGE, and why are they weak for LLM evaluation?" category="LLM Evaluation" level="Mid">
    They measure n-gram overlap with reference texts, BLEU for translation and ROUGE for summarization. Many correct answers use different words, and overlap says nothing about factual accuracy or helpfulness, so they correlate poorly with human judgment for open-ended LLM outputs.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you evaluate a model when labels arrive with a long delay?" category="Business Alignment" level="Senior">
    Use proxy metrics that arrive sooner but correlate with the final outcome, monitor input and prediction distributions for drift as an early warning, evaluate on matured cohorts as labels arrive, and report metrics by cohort age. Validate that the proxies track the final metric over time.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is the Brier score?" category="Calibration" level="Mid">
    The mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and actual binary outcomes. Lower is better, and it reflects both calibration and discrimination. It is a common way to compare probabilistic classifiers alongside log loss.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic used for in credit scoring?" category="Classification" level="Senior">
    It is the maximum difference between the cumulative score distributions of positives and negatives, measuring how well the model separates the two classes. Credit risk teams use it alongside AUC and Gini, where Gini equals 2 times AUC minus 1.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Why should you look at metrics by segment, not just overall?" category="Business Alignment" level="Mid">
    An overall metric can hide poor performance on important groups, such as new users, certain regions or rare classes, and Simpson's paradox can make overall comparisons misleading. Segment metrics reveal where the model fails, guide data collection and catch fairness issues.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How would you choose metrics for a medical screening model?" category="Business Alignment" level="Senior">
    Prioritize sensitivity (recall) so few true cases are missed, while keeping specificity high enough that follow-up tests are manageable. Report sensitivity and specificity at the chosen operating point with confidence intervals, positive and negative predictive values at the real prevalence, calibration, and performance across patient subgroups, validated on external data.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is specificity, and how does it relate to the false positive rate?" category="Classification" level="Junior">
    Specificity is true negatives divided by all actual negatives: the share of negatives correctly identified. The false positive rate equals 1 minus specificity. Sensitivity (recall) and specificity together describe a test's performance on positives and negatives separately.
  </InterviewQuestion>
</InterviewQuestions>
