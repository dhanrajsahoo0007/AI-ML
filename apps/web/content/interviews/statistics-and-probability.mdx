Data science interviews almost always include a statistics round. Interviewers check that you can explain core ideas in plain words, avoid classic misinterpretations (especially of p-values and confidence intervals), and solve short probability problems out loud.

Related: [A/B testing & experimentation](/interviews/ab-testing-and-experimentation) · [model evaluation metrics](/interviews/model-evaluation-metrics)

<InterviewQuestions>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is the difference between a population and a sample, and a parameter and a statistic?" category="Fundamentals" level="Junior">
    The population is the whole group you care about; a sample is the subset you observe. A parameter describes the population, such as the true mean, and is usually unknown; a statistic, such as the sample mean, is computed from the sample and used to estimate the parameter.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Mean vs median: when should you use each?" category="Fundamentals" level="Junior">
    The mean uses every value and suits symmetric data without extreme outliers. The median is the middle value and is robust to outliers and skew, so it is better for things like income, house prices or response times. Reporting both reveals skew.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is the difference between variance and standard deviation?" category="Fundamentals" level="Junior">
    Variance is the average squared deviation from the mean; standard deviation is its square root, so it is in the same units as the data and easier to interpret. Sample variance divides by n minus 1 instead of n to correct for estimating the mean from the same sample.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Explain the central limit theorem and why it matters." category="Distributions" level="Junior">
    For independent samples with finite variance, the distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as sample size grows, whatever the shape of the original distribution, with standard error equal to the standard deviation divided by the square root of n. It justifies normal-based confidence intervals and tests for means, including most A/B test analyses.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is the law of large numbers?" category="Distributions" level="Junior">
    As the number of independent observations increases, the sample average converges to the expected value. It explains why estimates stabilize with more data, but it says nothing about the shape of the sampling distribution, which is what the central limit theorem adds.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Describe the normal, binomial, Poisson and exponential distributions with an example of each." category="Distributions" level="Mid">
    Normal: continuous and symmetric, such as measurement errors or heights. Binomial: number of successes in n independent yes-or-no trials, such as conversions out of visitors. Poisson: count of events in a fixed interval at a constant rate, such as support tickets per hour. Exponential: time between Poisson events, such as time until the next ticket, and it is memoryless.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is a p-value?" category="Hypothesis Testing" level="Junior">
    The probability of observing data at least as extreme as what you got, assuming the null hypothesis is true. A small p-value means the data would be surprising under the null. It is not the probability that the null is true, and it does not measure the size or importance of an effect.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What are Type I and Type II errors?" category="Hypothesis Testing" level="Junior">
    A Type I error is a false positive: rejecting a true null hypothesis, with probability alpha, the significance level. A Type II error is a false negative: failing to reject a false null, with probability beta. Power is 1 minus beta, the chance of detecting a real effect.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is statistical power and what affects it?" category="Hypothesis Testing" level="Mid">
    Power is the probability of detecting an effect when it truly exists. It increases with larger sample size, larger true effect size, lower variance in the metric and a higher significance level. Experiments are sized in advance to reach a target power, commonly 80%, for the smallest effect worth detecting.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you interpret a 95% confidence interval?" category="Estimation" level="Mid">
    If you repeated the sampling and interval procedure many times, about 95% of the intervals would contain the true parameter. It does not mean there is a 95% probability that this particular interval contains the true value; in the frequentist view the parameter is fixed. Narrower intervals mean more precise estimates.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="When do you use a t-test vs a z-test vs a chi-square test?" category="Hypothesis Testing" level="Mid">
    A z-test compares means or proportions when the variance is known or samples are large. A t-test compares means when the variance is estimated from small samples. A chi-square test checks association between categorical variables or goodness of fit to expected counts. For large-sample proportions, z and chi-square tests agree.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What are non-parametric tests and when would you use one?" category="Hypothesis Testing" level="Mid">
    Tests that do not assume a specific distribution, such as Mann-Whitney U for comparing two groups, Wilcoxon signed-rank for paired data, and Kruskal-Wallis for several groups. Use them with small samples, heavy skew, outliers or ordinal data. Bootstrapping is another distribution-free way to get intervals.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is the multiple comparisons problem and how do you correct for it?" category="Hypothesis Testing" level="Mid">
    Running many tests at alpha 0.05 makes false positives likely: with 20 independent tests, the chance of at least one is about 64%. Corrections include Bonferroni, dividing alpha by the number of tests (conservative), and Benjamini-Hochberg, which controls the false discovery rate and keeps more power.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Explain Bayes' theorem with an example." category="Probability" level="Junior">
    Bayes' theorem updates the probability of a hypothesis given evidence: posterior equals likelihood times prior divided by the evidence. Example: a disease affects 1% of people and a test is 90% sensitive with a 5% false positive rate. A positive result gives a probability of disease of about 0.009 divided by (0.009 plus 0.0495), roughly 15%, because healthy people vastly outnumber sick ones.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is the difference between frequentist and Bayesian statistics?" category="Probability" level="Mid">
    Frequentists treat parameters as fixed and probability as long-run frequency, using p-values and confidence intervals. Bayesians treat parameters as uncertain, combine a prior with data to get a posterior distribution, and report credible intervals and direct probabilities, such as the probability that variant B beats A. Bayesian methods need a prior; frequentist results are often misread as Bayesian statements.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is conditional probability, and what is independence?" category="Probability" level="Junior">
    The probability of A given B is the probability of both divided by the probability of B. Events are independent when knowing one does not change the probability of the other, so the probability of both equals the product of their probabilities.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="You flip a fair coin until you get two heads in a row. What is the expected number of flips?" category="Probability" level="Mid">
    Six. Let E be the expected flips from the start and F the expected flips after one head. From the start: E equals 1 plus half of F plus half of E. After a head: F equals 1 plus half times zero plus half of E. Solving gives E equals 6.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is the birthday problem and what does it teach?" category="Probability" level="Mid">
    With only 23 people, the chance that two share a birthday is just over 50%, because the number of pairs grows quadratically. It teaches that coincidences and collisions, such as hash collisions or duplicate IDs, become likely much sooner than intuition suggests.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is selection bias? Give examples." category="Study Design" level="Mid">
    Selection bias occurs when the sample is not representative of the population because of how it was collected. Examples include survivorship bias, such as studying only successful companies, survey non-response, analyzing only users who opted in, and training data that excludes rejected loan applicants.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Correlation vs causation: how can you establish causation?" category="Study Design" level="Junior">
    Correlation shows variables move together, which can be due to confounders, reverse causation or chance. The gold standard for causation is a randomized controlled experiment. With observational data, methods such as difference-in-differences, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity and matching can estimate causal effects under stated assumptions.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is Simpson's paradox?" category="Study Design" level="Mid">
    A trend that appears in several groups reverses or disappears when the groups are combined, because group sizes differ and a confounding variable is unevenly distributed. A classic example is a treatment that looks better in every subgroup but worse overall. The fix is to analyze within the relevant strata.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is a confounding variable?" category="Study Design" level="Junior">
    A variable that influences both the supposed cause and the outcome, creating a misleading association. For example, ice cream sales and drowning both rise in summer because of temperature. Randomization or controlling for confounders in analysis addresses it.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What are the assumptions of linear regression and how do you check them?" category="Regression" level="Mid">
    Linearity, independent errors, constant error variance and, for inference, roughly normal errors, plus limited multicollinearity. Check with residual plots against fitted values, Q-Q plots of residuals, tests or plots for autocorrelation in time data, and variance inflation factors for multicollinearity.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you interpret R-squared, and why can it mislead?" category="Regression" level="Mid">
    R-squared is the share of variance in the target explained by the model. It always increases when features are added, even useless ones, so adjusted R-squared penalizes extra features. A high R-squared does not mean the model is correct or causal, and a low one can still be useful for prediction in noisy domains.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you interpret a coefficient in logistic regression?" category="Regression" level="Mid">
    Each coefficient is the change in the log-odds of the outcome for a one-unit increase in the feature, holding others constant. Exponentiating it gives an odds ratio: a coefficient of 0.7 means the odds multiply by about 2 for each unit increase.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is bootstrapping?" category="Estimation" level="Mid">
    Resampling the observed data with replacement many times and computing the statistic on each resample to approximate its sampling distribution. It gives standard errors and confidence intervals for statistics without a simple formula, such as medians or ratios, with few distributional assumptions.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is maximum likelihood estimation?" category="Estimation" level="Mid">
    Choosing parameter values that make the observed data most probable under the assumed model. Many familiar estimators are MLEs, such as the sample mean for a normal distribution's mean, and minimizing log loss in logistic regression is maximizing likelihood.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How would you detect outliers, and should you remove them?" category="Fundamentals" level="Junior">
    Use visual checks, the interquartile range rule, z-scores for roughly normal data, or model-based methods for multivariate data. Do not remove them automatically: first find out whether they are errors or real extreme events. Errors should be fixed or dropped; real extremes may matter most, and robust methods can limit their influence.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="A test result is statistically significant but the effect is tiny. What do you tell stakeholders?" category="Hypothesis Testing" level="Senior">
    Statistical significance only means the effect is unlikely to be zero; with large samples, trivial effects become significant. Report the effect size with a confidence interval, translate it into business impact, and compare it with costs and the minimum effect that matters. A significant but negligible effect may not justify a change.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is regression to the mean?" category="Study Design" level="Mid">
    Extreme measurements tend to be followed by values closer to the average, because extremes are partly due to random variation. It can make interventions look effective when applied to the worst performers, such as underperforming stores, which is why control groups are needed.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you calculate the sample size needed for a test?" category="Hypothesis Testing" level="Senior">
    Specify the significance level, desired power, baseline variance or rate, and minimum detectable effect, then use the power formula or a calculator. For comparing two means, the size per group is roughly 2 times (z for alpha over 2 plus z for power) squared times the variance, divided by the effect squared. Smaller effects or noisier metrics need much larger samples.
  </InterviewQuestion>
</InterviewQuestions>
