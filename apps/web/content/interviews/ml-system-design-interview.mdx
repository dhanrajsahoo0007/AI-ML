ML system design interviews are open-ended on purpose. Interviewers score structure, judgment and communication as much as technical depth. A reliable framework: **clarify → frame as ML → data → features → model → evaluation → serving → monitoring → iterate**, spending more time where the interviewer pushes.

<InterviewQuestions>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How should you structure a 45-minute ML system design interview?" category="Framework" level="Junior">
    About 5 minutes clarifying requirements and success metrics, 5 framing the ML problem, 10 on data and features, 10 on models and training, 5 on evaluation, 5 on serving and monitoring, and the rest on deep dives the interviewer chooses. Summarize decisions as you go and keep a visible diagram.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What clarifying questions should you ask first?" category="Framework" level="Junior">
    Who the users are and what the product goal is; the scale (users, items, requests per second); latency and freshness requirements; available data and labels; constraints such as privacy, fairness and cost; and how success will be measured, both offline and in business terms.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you frame a business problem as an ML problem?" category="Framework" level="Mid">
    Define the prediction target and its label source, the input available at prediction time, the type of task (classification, ranking, regression, generation), and how predictions turn into actions. Check that optimizing the ML objective would actually improve the business metric, and name a simple non-ML baseline.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you choose the right labels?" category="Data" level="Mid">
    Pick labels that reflect the true goal and are available at scale: explicit labels such as ratings or human annotations when possible, implicit signals such as clicks, watch time or purchases otherwise. Discuss noise, delay and bias in each, for example clicks favoring clickbait, and consider combining signals.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Why start with a simple baseline?" category="Modeling" level="Junior">
    It gives a reference point, is fast to build and debug, and may already be good enough. A heuristic, popularity ranking or logistic regression shows whether complex models are worth their cost, and it remains a fallback in production.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you pick a model architecture in a design interview?" category="Modeling" level="Mid">
    Match it to the data and constraints: gradient-boosted trees for tabular features, two-tower models for large-scale retrieval, deep rankers or cross-encoders for precise ranking, pretrained transformers for text and images, and LLMs where generation or reasoning is needed. Justify with latency, data size and interpretability, and mention what you would try next.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Why do large-scale systems use multi-stage architectures?" category="Architecture" level="Mid">
    Scoring every candidate with the best model is too slow and expensive. A cheap retrieval stage narrows millions of items to hundreds, a heavier ranker orders those, and a re-ranker applies business logic. Each stage trades accuracy for cost at the right point.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you design the evaluation section?" category="Evaluation" level="Mid">
    Name offline metrics tied to the task, such as recall at k for retrieval, nDCG for ranking or PR-AUC for imbalanced classification, with a proper time-based split. Then describe online evaluation with A/B tests on business metrics plus guardrails, and how you would check segments and fairness.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What should you say about serving?" category="Architecture" level="Mid">
    Whether predictions are batch or real-time, the latency budget per stage, where features come from (online store, request context), caching, scaling with traffic, fallbacks when the model fails, and how model versions are rolled out through shadow or canary deployments.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What should you say about monitoring and iteration?" category="Architecture" level="Mid">
    Monitor latency, errors, input drift, prediction distributions, model metrics as labels arrive, and business KPIs. Describe retraining triggers, how feedback becomes new training data, and how you would detect feedback loops. End with the next improvements you would try.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Design a news feed ranking system: outline your answer." category="Common Prompts" level="Senior">
    Clarify the goal, such as meaningful engagement rather than raw clicks. Candidates come from followed accounts, groups and recommended content; a multi-task ranker predicts click, like, comment, share and hide, combined into a value score; re-ranking adds diversity and integrity filters. Evaluate offline with ranking metrics and online with A/B tests on time well spent and retention, watching for feedback loops and harmful content.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Design a YouTube-style video recommender: key points?" category="Common Prompts" level="Senior">
    Candidate generation with two-tower models and co-watch item-to-item lists, a deep ranking model trained toward expected watch time and satisfaction signals rather than clicks, re-ranking for diversity and freshness, exploration for new videos, and evaluation via A/B tests on long-term engagement.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Design a spam or harmful content classifier: key points?" category="Common Prompts" level="Senior">
    Combine text, image and metadata features with account and behavior signals, use a cascade of fast filters then heavier models, set thresholds for auto-remove, human review and allow, handle adversarial adaptation with frequent retraining, and measure precision and recall per policy category with appeals and reviewer feedback as labels.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Design a RAG-based question answering system: key points?" category="Common Prompts" level="Senior">
    Permission-aware ingestion with structure-aware chunking, hybrid retrieval and re-ranking, grounded generation with citations and abstention, evaluation of retrieval recall and answer faithfulness, incremental re-indexing, caching and cost control, and guardrails against prompt injection in retrieved content.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Design an ad click-through rate prediction system: key points?" category="Common Prompts" level="Senior">
    Real-time scoring of candidate ads with features about the user, ad, context and their crosses; models such as gradient-boosted trees or deep models like Wide and Deep or DLRM; calibrated probabilities because they feed auctions; handling of very high cardinality ids with embeddings or hashing; frequent retraining; and evaluation with log loss, calibration and online revenue and user experience metrics.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Design an ETA prediction system for a delivery app: key points?" category="Common Prompts" level="Senior">
    Break ETA into parts such as preparation, pickup and travel; use real-time features such as traffic, courier location, restaurant load and weather; train regression models with asymmetric or quantile losses because being late costs more than early; evaluate with MAE and lateness rate by segment; and serve with low latency and frequent updates during the trip.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you handle cold start in a design interview?" category="Data" level="Mid">
    For new users, rely on context, onboarding questions and popular items, then adapt quickly from early interactions. For new items, use content features and give controlled exploration exposure. Mention measuring cold-start segments separately.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you discuss scale in an ML design?" category="Architecture" level="Mid">
    Estimate numbers: users, items, requests per second, data volume per day, model size and latency per stage. Use them to justify architecture choices, such as approximate nearest-neighbor search, caching, precomputation, sharding and batch versus real-time, rather than adding components without reason.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you address fairness and responsible AI in a design?" category="Evaluation" level="Senior">
    Identify who could be harmed, measure performance and outcomes across relevant groups, check training data for bias, consider constraints or re-ranking for exposure fairness, provide transparency and appeal paths, and monitor fairness metrics in production alongside accuracy.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What are the most common mistakes candidates make?" category="Framework" level="Junior">
    Jumping into models before clarifying the goal, choosing metrics that do not match the business, ignoring data and labels, proposing one huge model for everything, forgetting latency and cost, skipping evaluation and monitoring, and not communicating trade-offs or checking in with the interviewer.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you handle a deep-dive question you don't know the answer to?" category="Framework" level="Mid">
    Say what you know and reason from first principles out loud, state assumptions, propose how you would find out or test it, and connect back to the design. Interviewers value structured reasoning and honesty over bluffing.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do LLM system design interviews differ from classic ML ones?" category="Framework" level="Senior">
    They focus more on prompting, retrieval, tools, orchestration and guardrails than on training models, on evaluation of open-ended outputs with rubrics and LLM judges, on cost and latency per token, and on safety concerns such as prompt injection. Data and evaluation discipline remain just as important.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you design for privacy in an ML system?" category="Evaluation" level="Senior">
    Minimize collected data, anonymize or pseudonymize identifiers, enforce access controls and retention limits, consider techniques such as differential privacy or federated learning where appropriate, and comply with relevant regulations. Explain how privacy constraints affect features and evaluation.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you prioritize improvements after launch?" category="Framework" level="Mid">
    Analyze errors and segments to find the biggest losses, estimate the impact and cost of fixes such as better data, new features, model changes or product changes, and run the highest-value experiments first. Data quality fixes often beat model changes.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Design a system to detect duplicate listings or questions: key points?" category="Common Prompts" level="Mid">
    Generate candidates cheaply with blocking keys or embedding nearest neighbors, then classify pairs with a model using text similarity, image similarity and metadata features. Tune thresholds for precision since merging wrong items is costly, use human review for borderline cases, and evaluate with labeled pairs.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Design a demand forecasting system: key points?" category="Common Prompts" level="Senior">
    Forecast at the needed level, such as store and product per day, with features for seasonality, holidays, promotions, prices and weather; use gradient-boosted trees or deep time-series models with hierarchical reconciliation; evaluate with time-based backtests using WAPE and bias; produce prediction intervals for inventory decisions; and retrain frequently.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you close a system design interview well?" category="Framework" level="Junior">
    Summarize the design in a few sentences, restate the key trade-offs and risks, list what you would do next with more time, and invite questions. A clear summary shows you can communicate the design to stakeholders.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Design a visual search system ('find similar products from a photo'): key points?" category="Common Prompts" level="Senior">
    Detect and crop the product in the photo, embed it with a vision model fine-tuned on product images, search an ANN index of catalog embeddings filtered by category and availability, then re-rank with a more precise model and business signals. Evaluate with recall at k on labeled pairs and online conversion.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Design a notification system that decides when to send push notifications: key points?" category="Common Prompts" level="Senior">
    Predict the value of each notification, such as the probability of a meaningful open, against the cost of annoyance, such as disabling notifications or uninstalling. Rank candidates per user, apply frequency caps and quiet hours, and evaluate with long-term A/B tests on retention and opt-out rates, not just opens.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What does a strong candidate do differently?" category="Framework" level="Mid">
    They drive the conversation with a clear structure, tie every technical choice to requirements, quantify scale, discuss trade-offs openly, design evaluation and monitoring from the start, and adapt quickly when the interviewer redirects.
  </InterviewQuestion>
</InterviewQuestions>
