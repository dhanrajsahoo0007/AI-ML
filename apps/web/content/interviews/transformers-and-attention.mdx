Transformer questions show up in almost every ML engineer, applied scientist and LLM engineer interview. Junior rounds check that you can explain the blocks and the attention formula. Senior rounds push into cost: KV cache size, why decoding is memory-bound, FlashAttention, GQA, MoE and training parallelism.

<InterviewQuestions>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Why do we need multi-head attention?" category="Attention" level="Junior">
    One attention head can express only one relevance pattern per token. Several heads, each with its own projections in a smaller subspace, can capture different relationships at once, such as syntax, coreference and position. The total cost is similar to one full-width head because the width is split across heads.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is the difference between self-attention and cross-attention?" category="Attention" level="Junior">
    In self-attention, queries, keys and values all come from the same sequence. In cross-attention, queries come from one sequence and keys and values from another, for example the decoder attending to the encoder's output in translation, or text attending to image features in a multimodal model.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is a causal mask and why is it needed?" category="Attention" level="Junior">
    It sets the attention scores for future positions to minus infinity before the softmax, so each token can only attend to itself and earlier tokens. This lets a decoder be trained on next-token prediction for every position in one parallel pass without seeing the answers.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Why do transformers need positional encodings?" category="Positions & Tokens" level="Junior">
    Self-attention is a weighted sum over a set, so it is invariant to token order. Without positional information, sentences with the same words in different orders would produce the same representations. Positional encodings add order, either to the input vectors or inside attention.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Why do LLMs struggle with tasks like counting letters or exact arithmetic?" category="Positions & Tokens" level="Mid">
    They operate on sub-word tokens, so individual letters and digits are often hidden inside larger tokens, and numbers are split inconsistently. The model must infer spelling and digit structure indirectly. Spelling a word out first, or using tools for arithmetic, avoids the problem.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What does the feed-forward network in a transformer block do?" category="Fundamentals" level="Mid">
    It is a two-layer MLP applied to each token independently, typically expanding to about four times the model dimension and back. Attention mixes information across tokens; the feed-forward network transforms each token's representation. It holds most of a standard block's parameters, and research suggests it stores much of the model's factual knowledge.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Why are residual connections important in transformers?" category="Fundamentals" level="Junior">
    They add each sub-layer's input to its output, giving gradients a direct path through deep stacks and letting each layer learn an incremental update. Without them, training networks with dozens of layers would be unstable because gradients would vanish or explode.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Compare encoder-only, decoder-only and encoder-decoder transformers." category="Architecture Variants" level="Junior">
    Encoder-only models such as BERT use bidirectional attention and masked-token training, and excel at classification and embeddings. Decoder-only models such as GPT use causal attention and next-token training, and are the basis of most chat LLMs. Encoder-decoder models such as T5 read the input bidirectionally and generate with a causal decoder via cross-attention, which suits translation and summarization.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How is BERT pre-trained?" category="Architecture Variants" level="Junior">
    With masked language modeling, where about 15% of tokens are selected and the model predicts them from both directions, and originally with next sentence prediction, which classifies whether two sentences are consecutive. Later work such as RoBERTa showed that dropping next sentence prediction and training longer improves results.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is span corruption in T5?" category="Architecture Variants" level="Mid">
    Random spans of the input are replaced with sentinel tokens, and the decoder is trained to output the missing spans in order. It teaches the model to reconstruct multi-token gaps and fits T5's text-to-text framing.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Why did decoder-only models become the standard for LLMs?" category="Architecture Variants" level="Mid">
    One stack and one objective are simple to scale, and next-token prediction turns every token of text into a training signal. At scale they show in-context learning, and instruction tuning lets them handle almost any task as generation, so a single model can replace many task-specific ones.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Why do MoE models need load balancing?" category="Scaling" level="Senior">
    Routers tend to favor experts that are already slightly better, which then get more training and become even more favored, collapsing onto a few experts. An auxiliary balancing loss and per-expert capacity limits keep tokens spread out so all experts learn and hardware stays evenly loaded.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What are scaling laws?" category="Scaling" level="Mid">
    Empirical findings that language model loss decreases as a smooth power law as parameters, training tokens and compute increase. They let teams predict the loss of a large run from small ones and choose model size and data size for a budget.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you estimate training compute for an LLM?" category="Scaling" level="Mid">
    About 6 floating-point operations per parameter per training token, 2 for the forward pass and 4 for the backward pass. A 7-billion-parameter model on 2 trillion tokens needs about 8.4e22 operations.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Explain data, tensor and pipeline parallelism." category="Scaling" level="Senior">
    Data parallelism replicates the model and splits batches, averaging gradients, with ZeRO or FSDP sharding the states to save memory. Tensor parallelism splits individual weight matrices across GPUs, usually inside one server because it needs fast links. Pipeline parallelism places groups of layers on different GPUs and streams micro-batches through them. Large training runs combine all three.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Why is teacher forcing used when training decoders?" category="Training" level="Mid">
    During training, the decoder is fed the ground-truth previous tokens rather than its own predictions, which lets all positions be trained in parallel and stabilizes learning. The downside is exposure bias: at inference the model conditions on its own possibly wrong outputs, a situation it saw less during training.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is weight tying between input embeddings and the output layer?" category="Training" level="Mid">
    Using the same matrix for the token embedding lookup and the final projection to vocabulary logits. It reduces parameters, which matters for large vocabularies, and often improves quality because both directions share one representation of each token.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What is dropout's role in transformers, and why do many large LLMs train without it?" category="Training" level="Senior">
    Dropout regularizes by randomly zeroing activations, which helps when data is limited relative to model size. Large LLMs are often trained for about one pass over trillions of tokens, so overfitting is not the main risk and dropout mainly slows learning. Fine-tuning on small datasets is where dropout or other regularization becomes useful again.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do Vision Transformers apply attention to images?" category="Architecture Variants" level="Mid">
    They split an image into fixed-size patches, flatten and linearly project each patch into a token, add position embeddings and a classification token, and run a standard transformer encoder. With enough data they match or beat convolutional networks, though they have weaker built-in assumptions about locality.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Where are most parameters in a transformer, and where is most compute at long context?" category="Scaling" level="Senior">
    In a standard block the feed-forward network holds about two-thirds of the parameters, attention projections about one-third. At short context, compute follows parameters. At long context, the quadratic attention term and the KV cache dominate, which is why long-context work focuses on attention efficiency.
  </InterviewQuestion>
</InterviewQuestions>
