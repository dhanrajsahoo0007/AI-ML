"How many GPUs, and what will it cost?" comes up in most LLM design interviews. Interviewers want a chain of small, checkable steps from users to tokens to GPUs to dollars, with every assumption named, not a guess. This page builds that chain for Brightpost, and the [LLM Serving and Inference](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/llm-serving-inference) page supplies the sizing it ends on.

## TL;DR

- Work in a fixed order: **users → requests a day → peak requests/s → tokens/s in and out → GPUs → dollars a month**. Size the fleet from the **peak**, and price an API from the **average**.
- Brightpost: 200,000 requests a day is **2.3 requests/s on average and 12 at the peak**, which is **26,400 prompt tokens/s and 3,000 output tokens/s**. That needs **2 replicas plus 1 spare**.
- Tokens are priced separately: output costs more than input, and **cached input** costs a fraction of normal input. Cost per request = input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price.
- A self-hosted fleet is a **fixed bill that steps up in whole GPUs**; an API bill is a **line through zero**. At illustrative prices the lines cross at **about 21,000 requests a day** against a mid-size model, and **never** against a model a tenth of the price.
- Utilisation near 100 % makes queues explode. Plan for about **60 %** at the peak, and spend the headroom on bursts and failures.
- Every number here is **illustrative** until a load test replaces it. Say so in the interview.

## The idea in plain English

Planning a restaurant is the same problem. You count the diners a night, find the Saturday rush, work out how many plates leave the kitchen a minute, and then decide how many cooks to hire. You do not hire for the average Tuesday, or the Saturday rush leaves people waiting an hour. You also do not hire for the busiest night of the year, or the cooks stand idle all winter. Renting a table at someone else's restaurant (an API) costs you per plate and nothing on quiet nights. Hiring your own cooks (a GPU fleet) costs the same every night, and wins only when the room is full often enough.

## Core concepts

### From users to tokens per second

Four multiplications take you from the product to the hardware.

1. **Users to requests.** Daily users × requests per user. Brightpost assumes 50,000 customers a day with 4 requests each: 200,000 requests a day.
2. **Requests to a rate.** Divide by 86,400 seconds: 200,000 ÷ 86,400 = 2.3 requests/s.
3. **Average to peak.** Multiply by a peak factor, the busiest hour against the day's mean. Brightpost uses 5 (a sale), giving 11.6, written 12. The factor is a measurement from your own traffic, not a constant.
4. **Requests to tokens.** Multiply the rate by tokens per request. A request has two sizes: 2,200 input tokens (instructions, five retrieved passages, the question) and 250 output tokens. At 12 requests/s that is 26,400 input and 3,000 output tokens a second.

The LLM-specific change is the unit: you count **tokens**, not requests, because a request with a 20,000-token prompt costs ten times the compute of a 2,000-token one.

### API pricing: input, output and cached tokens

A provider charges per million tokens, with separate prices for three kinds:

- **Input tokens** are the prompt. **Output tokens** are the answer, and cost several times more per token, because each one needs its own pass through the model.
- **Cached input tokens** are a prompt prefix the provider has seen recently. As of November 2026, Anthropic bills a cache read at 0.1× the base input price on most models (0.05× on some newer ones) and a cache write at 1.25× for a 5-minute entry or 2× for a one-hour entry. OpenAI bills cached reads at 0.1× on its newer models, with a 30-minute default lifetime and a 1,024-token minimum prefix.
- **Batch** requests, which the provider runs within 24 hours, cost 50 % of list at the three large providers (see [Batch vs Real-time Inference](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/batch-vs-realtime-inference)).

The formula never changes: cost per request = input tokens × input price ÷ 1,000,000 + output tokens × output price ÷ 1,000,000. The figure applies it to a different, smaller workload (50,000 requests a day, 2,000 tokens in, 400 out, and prices of \$5 and \$20 per million), so you can see the formula and a routed mix side by side. Its prices, like every price on this page, are illustrative.

Brightpost's 200-token instruction block is below OpenAI's 1,024-token minimum, so it earns nothing from caching today. If the stable prefix grew to 1,100 tokens (instructions plus tool definitions), cache reads at 0.1× would cut one request from \$0.0069 to \$0.0049, which is 29 % off. [Prompt Caching](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/prompt-caching) covers hit rates and what breaks a prefix.

### GPU throughput and how many GPUs you need

A GPU has two limits, and an LLM request uses them in turn. **Prefill** reads the whole prompt in one pass and is limited by arithmetic. **Decode** makes one token at a time and is limited by memory bandwidth ([GPU Performance for LLMs](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/gpu-performance-for-llms) explains why). Brightpost's prompts are 90 % of its tokens, so prefill sets the fleet size. Memory is the second check: the weights plus the KV cache of every answer in flight must fit.

Three numbers turn tokens into GPUs: the compute per prompt, the sustained speed of a GPU, and the share of that speed you allow yourself to use. The funnel figure below shows the sizing. **Throughput is measured, not read off a datasheet**: a datasheet gives peak FLOP/s, and real engines sustain a fraction of it. Measure tokens a second at your real prompt lengths, at the latency target, on your engine.

## Deep dive

### API versus self-hosting break-even

An API bill grows with every token. A fleet's bill is fixed until the traffic needs another GPU. The two cross where the fixed bill stops being the expensive one.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/serving/capacity-break-even"
  stepper
  caption="The API bill is a straight line; the fleet is a staircase, one step per replica. Against a mid-size API model they cross at about 20,870 requests a day. Against a model a tenth of the price they do not cross at all. All prices are illustrative."
/>

**How to read it.** Green lines are API bills, the teal staircase is the self-hosted fleet. The fleet starts at 2 replicas because one failure must not stop the service, so its smallest bill is 2 × 720 hours × \$3 = \$4,320 a month. It steps to 3 replicas at 117,818 requests a day, using the sizing rule from the funnel (peak 5×, 60 % target, one spare).

The numbers, with illustrative prices of \$2 in and \$10 out per million tokens for the mid-size model, a tenth of that for the small one, and \$3 per GPU-hour:

- Mid-size API: 2,200 × \$2 ÷ 1,000,000 + 250 × \$10 ÷ 1,000,000 = \$0.0069 a request, so 200,000 × 30 × \$0.0069 = **\$41,400** a month.
- Small API: a tenth, \$0.00069 a request, **\$4,140** a month.
- Fleet: 3 replicas × 720 h × \$3 = **\$6,480** a month.
- Break-even against the mid-size API: \$4,320 ÷ (\$0.0069 × 30) = **20,870 requests a day**, about a tenth of Brightpost's volume.

Read the two comparisons with care. Against a mid-size model, self-hosting wins easily, but only if the 8B model you host answers as well as the mid-size model would, and a golden-set run settles that, not hope. Against a small API model of similar quality the fleet **does not win at this size**: the API bill follows the average (2.3 requests/s) while the fleet is sized for the peak (12). Self-hosting also adds costs the line leaves out: an engineer on call, monitoring, idle spares, cold starts and weights storage. Past break-even, the case for self-hosting is then usually control (data stays in your network, a fine-tuned model, a latency guarantee), not the bill alone. See [Choosing a Model](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/choosing-a-model) and [Cost & Latency Optimization](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/cost-latency-optimization) for the levers on each side.

GPU prices in the sources I checked in November 2026 ranged from about \$2 to \$4 an hour on specialist clouds to well above \$6 on the large clouds, and they moved month to month. The \$3 here is a round number; replace it with your quote.

### Peak load, headroom and queues

Size from the busiest hour, not the day. The same traffic feeds two bills.

  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={2} title="The average sets one bill, the peak the other">
    A pay-per-token API charges for what you send, so the bill follows the average: 200,000 × 30 × \$0.0069 = \$41,400 a month. A fleet is paid for every hour of the month at the size the peak needs: 3 × 720 × \$3 = \$6,480.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={3} title="A fleet sized for the peak is idle most of the day">
    On average the fleet carries 2.3 ÷ 12 = 19 % of the rate it is built for, the same 19 % as on the batch page. Decode keeps GPUs busy too, so a utilisation graph will read differently, but the shape holds.
  </StepCard>
</StepCards>

**Why 60 % and not 95 %.** Queues grow faster than load. For the simplest queue (random arrivals, one server), the average wait in the queue is ρ ÷ (1 − ρ) service times, where ρ is utilisation. A real batching engine is not that queue, but the shape is the same:

| Utilisation ρ | Average wait, in service times |
| --- | --- |
| 50 % | 1 |
| 60 % | 1.5 |
| 80 % | 4 |
| 90 % | 9 |
| 95 % | 19 |

Going from 60 % to 90 % busy multiplies the wait by six. That is why the sizing keeps prefill under 60 % and adds a spare. Little's law gives the second check: answers in flight = arrival rate × time in system, here 12 × 1.5 s = 18, which sets the KV-cache memory.

**Growth is not linear.** Doubling traffic to 24 requests/s at the peak does not double the fleet: 1.76 × 2 = 3.52 rounds up to 4, plus the spare, gives 5 replicas, not 6. The spare is a fixed cost, so small fleets pay proportionally more for resilience. Plan a quarter ahead, and let [autoscaling](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/load-balancing-autoscaling) cover the spikes a plan cannot predict, remembering that a new replica takes minutes to load its weights.

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  Sizing from the average. At 2.3 requests/s one replica looks enough, and on the first sale day the queue grows past the 80 ms budget, then past the 400 ms first-token target, then clients time out and retry, which adds load. Sizing from the peak without a cap on prompt length fails the same way: one customer pasting a 30,000-token email costs the compute of 14 normal requests.
</Callout>

### A worked estimate

Put the chain together for Brightpost, the way you would at the whiteboard. This follows the funnel above and ends in a monthly bill.

1. **Requests.** 50,000 users × 4 = 200,000 a day; 2.3 requests/s average; × 5 = 12 at the peak.
2. **Tokens.** 12 × 2,200 = 26,400 input tokens/s and 12 × 250 = 3,000 output tokens/s. Per day: 440 M input and 50 M output. Per 30-day month: 13.2 B input and 1.5 B output.
3. **GPUs.** 35.2 TFLOP per prompt ÷ 400 TFLOP/s = 88 ms; 12 × 0.088 = 1.06; ÷ 0.6 = 1.76, so 2; plus 1 spare = 3 replicas. Memory check: 18 answers in flight need about 5.4 GiB of KV cache beside 16 GB of weights, which fits an 80 GB GPU.
4. **API bill.** 13,200 M × \$2 = \$26,400 for input, plus 1,500 M × \$10 = \$15,000 for output: **\$41,400** a month.
5. **Fleet bill.** 3 × 720 × \$3 = **\$6,480** a month, or about \$1.08 per 1,000 requests (\$6,480 ÷ 6,000 thousand requests).
6. **Decision.** If the 8B model passes the golden set, self-hosting costs 16 % of the mid-size API. If a small hosted model also passes, its \$4,140 beats the fleet at this size and, at these prices, at every volume on the plot. Write down which of those two comparisons you are making.

The whole estimate fits in a few lines, which is the point of an interview answer. A sketch (the constants are Brightpost's illustrative ones from this page):

```python
import math
users, per_user = 50_000, 4
req_day = users * per_user                      # 200,000
peak = req_day / 86_400 * 5                     # 11.6 requests/s
gpu_s = 2 * 8e9 * 2_200 / 1e12 / 400            # 0.088 s of prefill
need = peak * gpu_s / 0.6                       # 1.70 replicas
replicas = max(2, math.ceil(need) + 1)          # 3
fleet = replicas * 720 * 3                      # $6,480 a month
api = req_day * 30 * (2200*2 + 250*10) / 1e6    # $41,400 a month
```

All the throughput, price and peak figures are assumptions. A load test at real prompt lengths, with the real engine and the latency target, replaces each one.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pay-per-token API | No idle cost, no ops, scales with the average, newest models | Bill grows with volume; rate limits; data leaves your network | Low or spiky volume; early product; quality needs the best models |
| Self-hosted fleet, fixed size | Fixed, predictable bill; control of data, model and latency | Idle at the average; needs ops and a spare; steps up in whole GPUs | High, steady volume; data cannot leave; a fine-tuned model |
| Self-hosted with autoscaling | Pays closer to the average | Cold starts take minutes; spikes still need warm capacity | Daily cycles with a clear low |
| Reserved or committed GPUs | Lower hourly rate than on-demand | A commitment, paid whether used or not | A baseline you are sure of |
| Hybrid: fleet for base load, API for spikes | Fleet stays busy; the API absorbs the peak | Two paths to test, route and monitor | Steady base with a sharp peak |
| Batch API for offline work | Half the list price | Results within hours, not seconds | Nightly jobs, evaluations, re-indexing |

## Real-world examples

All as of November 2026, from each provider's own documentation:

- **Anthropic prompt caching**: cache reads cost 0.1× the base input price on most models (0.05× on some), writes 1.25× for 5 minutes or 2× for one hour, and the default lifetime is 5 minutes, refreshed on every hit.
- **OpenAI prompt caching**: on, by default, for supported models; reads at 0.1× on its newer models, a 30-minute default lifetime and a 1,024-token minimum prefix.
- **Message batches at the large providers**: Anthropic, OpenAI and Google each price their batch API at 50 % of standard, with a 24-hour window.
- **NVIDIA H100 SXM**: 989 dense BF16 TFLOP/s and 3.35 TB/s of memory bandwidth on the datasheet; real engines sustain a fraction of the peak, and this page assumes 40 %.
- **vLLM**: its paper reports 2 to 4 times the throughput of earlier systems at the same latency, which is why a capacity plan quotes measured tokens a second for your engine, not the hardware's peak.

## Common pitfalls

- Sizing from the average request rate, so the first busy hour exposes the queue.
- Counting requests, not tokens: one long prompt costs as much compute as many short ones.
- Forgetting that output tokens cost several times more than input tokens, and that retries and tool loops multiply both.
- Comparing a self-hosted 8B model with an API model of a different quality, and calling the gap a saving.
- Leaving out the fixed costs of self-hosting: spares, on-call time, monitoring, idle nights and cold starts.
- Quoting a datasheet speed as throughput, and trusting a price or a peak factor that nobody measured.

## How to answer in an interview

1. Ask for the inputs: users or requests a day, tokens per request (input and output), the peak factor, the latency target and any data constraint. State the ones you assume.
2. Walk the chain out loud: requests a day, average and peak requests/s, tokens/s in and out, GPUs, dollars a month. Show the arithmetic at each step.
3. Size from the peak and the prompt (prefill), cap utilisation near 60 %, add a spare, and check memory for the answers in flight.
4. Compare the API line with the fleet staircase, say where they cross and what quality assumption that needs, and name the costs the staircase leaves out.
5. Finish with how you would check it: a load test at real prompt lengths, the golden set on the cheaper model, and an alert on queue depth.

**What interviewers listen for:** tokens not requests, peak not average, a spare in the count, a break-even with its quality caveat, and "I would measure that".

**Red flags:** a single GPU count with no working, an average used to size a fleet, a datasheet figure presented as throughput, or "self-hosting is cheaper" with no volume.
