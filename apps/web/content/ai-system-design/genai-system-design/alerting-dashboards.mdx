An LLM system can fail without a single error: the answers get worse, or the first token arrives late, and every status code stays 200.

- Interviewers ask about alerting to see whether you know what to put on a dashboard, what is worth waking a person for, and how a quality problem becomes an alert.
- The general method is in [SLOs, Alerting and On-Call](/cloud/operations/slos-alerting-and-on-call); this page covers what is different for LLMs.

## Overview

- **Four dashboard rows, in order:** traffic, latency, quality, cost. Brightpost's quality row is a **graded sample** (90 % pass, floor 85 %), because no status code marks a wrong answer.
- **Page on symptoms, ticket on causes.** A symptom is something a customer feels (pass rate under the floor, TTFT p95 over **400 ms**). A cause (cache hit rate, stale index) sits on the dashboard to explain the page.
- **Alert on the burn rate, not the instant rate.** A burn rate of 1 spends the budget in exactly 30 days. Two windows per alert catch both a spike and a slow leak.
- **Quality alerts are statistical.** A pass rate on 200 graded answers carries about **±4 points**, so alert on a drop larger than the noise, with a minimum sample.
- **Cost belongs on the same board.** An incident that spares latency can still double the token bill.
- **Every page links a runbook and the last deploy.** The bundle and prompt version are on the dashboard.

## Mental model

- Think of the instrument panel in a car. The speedometer, the fuel gauge and the temperature light are not the same kind of signal.
- A flashing "engine hot" light means pull over now.
- A low tyre-pressure reading means book a garage visit this week.
- The skill is deciding which gauge gets a light that wakes you.
- An LLM application has one more gauge, the one for "is it right?", and it only moves if someone checks a sample of answers.

## Core concepts

1. **The four rows.** Traffic, latency, quality and cost: one question each, read top to bottom. They extend Google's four golden signals (latency, traffic, errors, saturation) with the two an LLM adds, quality and spend.
2. **Percentiles, not means.** TTFT and full-answer time are skewed. Plot p50, p95 and p99; see [Latency & Token Monitoring](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/latency-token-monitoring).
3. **A quality signal.** Grade a sample of live answers with a judge or a rubric and plot the pass rate. See [Online Production Evaluation](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/online-production-evaluation).
4. **Symptom and cause.** Page on symptoms; keep causes (cache hit rate, queue depth, index age, provider 429s) as aids to debugging and as tickets.
5. **SLO and error budget.** An SLO of 99 % over 30 days allows 1 % bad requests. The budget is that allowance, spent as failures happen.
6. **Burn rate.** The share of requests failing now, divided by the share the SLO allows. Burn rate 1 spends the budget in exactly the window.
7. **Multiple windows.** A long window proves it is not a blip; a short window proves it is still happening. Google's SRE Workbook suggests a short window of one twelfth of the long one.
8. **Drift alerts.** Slow changes in inputs or the provider's model need their own alert; see [Drift Detection](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/drift-detection).
9. **Routing and runbooks.** Each alert names an owner, a runbook and a dashboard filtered to the failing route, model and bundle version.
10. **Anomaly detection.** Seasonal baselines (weekday against weekend, sale days) cut false alarms from static thresholds, but they need to know about launches and model changes.

## Technical mechanics

### Page on symptoms, ticket on causes

- A symptom is something a customer feels. A cause is something wrong inside the system that may never reach them.
- Google's SRE book says monitoring should answer "what's broken, and why?", and recommends paging on the first and using the second as an aid to debugging.

  caption="Pass rate, TTFT and budget burn page a person. Stale index, a falling cache hit rate and provider 429s open tickets. The cause readings are illustrative."

**How to read it:**

- Step 1 lists three symptoms. Step 2 lists three causes, each of which can produce one of them.
- Step 3 draws the dashed arrows: a stale index gives stale answers, a falling cache hit rate gives a slow first token, and rising 429s give failed requests.
- Only the symptom row wakes someone. The cause row sits on the dashboard so the person who wakes up sees the explanation next to the page.

A cause can still deserve a page when it is "very definite, very imminent" in the SRE book's words, such as a certificate expiring in an hour. Most are tickets.

### Burn rate and two windows

The burn rate is the share of requests failing now divided by the share the SLO allows. With a 99 % SLO the allowance is 1 %, so 14.4 % failing is a burn rate of 14.4.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/cloud/error-budget-burn"
  stepper
  caption="A 30-day error budget: a fast burn pages within minutes, a slow leak opens a ticket, and an empty budget freezes risky changes."
/>

**How to read it:**

- This is the cloud track's figure for a checkout SLO; an LLM answer SLO works the same way.
- The dashed line is burn rate 1. Step 2 is a fast burn that pages.
- Step 3 is a slow leak that opens a ticket. Step 4 shows the budget at zero and the rule that follows: risky changes freeze.
- The cloud page also draws the [alert path](/cloud/operations/slos-alerting-and-on-call) from the SLI to the paging tool.

**The numbers:**

- The SRE Workbook's example for a 99.9 % SLO pages at a burn rate of 14.4 over 1 hour (short window 5 minutes) and 6 over 6 hours (30 minutes), and opens a ticket at 1 over 3 days (6 hours).
- The budget used is burn rate × window ÷ 30 days: 14.4 × 1 h ÷ 720 h = 2 %, 6 × 6 h ÷ 720 h = 5 %, and 1 × 72 h ÷ 720 h = 10 %.
- For Brightpost's illustrative 99 % answer SLO, 30 days is about 6,000,000 requests and the budget is 60,000 bad ones.
- At 14.4 times the safe rate, about 1,200 bad requests in an hour use 2 % of it.

### Alerting on quality

- A graded sample is noisy, so a quality alert needs three guards.
- Use a minimum sample (at least 100 graded answers in the window), a drop larger than the noise (the 95 % range on 200 answers is about ±4 points, so a fall from 90 % to 87 % is not yet news), and a second window as with burn rates.
- Route a slow drift to a daytime ticket. Page only when the pass rate is under the floor with the minimum sample behind it.
- A flaky judge produces false alarms, so keep the judge version fixed and log it with the score.

### Worked example: a slow first token on Tuesday

1. **11:40, the page.** The latency row crosses a TTFT p95 of 400 ms. It has held for 10 minutes, so the symptom alert pages the on-call engineer. The alert links the runbook and the dashboard filtered to bundle 42.
2. **11:45, the cause.** The engineer opens the cause panel under the latency row. Cache hit rate fell from 62 % to 31 %, and input cost per request rose with it. The cache row is a cause; it had only opened a ticket at 11:15, which nobody had read yet.
3. **11:50, the link.** The last deploy moved a timestamp to the top of the prompt. A prefix cache only helps when the start of the prompt repeats, so every request missed. Readings are illustrative.
4. **11:55, the fix.** The engineer rolls back to bundle 41 using [Rollback & Blue-Green Deployment](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/rollback-blue-green-deployment). TTFT p95 returns under 400 ms within the short window and the alert clears.
5. **Afterwards.** A ticket moves the timestamp below the cached prefix and adds a regression test; the cause alert on cache hit rate is kept as a ticket.

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  A dashboard with latency and errors but no quality row. Everything is green while the index goes stale and answers turn wrong. Add a graded sample, and alert on it, before the first incident teaches you.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Static threshold | Easy to read and explain | Noisy; misses slow burns | Hard limits such as a certificate expiry |
| Multi-window burn rate | Pages by customer impact; catches spikes and leaks | Needs an SLO and tuning | Any user-facing LLM route |
| Anomaly detection | Handles daily and weekly cycles | Needs baselines that know launches and model changes | Traffic and cost with strong seasonality |
| Quality alert on graded samples | Sees silent failures | Judge cost; sampling noise | Every production assistant, with a minimum sample |
| Page on causes | Early warning | Alert fatigue; many causes never hurt anyone | Definite, imminent causes only |
| Ticket on slow burns | Protects sleep | Slower reaction | Slow leaks, drift, cost creep |

## Production systems

- **Google SRE.** The Workbook's multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alerts (14.4, 6 and 1) are the standard pattern that many monitoring products implement.
- **Grafana, Datadog and cloud monitors.** Dashboards and routing on existing on-call tools; LLM teams add a quality panel fed by sampled evaluations.
- **Cloud SLO objects.** Amazon CloudWatch Application Signals and Google Cloud Monitoring can alarm on an SLO's burn rate directly; see the cloud page.
- **LLM observability tools.** Trace-based products show token counts, latency and evaluation scores on one board; see [LLMOps & Observability](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/llmops-observability).

## Failure modes & pitfalls

- Alerting on every machine metric and training the team to mute the channel.
- No quality row, so silent degradation never alerts.
- Paging on one graded answer, or on a sample too small to mean anything.
- Averaging latency instead of using p95 and p99.
- Changing the judge or the sample without recording it, so the pass rate jumps for no reason.
- Alerts with no runbook, no owner and no link to the last deploy.

## Interview framework

1. Start with the four rows and what each answers, and add the row an LLM needs: quality.
2. Give the rule: page on symptoms, ticket on causes, and name examples of each.
3. Explain burn rate with one number: burn rate 1 spends a 30-day budget in 30 days; 14.4 over an hour spends 2 %.
4. Handle quality honestly: a graded sample, a minimum size, an interval, a fixed judge.
5. Close with the runbook: the alert links the dashboard, the bundle version and the rollback step.

**What interviewers listen for:** a quality signal that is not a status code, multi-window burn rates, the symptom and cause split, and noise awareness on small samples.

**Red flags:** "alert when error rate is over 1 %" as the only rule, paging on every metric, and a judge score treated as exact.
