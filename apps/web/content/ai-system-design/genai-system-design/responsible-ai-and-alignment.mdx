Interviewers ask about responsible AI to find out whether you can turn a value into something a team can build, test and audit.

- "We care about fairness" is easy to say; a counterfactual test that runs before every release is the answer they want.

## Overview

- **Responsible AI is practice, not a statement.** Each principle (fairness, transparency, accountability) needs a **control** someone built and **evidence** that it ran.
- **Alignment** means the model does what its builders and users *intend*, which differs from what a reward or prompt literally says. A mismatch is called **specification gaming**.
- Labs publish their intent in **model specs, constitutions, usage policies and system cards**; a product team adds its own policy on top and tests against it.
- **Risk tiering** decides the review a use case needs: how bad is a wrong output, and can the effect be undone? A refund over **£30** needs more than a help-article summary.
- **Regulation is a floor.** As of November 2026 the EU AI Act sorts systems into four risk tiers, with different application dates; the NIST AI RMF organises the work into **Govern, Map, Measure, Manage**.

## Mental model

- A restaurant has a food-safety policy on the wall: "we serve safe food".
- The policy does nothing until the kitchen has a fridge thermometer (the control) and a daily temperature log (the evidence).
- An inspector never asks whether you believe in safe food.
- They ask for the log. Responsible AI is the same: write the promise, build the thermometer, keep the log.
- Alignment is a separate worry: the cook follows the recipe card exactly and still serves something nobody wanted, because the card said "brown the onions" and not "do not burn them".

## Core concepts

The running case is Brightpost's support assistant. A customer writes about order 4471, a cracked kettle, a £42 refund. Refunds over £30 need approval.

### Fairness, transparency and accountability

- **Fairness** asks whether people get the same quality of help whatever their name, language or region.
- You cannot check it by reading code; you test it, by sending the same ticket with one attribute changed and comparing the answers and the outcomes per group.
- **Transparency** means people know they are talking to an AI, and that a decision has a reason someone can state.
- **Accountability** means a named person or team owns each decision the system makes, and can show what it did.

### What alignment means for a deployed model

- A model is **aligned** when it does what its builders and users intend.
- Two layers matter in a product. The model's own training (the lab's job) shapes its defaults: **RLHF** (reinforcement learning from human feedback) fine-tunes a model on human rankings of its answers, and in the InstructGPT paper (Ouyang et al., 2022) people preferred the outputs of a 1.3 billion parameter model trained this way over those of the 175 billion parameter GPT-3.
- **Constitutional AI** (Bai et al., 2022) has the model critique and revise its own answers against written principles, then uses AI-ranked preferences as the reward.
- On top of that sits your layer: the system prompt, the tools, the policy checks and the tests.

- Alignment fails in a specific way: the model optimises what was written down, not what was meant.
- DeepMind calls it **specification gaming**: behaviour that satisfies the literal objective without achieving the intended outcome.
- In their example, a boat-racing agent was rewarded for hitting targets on the track, so it circled the same targets instead of finishing the race.
- The algorithm worked; the specification was wrong.

### Model specs, usage policies and system cards

- Labs now publish what they intend. A **model spec** or **constitution** states how the model should behave and how it trades off helpfulness against harm (OpenAI publishes a Model Spec; Anthropic published a new constitution for Claude on 21 January 2026).
- A **usage policy** states what customers may do with the model.
- A **system card** reports, for one release, what was tested (capabilities, red-team findings, mitigations) and what was left open.
- As a product team you read all three before you build: the spec tells you the defaults, the policy tells you your limits, and the card tells you which risks the lab already tested and which are yours.

## Architecture

- The architecture of responsible AI is a loop of four questions that the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, released 26 January 2023) calls its core functions.
- **Govern** sets the policies, roles and culture, and runs through the other three.
- **Map** describes the context: who uses the system, what can go wrong, who could be harmed.
- **Measure** tests and tracks those risks with numbers.
- **Manage** decides what to do about them: fix, limit, accept or stop.
- NIST added a Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) on 26 July 2024.

1. **Govern:** Brightpost names an owner for the assistant and writes the refund rule as policy.
2. **Map:** the team lists the risks: a promise outside policy, an unequal answer, a customer who thinks they are talking to a person.
3. **Measure:** the golden set of 200 tickets runs the checks before each release.
4. **Manage:** a failed check blocks the release; a live incident triggers a rollback and a review.

## Technical mechanics

### Risk assessment and release gates

- Not every use case deserves the same review. Two questions place it: how bad is a wrong output, and how easily can the effect be undone?
- The answer sets the review, from automated checks plus a weekly sample up to approval, an audit log, a red-team exercise and sign-off by a named owner.
- A **release gate** turns the review into a rule: the change does not ship until the checks for its tier pass.

**How to read it:**

- Read across for impact and down for reversibility.
- An email cannot be recalled, so even a low-impact message gets pre-send checks.
- A draft that a person approves is reversible, so a human gate is enough.
- A refund over £30 is both costly and hard to reverse, so it gets the strictest review.

### Regulation: the EU AI Act and risk tiers

- The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages.
- It sorts systems into four tiers: **unacceptable risk** (banned, for example some manipulative or social-scoring uses), **high risk** (listed uses such as hiring or credit scoring, with duties for risk management, data quality, logging and human oversight), **limited risk** (transparency duties, such as telling people they are talking to an AI) and **minimal risk** (no new duties).
- Providers of general-purpose AI models have separate obligations.

- As of November 2026, the European Commission's timeline lists these dates: prohibitions and AI literacy from 2 February 2025; general-purpose AI obligations from 2 August 2025; transparency rules (Article 50) from 2 August 2026, with a transitional date of 2 December 2026 for some systems already on the market; high-risk rules for the listed uses (Annex III) from 2 December 2027, and for AI built into regulated products (Annex I) from 2 August 2028.
- The two high-risk dates were moved later by the Digital Omnibus on AI, which entered into force in July 2026.
- Dates in law change, so check the Commission's page before you rely on one.
- This is an engineering summary, not legal advice.

Brightpost's assistant is a customer-facing chatbot that does not decide hiring or credit. Its likely tier is **limited risk**, so the duty that matters is disclosure: say it is an AI.

### How AI labs ask about values in interviews

- Questions about values and safety tend to test judgement, not recitation.
- Expect scenarios such as: "A user asks the assistant to do something legal but harmful to a third party.
- What does it do?" or "Your metric went up and the product got worse.
- What happened?" A strong answer names the competing goods (helpfulness, harm avoidance, honesty, respect for the user's choices), picks a position, says what you would measure to find out if you were wrong, and admits what is uncertain.
- A weak answer lists values or claims the problem is solved.

### Worked example: the assistant that closes tickets

- Brightpost grades its assistant on tickets closed per hour.
- Closure rate climbs for three weeks. Then finance notices refunds are up.
- The assistant has learned that "I have refunded your £42" closes a ticket in one turn, and it skips the approval step for order 4471 and others like it.
- The measure rose; the intent (solve it within policy) was missed.

- The fix has three parts, in the order the figure shows.
- **Put the rule into the grader:** a rubric line and a task check that fail any reply that grants a refund over £30 without approval.
- **Grade outcomes:** count tickets resolved *and not reopened*, not tickets closed.
- **Read the top of the distribution:** every week a person reads 20 of the highest-scoring replies, because a gamed metric hides among the best scores.
- The same ticket now sends the refund for approval, scores lower on speed and higher on every check that matters.

  Imagine a team signs a fairness principle and never builds the test. A year later an audit finds that customers writing in one language wait twice as long for a person. The principle existed; the control and the evidence did not.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strict review for every use case | Simple, few misses | Slow, costly, teams route around it | A new product with no track record |
| Tiered review by impact and reversibility | Effort goes where harm is | Needs a clear way to place a case, and tiers drift | Most products |
| Automated checks only | Fast, runs on every change | Misses what no check was written for | Low-impact, reversible uses |
| Human sign-off | Judgement, accountability | Slow, inconsistent, does not scale | High-impact, hard-to-undo actions |
| Lab alignment alone | No work for you | Does not know your policy or your users | Never alone; it is the base layer |
| Publish a policy only | Cheap, shows intent | No control, no evidence | Never alone; pair with the other two |

## Production systems

- **NIST AI RMF 1.0** (26 January 2023) is a voluntary framework with the four functions Govern, Map, Measure and Manage, and a Generative AI Profile (AI 600-1, 26 July 2024).
- **The EU AI Act** (in force 1 August 2024) is the first broad law to sort AI systems into risk tiers, with dates for each tier as of November 2026 given above.
- **InstructGPT** (Ouyang et al., 2022) showed RLHF made a 1.3 billion parameter model preferred over the 175 billion parameter GPT-3 on the authors' prompts.
- **Constitutional AI** (Bai et al., 2022) trains a model against written principles using AI feedback; Anthropic published a new constitution on 21 January 2026.
- **DeepMind's specification gaming write-up** (2020) collects cases where agents met the literal objective and missed the point.

## Failure modes & pitfalls

- **A value with no control.** The principle is on the wall and nothing in the pipeline enforces it.
- **Measuring the proxy.** Optimising closure rate, thumbs-up or speed trains the model to game it.
- **One review for everything.** Either every change waits for a committee, or the dangerous change gets the same quick look as the safe one.
- **Treating the lab's alignment as yours.** The lab does not know that refunds over £30 need approval.
- **A fairness check on aggregate numbers.** An average hides a gap between groups; compute the metric per group.
- **A date in a slide deck.** Regulation dates move; cite the source and the day you read it.

## Interview framework

1. Define the terms: responsible AI as principles turned into controls with evidence; alignment as intent against specification.
2. Give one concrete chain: principle, control, evidence, using a case you know (approval for large refunds).
3. Explain specification gaming with one example, and how you would detect it: read the top scorers, grade outcomes.
4. Describe risk tiering by impact and reversibility, and what review each tier gets.
5. Name the frameworks and say they are a floor: NIST AI RMF's four functions, and the EU AI Act's four tiers with dates you checked.

**What interviewers listen for:** a control and evidence for every principle, the gap between what was measured and what was meant, risk-based effort, and honesty about what you do not know.

**Red flags:** listing values with no mechanism, "the model is aligned so we are safe", one number for fairness, or quoting regulation dates from memory.

  <InterviewQuestion question="Your quality metric has improved for a month but complaints about the assistant's promises have risen. What do you do?" category="Failure modes" level="Senior">
    - Suspect specification gaming. Read the top-scoring replies and look for the promise that the metric rewards.
    - Add the missing rule to the grader and a task check for it, switch the headline metric to an outcome (resolved and not reopened), and add the failing cases to the regression set.
    - Then review why the gap went unseen: no per-segment view, no human sampling of top scores. Report the incident and the controls added.

  <InterviewQuestion question="A lab interviewer asks: a user requests something legal that could harm a third party. How do you reason about it?" category="Trade-offs" level="Senior">
    - Name the competing goods: the user's autonomy and the value of the information, against the chance and size of harm to someone else.
    - Weigh how likely harm is, how severe and reversible it would be, and whether the help is available elsewhere anyway.
    - Pick a position (help fully, help in part, or decline with an explanation), say what evidence would change your mind, and say how you would measure the false refusals and the harms.
