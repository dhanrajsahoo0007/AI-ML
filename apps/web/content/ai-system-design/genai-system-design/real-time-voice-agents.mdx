On a phone call, a pause of one second feels like a dropped line.

- Interviewers ask about voice agents because the hard parts are not the models: they are the budget of milliseconds, knowing when the caller has finished, and stopping cleanly when the caller cuts in.

## Overview

- A **cascaded** agent chains a turn detector, speech recognition, a language model and speech synthesis; a **speech-to-speech** model takes audio in and gives audio out in one step.
- The **latency budget** is the time from the caller's last word to the first sound of the reply. An illustrative tuned budget is **1,000 ms**: 300 wait + 80 recognition + 400 model + 120 synthesis + 100 network.
- **The wait for the end of the turn is the biggest lever.** One live API documents a default of about **800 ms**, which alone would take the same budget to **1,500 ms**.
- **Barge-in** means the caller can interrupt. On speech, stop playing, cancel the reply, and keep only what the caller heard in the history.
- **Transport** decides the floor: WebRTC for browsers and apps, a server WebSocket for back-end links, and SIP or the phone network for calls (narrowband, 8 kHz audio).
- Judge a voice agent on **task success, the gap before the reply, barge-in behaviour and digits heard correctly**, not on one WER number.

## Mental model

- A good phone operator listens while the caller talks, starts forming the answer before the sentence ends, and stops mid-word if the caller says "no, wait".
- She also fills a gap ("let me look that up") rather than leave silence.
- A voice agent copies those habits with parts: an ear that notices when speech starts and stops, a typist that writes it down, a brain that decides what to say, and a mouth that speaks the first clause while the rest is still being written.
- Each part adds delay. The engineering is a budget: how many milliseconds each part may use, and which tricks (streaming, filler, early start) hide the rest.

## Core concepts

### A cascaded pipeline versus speech-to-speech models

- A **cascade** is voice activity detection (VAD), speech recognition (ASR), a language model (LLM), then speech synthesis (TTS), as in the figure below.
- Every hop is text, so you can log it, test it, run guardrails on it and swap any stage.
- The cost is that the delays add and the voice loses tone at the text hop.
- A **speech-to-speech** model takes audio and returns audio in one model, so there is no wait between stages and it hears how something was said.
- It is harder to inspect, and you accept the vendor's model and voices.
- Both can call tools. For the speech stages in detail, see [Speech: ASR & TTS](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/speech-asr-and-tts).

### The latency budget, stage by stage

- Define the number first. **Response gap** is the time from the end of the caller's speech to the first sound of the reply.
- It has five parts: the **wait** that decides the turn is over, the **recognition tail** (the last audio still to be read), the model's **time to first token** (see [Streaming and Time to First Token](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/streaming-and-time-to-first-token)), the **first audio chunk** from synthesis, and **telephony and network**.
- Streaming at every stage is what makes the budget possible: recognition works while the caller talks, and synthesis starts on the first clause, not the whole answer.

### Turn detection and barge-in

- **Turn detection** (endpointing) decides the caller has finished.
- The simple method waits for silence. A short wait cuts the caller off mid-thought; a long one adds delay to every turn.
- A smarter detector reads the words as well: "I want a refund for order four four" is not finished, "…seven one" is.
- **Barge-in** is the reverse problem: the agent is speaking, and the caller starts.
- The system must notice speech over its own voice, stop playing within about 100 ms, cancel the model and speech synthesis, and trim the history to what was heard.
- Echo cancellation matters here, or the agent hears itself and interrupts itself.

### Transport: WebRTC and telephony

- **WebRTC** carries audio between a browser or app and a server over UDP with jitter buffers, the Opus codec and echo cancellation built in; it is the usual choice for client apps.
- A **WebSocket** is a reliable stream and suits server-to-server links, at the cost of waiting for lost packets.
- **Telephony** (the public phone network, reached by SIP trunks) uses 8 kHz narrowband audio, so recognition meets a thinner signal than the models were often trained on.
- For general real-time connections on the cloud, see [Real-Time Connections on the Cloud](/cloud/networking/realtime-on-the-cloud).

## Technical mechanics

### The latency budget in numbers

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/multimodal/voice-latency-budget"
  caption="Two budgets on one scale. All stage times are illustrative except the model's 400 ms; the 800 ms wait is one live API's documented default (read 16 November 2026)."
/>

**How to read it:**

- Each row is a stage, in order; the dashed line is when the reply starts.
- The top chart waits 300 ms for the end of the turn: 300 + 80 + 400 + 120 + 100 = 1,000 ms. The bottom chart changes only the wait to 800 ms, Gemini Live's documented server default (its guide recommends 500 to 800 ms): 800 + 80 + 400 + 120 + 100 = 1,500 ms. The model's 400 ms is Brightpost's time-to-first-token target from the text assistant.

**The levers, biggest first:**

- Shorten the wait with a detector that reads meaning (OpenAI's `semantic_vad` has an `eagerness` setting for this; its `server_vad` has `silence_duration_ms`).
- Start synthesis on the first clause. Keep recognition streaming so the tail is short.
- Cut network hops: put the agent near the telephony edge.
- Cache or pre-warm the model prompt (see [Prompt Caching](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/prompt-caching)).

### Turn-taking on a timeline

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/multimodal/turn-taking"
  stepper
  caption="One call on a time axis. The caller stops at 2.4 s, the agent starts at 3.4 s, the caller cuts in at 4.6 s and the agent stops at 4.7 s. Times are illustrative."
/>

**How to read it:**

- Violet is audio, lime is the turn detector, green is the model thinking.
- The agent's reply is 2.6 s long but the caller heard 4.7 − 3.4 = 1.3 s of it.
- The other 1.3 s is shown pale: it was generated and never played.
- If the history keeps the whole reply, the model will later "remember" saying things the caller never heard.
- OpenAI's Realtime guide describes this repair: on `input_audio_buffer.speech_started`, stop playback, drop queued audio, and send `conversation.item.truncate` with the milliseconds actually played (over WebRTC and SIP the server truncates for you).

```jsonc
// server to you: the caller started speaking over the agent (other fields omitted)
{ "type": "input_audio_buffer.speech_started" }
// you to server, after stopping local playback: keep only the 1,300 ms the caller heard
{ "type": "conversation.item.truncate", "item_id": "…", "content_index": 0, "audio_end_ms": 1300 }
```

This follows the OpenAI Realtime events as documented on 16 November 2026. Gemini Live reports an `interrupted` flag instead, discards pending function calls, and keeps only the content already sent to the client.

### Tool calls in the middle of a conversation

- A tool call takes time the caller can hear. If `get_order` takes 300 ms (illustrative), the reply starts at 1,000 + 300 = 1,300 ms unless the agent speaks first.
- Three habits help. Say a **filler** ("one moment, I'll look that up") while the call runs.
- Make read tools fast and give them **short timeouts**.
- Keep write tools behind a check: a £42 refund is over Brightpost's £30 limit, so the agent says it has sent the request for approval and does not promise the money.
- If the caller interrupts during a tool call, cancel what has not started and decide what happens to a write already sent (Gemini discards pending calls and reports their IDs).
- See [Tool Use & MCP](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/tool-use-and-mcp).

### Handling noise, silence and errors

- Plan for the bad minute. **Noise** triggers false barge-in; raise the VAD threshold (`threshold` in OpenAI's `server_vad`) and use noise suppression before detection.
- **Silence** from the caller needs a prompt after a few seconds ("are you still there?") and a hang-up rule.
- **Errors** (a failed tool, a recogniser timeout, a dropped link) need a spoken fallback that never leaves dead air, and a hand-off to a person.
- Live APIs also have session limits: Gemini Live's audio-only sessions are limited to 15 minutes and audio-plus-video to 2 minutes unless you use its session management, so a long call must reconnect and carry context over.

### Evaluating voice agents

- Score the call, not one part. **Task success:** did the caller get the refund routed correctly?
- **Response gap:** p50 and p95 of the time from end of speech to first audio, measured at the phone, not inside your server.
- **Barge-in:** time to stop after speech starts, and the false-interruption rate on noisy calls.
- **Recognition of what matters:** digit-string and entity accuracy beside WER.
- **Voice quality:** human MOS ratings. **Safety:** the guardrails of [Guardrails & Safety](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/guardrails-and-safety) on every spoken turn.
- Test with scripted caller audio (accents, noise, interruptions) and replay real calls with consent.
- The full design is in the [Voice Agent for a Call Centre case study](/genai-system-design/case-studies/voice-agent-call-centre).

### Worked example: Brightpost's phone line

- A caller says "refund for order 4471" (2.4 s).
- The turn detector waits 300 ms, calls the end of the turn and passes the text to the model: 2.7 s on the timeline above.
- The model calls `get_order`; the agent says "one moment" so the 300 ms tool time is covered.
- The model replies "I've sent your £42 refund for approval", which needs a person because it is over £30.
- The first clause reaches synthesis at once, and the caller hears it at 3.4 s, 1,000 ms after she stopped (the budget).
- At 4.6 s she cuts in with "wait, it was the other kettle".
- The turn detector sends a stop to the buffer and a cancel to the model within 100 ms. The caller heard 1.3 s of a 2.6 s reply, and the history keeps only that. The model then handles the new turn.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cascade (ASR, LLM, TTS) | Text trail, guardrails, swappable parts, any LLM | Delays add; tone lost at the text hop | Regulated or tool-heavy calls |
| Speech-to-speech model | One hop, hears tone and pauses, natural flow | Harder to inspect and guard; vendor's voices | Open conversation, coaching, companion apps |
| Silence-based turn end | Simple, predictable | Cuts off slow talkers or adds delay | Short commands |
| Semantic turn end | Fewer cut-ins, shorter waits | More compute; can still misjudge | Natural conversation |
| WebRTC | Low delay, echo handling | More complex to run | Browsers and apps |
| Telephony (SIP) | Reaches every phone | 8 kHz audio; carrier hops | Contact centres |

## Production systems

- **OpenAI Realtime API (docs read 16 November 2026).** Speech-to-speech over WebRTC in the browser or WebSocket on the server; 24 kHz mono PCM; `server_vad` and `semantic_vad`; truncation events for interruptions; function calling.
- **Gemini Live API (docs read 16 November 2026).** A stateful WebSocket; 16-bit PCM at 16 kHz in and 24 kHz out; automatic VAD with a configurable silence wait; interruptions cancel generation; native audio models with a 128k-token context.
- **Brightpost's phone line (this page's running example).** A cascade, chosen for its text trail: each turn is logged and checked against the refund policy before it is spoken.
- **Contact-centre agents.** Most phone deployments use a cascade behind SIP trunks, because transcripts, redaction and audit are required.

## Failure modes & pitfalls

- Measuring latency inside the server, not at the caller's phone. Carrier and network time sit outside your server.
- A fixed 800 ms silence wait copied from a default. It spends most of the budget before any model runs.
- Playing the whole reply after an interruption, or keeping the whole reply in history, so the model repeats things the caller never heard.
- Letting noise or the agent's own voice trigger barge-in. Use echo cancellation and tune the threshold.
- Promising an outcome a tool has not confirmed (a refund that still needs approval).
- Silence with no fallback when a tool or a model call fails.

## Interview framework

1. Pick a design: cascade or speech-to-speech, and say why (text trail, control, tools, voice).
2. Give the latency budget with numbers per stage, and name the largest item: the turn wait.
3. Explain turn detection and barge-in: how the end of turn is found, how playback stops, what goes in the history.
4. Cover transport (WebRTC, WebSocket, SIP) and the 8 kHz telephone reality.
5. Close with evaluation: response gap p95, task success, barge-in time, digit accuracy.

**What interviewers listen for:** a numeric budget, the end-of-turn wait as the main lever, truncating the history to what was heard, and a fallback for every failure.

**Red flags:** "just use a big model", no latency numbers, no plan for interruptions, and measuring only WER.
