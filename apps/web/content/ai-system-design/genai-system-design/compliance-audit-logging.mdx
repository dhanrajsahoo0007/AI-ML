When an AI system pays a refund, a regulator, an auditor or an angry customer will ask who decided, on what basis and with which version of the system. Interviewers ask about audit logging to see whether you can build the record that answers those questions without turning the log into a new privacy problem.

## TL;DR

- An **audit record** answers six questions: **who** asked, **what** was done, **when**, **which model and prompt version** ran, what the **decision** and its rule were, and what **evidence** it used (by reference). Replaying a decision needs all six.
- Store records **append-only**, with each record carrying the **hash of the one before** (a hash chain). An edit or a deletion then shows up. Nobody, engineers included, can write or delete by hand.
- **Redact before you store.** Records hold ids and hashes, not the customer's words or card number. A store you cannot edit cannot be cleaned up later.
- **Retention is a decision with two clocks:** raw detail (prompts, traces) expires fast; the small record lives longer. Brightpost's 30 days and 13 months are illustrative choices.
- **Rules differ in who they bind.** As of November 2026, the EU AI Act logging duty (Art. 12) binds **high-risk** systems, with Annex III duties applying from **2 December 2027**. GDPR binds Brightpost today because tickets hold personal data. SOC 2 and ISO/IEC 42001 are voluntary.

## The idea in plain English

A bank keeps a ledger in ink. Each page is numbered, each entry is signed, and a wrong entry is never scratched out; it is corrected by a new entry that refers to the old one. The ledger says who moved the money and when, not the customer's life story. An auditor can read it and a clerk cannot rewrite it. An audit log for an AI system is that ledger: the assistant adds entries, but nobody changes the entries already in the book.

## Core concepts

1. **Audit log versus debug log.** A trace helps an engineer find a slow step (see [Tracing & Logging](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/tracing-logging)). An audit log helps someone outside the team reconstruct a decision. It is smaller, stricter and kept longer.
2. **The decision record.** Six fields: who, what, when, model and prompt version, decision and rule, evidence by reference. One record per decision, including denials and holds.
3. **Versions are evidence.** "Model X" is not enough. Record the registry version and the prompt version, so the answer to "what was running that day?" is a lookup (see [Model Registry & Governance](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/model-registry-governance)).
4. **Tamper evidence.** An append-only store plus a **hash chain** (each record stores the SHA-256 hash of its predecessor) makes a change or a gap detectable.
5. **Data minimisation.** GDPR Art. 5(1)(c) limits data to what the purpose needs. Log ids, categories and hashes; redact personal data first (see [Data Privacy & PII Handling](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/data-privacy-pii-handling)).
6. **Retention.** Keep each category as long as its purpose needs and no longer (GDPR Art. 5(1)(e)). Where a rule sets a floor, such as six months for AI Act logs of high-risk systems, that is the minimum.
7. **Who may read.** A named role reads through an API that is itself logged. Reading an audit log is an audited act.
8. **Explanations.** For a generative system, log the policy version, the retrieved passage ids and the tool trace, not a claimed chain of reasoning. Those show what the system had, which is what a reviewer can check.

## Architecture

The record is the unit; the pipeline keeps it honest. First the record, drawn for Brightpost's refund request.

1. **Who, what, when:** the user and role, the tool call with its arguments, and a timestamp with a request id that joins every record of this request.
2. **Model and prompt version:** the identifiers from the registry, so the run can be reproduced.
3. **Decision:** the outcome and the rule behind it. Here the refund is held, because £42 is over the £30 limit.
4. **Evidence and chain:** the ids of the passages used and a hash of the input, plus the hash of the previous record.

This is the same record as JSON. It follows `audit-record`; the ids, model name and prompt version are illustrative.

```json

  "ts": "2026-11-16T10:08:41Z",

  "actor": { "user": "1847", "role": "customer", "channel": "chat" },
```

## Deep dive

### The pipeline

Services emit an event for every decision. A redaction step removes personal data and computes the hash, a store appends the record, a retention job removes raw detail by age, and a read API gives a named role read-only access.

<StepCards>
  <StepCard n={1} title="Services emit an event per decision">
    The chat app sends who, what and when. The tool gateway sends the decision and the rule. Neither waits for the store: events go through a queue, so logging never blocks a customer's answer.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={2} title="Redact, then hash">
    Card numbers and email addresses are replaced by placeholders, and the record is hashed with the previous record's hash. Redacting first matters because the next step cannot be undone.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={3} title="Append to a store nothing can edit">
    A write-once store, such as object storage with Object Lock in compliance mode, which stops any user, root included, from overwriting or deleting a version until its retention date.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={4} title="Retention drops raw detail by age">
    The full prompt and trace expire after 30 days; the small record stays 13 months. Both ages are Brightpost's illustrative choices.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={5} title="Only an auditor role can read">
    Engineers can write events but cannot write to or delete from the store. The read API is read-only and logs every query.
  </StepCard>
</StepCards>

**The numbers (illustrative).** Assume 1 KB per record. Brightpost handles 200,000 requests a day, so one record per request is 200,000 × 1 KB = 200 MB a day. Over 13 months (about 395 days) that is 200 MB × 395 = 79,000 MB, about **79 GB**. That is small enough to keep in a cheap, locked, searchable store. Raw prompts at about 10 KB each would be ten times larger, which is why they expire first.

### Which rule asks for which control

Rules differ in who they bind and what they ask. This figure is dated: checked on 16 November 2026.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/safety/audit-regulation-map"
  caption="Only GDPR binds Brightpost’s assistant today. The AI Act’s logging duty targets high-risk systems; SOC 2 and ISO 42001 are voluntary. Checked 16 November 2026."
/>

- **EU AI Act** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689). Art. 12(1) says high-risk AI systems must technically allow automatic recording of events over their lifetime. Art. 12(2) ties the logs to three purposes: spotting risk situations, post-market monitoring and monitoring operation. Art. 19(1) (providers) and Art. 26(6) (deployers) say to keep logs under their control for at least six months, unless other law says otherwise. The Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since 27 July 2026, moved the Annex III high-risk duties to 2 December 2027 (Annex I products: 2 August 2028). A customer-support assistant is not an Annex III use, so Art. 12 does not bind Brightpost on its own. It is still a sound design target.
- **GDPR.** Art. 30(1) requires a record of processing activities: purposes, categories of data and recipients, planned erasure time limits and a description of security measures. Art. 22 limits decisions based solely on automated processing with legal or similarly significant effects, and where it allows them, the person has at least the right to human intervention, to give their view and to contest the decision. Art. 5(1)(c), (e) and (f) set minimisation, storage limitation and security for the log itself. Art. 30(5) exempts organisations under 250 people only in narrow cases.
- **SOC 2** (AICPA 2017 Trust Services Criteria, with revised points of focus from 2022). It does not name AI. The criteria that logging serves: CC6.1 (logical access), CC7.2 (monitoring system components for anomalies) and CC8.1 (authorised, recorded changes). A report is something a buyer asks for.
- **ISO/IEC 42001:2023.** A certifiable management system for AI. Annex A has a control on recording event logs while an AI system is in use (A.6.2.8, per published control lists; the standard's text is paid). Certification is voluntary.

**What the log cannot do.** No rule prescribes one interpretability technique. For a generative system, do not claim the log explains why the model wrote a sentence. It shows the inputs, versions, rules and tool calls, which is what an auditor can verify.

### Logs hold personal data too

A user id, an order id and an amount are personal data once they link to a person. The log is therefore under GDPR itself. Use a pseudonymous id with the mapping in a separate, access-controlled table. Keep content out. Limit who reads. When a customer asks for erasure, the common approach is to delete the mapping, so the record can no longer be tied to them, and to keep the record only where a lawful basis allows it. Ask counsel which records fall under that basis; this is a legal decision, not an engineering one.

### Worked example: the refund, replayed

1. **10:08:41.** The model asks for a refund of £42 on order 4471. The gateway holds it. Record `aud-0001`: actor user 1847, decision `held_for_approval`, rule `refund_limit_30`, model `assistant-small`, prompt `support-v14`.
2. **10:20.** A support agent approves. Record `aud-0002`: actor the agent, same `request_id`, decision `approved`, `prev_hash` equal to the hash of `aud-0001`. The refund `R-2291` is created.
3. **Three months later.** An auditor asks who approved `R-2291` and which prompt was live. One query on `request_id` returns both records. The registry resolves `support-v14` to its evaluation report.
4. **A tampering test.** Someone deletes `aud-0001`. Record `aud-0002` now points to a hash that no record has, and the chain check fails. The gap is the evidence.
5. **A customer asks for erasure.** The pseudonym mapping for user 1847 is deleted. The 30-day raw prompts have already expired. The records stay without a way to name the customer, if counsel confirms the basis.

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  A team logs the full prompt for debugging into the same write-once store. A customer's ticket holds an email address and a card number, now locked for 13 months in a store that cannot be edited. The log became the biggest leak in the system, and erasure is impossible. Redact before the store, keep raw detail in a separate store with a short life.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Log full prompts and outputs | Best for debugging and replay | Large, holds personal data, hard to erase | Short-lived store with tight access, never the audit store |
| Log references and hashes | Small, safer, enough to verify | Cannot read the original text from the record | The audit record, always |
| Append-only with hash chain | Edits and deletions are detectable | Cannot fix a mistake in place; needs a correction record | Any record an outsider may rely on |
| Long retention | Covers late audits and disputes | Cost, and more personal data held | Records; where a rule sets a floor |
| Short retention | Less data to leak | Evidence may be gone when asked | Raw detail |
| Voluntary certification (SOC 2, ISO 42001) | Buyers trust it; forces discipline | Cost and effort; says nothing about model quality | When customers require it |

## Real-world examples

- **AWS CloudTrail** log file integrity validation hashes each log file with SHA-256, and delivers hourly digest files signed with RSA, each holding the signature of the previous digest. That is a hash chain in production.
- **Amazon S3 Object Lock** gives write-once-read-many storage; in compliance mode no user, including the account root user, can overwrite or delete a locked version until the retention date.
- **The EU AI Act** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) makes automatic event logging a design requirement for high-risk systems, kept at least six months.
- **Brightpost** logs each tool decision as a 1 KB record with ids and hashes, keeps the record 13 months and raw prompts 30 days (illustrative).

## Common pitfalls

- **Logging content into the audit store.** The log becomes a new sensitive datastore that cannot be cleaned.
- **Missing versions.** A record without the model and prompt version cannot answer "what was running?".
- **Only logging successes.** Denials and holds are the interesting records.
- **Letting engineers write by hand.** A log that an administrator can edit is not evidence.
- **Assuming a rule applies.** Check whether the system is high-risk, whether personal data is involved, and who asks for a report.
- **Treating the log as an explanation.** It records inputs and rules, not the model's reasoning.

## How to answer in an interview

1. Say what the audit log is for: reconstructing a decision for someone outside the team.
2. List the six fields and show one record, with versions and references instead of content.
3. Describe the pipeline: redact, hash and chain, append-only store, retention by category, read-only access by role.
4. Map rules to controls without overclaiming: AI Act Art. 12 for high-risk systems, GDPR Arts. 30 and 22, SOC 2 CC6.1, CC7.2, CC8.1, ISO/IEC 42001. Date what you say.
5. Close with the tension: the log is personal data, so minimise, pseudonymise and plan for erasure.

**What interviewers listen for:** the difference between audit and debug logs, tamper evidence, redaction before storage, and a precise view of which rule binds which system.

**Red flags:** "log everything", one retention period for all data, claiming the AI Act requires logging for every chatbot, or saying the log explains the model.
