GenAI interviews test the same small set of ideas from different angles: how a model turns tokens into text, how you ground it in data, how you measure it, and what it costs to run. Learn those once, in order, and every round becomes a variation on them.

## Overview

- **Four roles, one core.** AI engineers, ML engineers, research engineers and forward-deployed engineers all get asked about tokens, context windows, retrieval, evaluation, latency and cost. The roles differ in how deep each round goes, not in which topics exist.
- **The hub has an order.** Start with the [map of the whole stack](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/anatomy-of-an-llm-application), then [LLM Foundations](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/transformer-architecture), then RAG and agents, then the groups that run a system in production; [Architecture](#architecture) draws it. The rest is depth for your role.
- **Practice beats reading.** Each page ends with interview questions, and the [question bank](/interviews) files more by topic. Answer them aloud, then compare with the written answer.
- **One running example.** This page follows one design prompt through every section: "Design an assistant that answers customer questions from our help-centre articles." It is a common shape of GenAI design question, and it touches almost every group in the hub.

<KeyIdea>Study the hub in dependency order (the map, then foundations, then RAG and agents, then evaluation, serving and safety), and practise every topic as an answer spoken aloud, because every role and round asks the same core from a different angle.</KeyIdea>

## Mental model

Think of the hub as a driving licence course rather than a library.

- **Theory before the road.** You learn what the pedals do (tokens, attention, decoding) before you drive in traffic. Skipping it works until a question such as why long prompts get slower.
- **Manoeuvres are the building blocks.** Parking, roundabouts and motorway merges map to retrieval, tool calling and prompting. Each one is practised on its own, then combined.
- **The test is a route you have not seen.** The examiner does not ask you to recite the manual. They give you a route (a design prompt) and watch how you handle it: checking mirrors (clarifying requirements), signalling (saying your plan out loud) and recovering from a mistake (a failure mode the interviewer raises).

## Core concepts

### Roles: AI engineer, ML engineer, research engineer and forward-deployed engineer

Job titles vary between companies, so read the job description, not the title. The patterns below are common, not universal.

- **AI engineer:** Builds products on top of foundation models, through APIs or open-weight models. The work is prompts, [retrieval](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/rag-fundamentals), [tool calling](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/tool-use-and-mcp), [agents](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/agent-architectures), [evaluation](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/llm-evaluation-fundamentals) and shipping. Latent Space's 2023 essay "The Rise of the AI Engineer" described the role as software engineers building on foundation models, with product-specific data and evals as their concern. Interviews lean on system design and practical builds.
- **ML engineer:** Trains, adapts and serves models. On a GenAI team that means [fine-tuning](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/fine-tuning-llms), [serving and inference](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/llm-serving-inference), [GPU performance](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/gpu-performance-for-llms), data pipelines and [release pipelines](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/cicd-ct-pipelines). Interviews add ML fundamentals and classic [ML system design](/ml-system-design) to the GenAI core.
- **Research engineer:** Turns research ideas into working training and evaluation code. Interviews go deepest on [LLM Foundations](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/transformer-architecture): attention, [scaling laws](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/scaling-laws-and-training), [post-training](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/reinforcement-preference-learning), and often a paper discussion or an implementation from scratch.
- **Forward-deployed engineer:** Works with a customer to scope, build and debug a GenAI system on the customer's data. Interviews test scoping an ambiguous problem, building fast, and explaining trade-offs to a non-specialist. [RAG](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/rag-fundamentals), [access control](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/rag-access-control-multi-tenancy) and [data privacy](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/data-privacy-pii-handling) come up because customer data is messy and sensitive.

### Round types and what each one tests

- **Coding:** Data structures and algorithms, or a practical task such as parsing a file, calling an API or writing a small service. It tests correct, readable code under time pressure. The [DSA section](/dsa) covers the algorithm side.
- **LLM and ML concepts:** Short questions on how things work: why attention scales with the square of the sequence length, what temperature does, how LoRA cuts trainable parameters. It tests understanding of the mechanism, not recall of names.
- **GenAI system design:** An open prompt such as the help-centre assistant. It tests requirements, architecture, retrieval, model choice, evaluation, cost and failure handling, in about 45 to 60 minutes.
- **Practical build or debugging:** A take-home or live session where you build a small RAG pipeline or agent, or fix a broken one. It tests whether you can make something work and measure it.
- **Paper or research discussion:** Mostly for research roles. You explain a paper's idea, its experiments and its weaknesses, or propose a follow-up.
- **Customer or scoping case:** Mostly for forward-deployed roles. The interviewer plays a customer with a vague goal; you ask questions, set a pilot scope and explain risks in plain language.
- **Behavioural and project deep dive:** Stories about past work, and one project explored in detail: what you built, what broke, what you measured. The [behavioural section](/behavioral) covers story framing.

### Topics every candidate needs

Whatever the role, these come up in almost every loop. Each links to the page that teaches it.

- **Tokens and context:** [tokenization](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/tokenization) and [context windows](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/context-windows-and-in-context-learning), because cost, latency and truncation are all counted in tokens.
- **How a transformer works:** the [transformer block](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/transformer-architecture) and [self-attention](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/self-attention-explained), at the level of "what each part does and why it costs what it costs".
- **Decoding:** [temperature, top-p and constrained decoding](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/decoding-and-sampling), and [structured output](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/structured-output-generation).
- **Grounding:** the [RAG pipeline](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/rag-fundamentals) end to end, and [when to prompt, retrieve or fine-tune](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/prompt-vs-rag-vs-fine-tuning).
- **Tools and agents:** [function calling](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/tool-use-and-mcp) and the difference between a workflow and an [agent](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/agent-architectures).
- **Evaluation:** [offline evals, golden sets and LLM judges](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/llm-evaluation-fundamentals).
- **Serving, cost and latency:** [time to first token](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/streaming-and-time-to-first-token), the KV cache, and [cost per request](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/cost-latency-optimization).
- **Safety:** [guardrails](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/guardrails-and-safety) and [prompt injection](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/prompt-injection-and-jailbreaks).

### How to study one page

1. Read the Overview and the one-sentence key idea. Say it back in your own words.
2. Step through each figure, then close the page and redraw the main one from memory. A diagram you can redraw is a diagram you can use on a whiteboard.
3. Read Trade-offs and Failure modes twice. Interviewers spend most of their follow-ups there.
4. Answer the interview questions aloud before opening the answers. Note the ones you missed and come back to them two days later.

## Trade-offs

Every study plan gives something up. Choose on purpose.

## Failure modes & pitfalls

- **Reading without recall.** Ten pages read is not ten topics known. If you cannot redraw the figure or answer the questions aloud, you have not learned the page yet.
- **Starting with agents.** Agents are the newest topic, but an agent answer falls apart when the interviewer asks how retrieval or evaluation works underneath.
- **Architecture without numbers.** Boxes and arrows with no token counts, latency budget or cost per request read as a junior answer. Every design page in the hub has the arithmetic; practise it.
- **Evaluation as the last slide.** Saying "and then we would evaluate it" in the final minute signals you have not shipped. Name the eval set and the metric when you name the component.
- **Memorising one company's loop.** Formats change and differ between teams. Prepare for round types, then confirm your loop with the recruiter.
- **Skipping the behavioural round.** Every role in the table meets it. Prepare two or three project stories with what you measured, using the [behavioural section](/behavioral).

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  Over-specialising early. A candidate who reads one group in full during week 1 often meets a generalist question on day one and has nothing to say. Do the one-week core first, then go deep.
</Callout>

## Interview framework

Most GenAI design rounds reward the same structure. Here it is applied to the help-centre assistant, with an **illustrative** budget for a 45-minute round; the timeline shows where the minutes go.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/llm/roadmap-answer-timeline"
  caption="Almost half the round is the deep dive, so the first ten minutes exist to earn it: numbers first, then the simplest system that could work (illustrative minutes)."
/>

**How to read it:** the strip is the round, each segment as long as its minutes; the rows say what to cover. The interviewer steers the deep dive, so go deepest where they lean in. You can follow only if the requirements and the baseline are already on the board.

**The numbers:** 5 + 5 + 20 + 10 + 5 = 45 minutes; the deep dive is 20 ÷ 45 = 44 % of the round.

1. **Clarify (about 5 minutes).** Who asks, questions a day, languages, how fresh the articles must be, what a wrong answer costs, and the latency target. Write the numbers down.
2. **Baseline (about 5 minutes).** Retrieve the top articles, put them in a prompt, answer with citations. Say why you start there and not with an agent or fine-tuning.
3. **Deep dive (about 20 minutes).** Chunking and embeddings, hybrid search and re-ranking, the prompt and its context budget, [model choice](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/choosing-a-model), and the hand-off to a person when retrieval finds nothing.
4. **Evaluate and operate (about 10 minutes).** A golden set of real questions, retrieval and answer metrics, an LLM judge checked against people, then latency, cost per request, caching, guardrails and monitoring.
5. **Wrap up (about 5 minutes).** Restate the design in three sentences, name the biggest risk and what you would build next.

- **What interviewers listen for:** Requirements as numbers, a baseline before complexity, evaluation tied to each component, cost and latency arithmetic, and failure modes named before they are asked.
- **Red flags:** Starting with a framework name, no eval plan, ignoring the "no answer found" case, and agents where a fixed pipeline would do.

  <InterviewQuestion question="How would you structure a 45-minute GenAI system design answer?" category="Concepts" level="Mid">
    - **Clarify first:** Spend the first few minutes turning the prompt into numbers: users, requests a day, latency target, freshness, languages and the cost of a wrong answer.
    - **Baseline, then depth:** Propose the simplest working system (for a document assistant, retrieve-then-generate with citations), then go deep on two or three parts where the interviewer shows interest.
    - **Evaluate and operate:** Name the eval set and metrics, then latency, cost per request, guardrails and monitoring.
    - **Close:** Restate the design in a few sentences and name the biggest risk. An illustrative split is 5, 5, 20, 10 and 5 minutes.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="You are asked to design an assistant that answers questions from a help centre. What do you ask before drawing anything?" category="Concepts" level="Junior">
    - **Scale and latency:** How many questions a day, at what peak, and how fast the first token must appear.
    - **Content:** How many articles, how often they change, which languages, and whether some are private to certain customers.
    - **Risk:** What a wrong answer costs, and whether the assistant may say "I don't know" and hand off to a person.
    - **Why it matters:** Each answer changes the design: freshness sets the indexing pipeline, privacy adds access control, and the cost of errors sets how strict the evaluation and guardrails must be.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="The interviewer asks why you used RAG instead of fine-tuning. How do you answer?" category="Trade-offs" level="Mid">
    - **Match the method to the gap:** RAG adds knowledge the model lacks and keeps it fresh; fine-tuning changes behaviour, format or style.
    - **Use the prompt's facts:** Help-centre articles change weekly and need citations, so retrieval fits; re-training for every edit would be slow and would not cite sources.
    - **Leave room for both:** Fine-tuning can still help later, for tone or a smaller, cheaper model, once an eval set shows where the baseline falls short.
    - **Show the decision rule:** Prompt first, retrieve for knowledge, fine-tune for behaviour, as in [Prompt vs RAG vs Fine-Tuning](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/prompt-vs-rag-vs-fine-tuning).
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you make evaluation part of a design answer rather than an afterthought?" category="Evaluation" level="Senior">
    - **Attach a metric to each component:** Retrieval gets recall at k on labelled questions; the answer gets faithfulness to the retrieved text and correctness against a golden set.
    - **Say where the data comes from:** Real user questions, sampled by type, with edge cases such as unanswerable questions and other languages.
    - **Calibrate the judge:** If an LLM judge scores answers, check it against human labels before trusting it.
    - **Close the loop:** Run the eval set as a regression gate on every prompt or model change, and sample live traffic for online scoring.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you show depth on a topic you have not used in production?" category="Concepts" level="Mid">
    - **Say so plainly:** Interviewers trust a candidate who marks the edge of their experience more than one who bluffs.
    - **Reason from mechanism:** Explain how it works and derive the trade-off, for example why a longer context raises cost and time to first token.
    - **Bring numbers from a small build:** A weekend RAG pipeline with a 30-question eval gives real latency, cost and failure examples to quote.
    - **Name what you would measure first:** It shows you know how you would find out.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How does your preparation change if the role is ML engineer rather than AI engineer?" category="Trade-offs" level="Senior">
    - **Shared core:** Both need tokens, attention, RAG, evaluation, serving cost and safety at a working level.
    - **ML engineer adds:** ML fundamentals, training and fine-tuning, serving internals (batching, KV cache, quantization, GPU memory) and classic ML system design.
    - **AI engineer adds:** Agents, prompt and context engineering, tool calling and product-level evaluation, with more practical builds.
    - **Plan:** Do the one-week core either way, then spend month 2 on the role's groups, and confirm the round list with the recruiter.
  </InterviewQuestion>
