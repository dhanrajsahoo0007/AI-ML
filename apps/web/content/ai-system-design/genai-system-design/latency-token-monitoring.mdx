An LLM request has no single latency, and no single size: a short question and a pasted order history can differ by 70 times in tokens.

- Interviewers ask about latency and token monitoring to see whether you report percentiles instead of averages, split the wait from the flow, and count every class of token the provider bills.

## Overview

- **Report percentiles, never the mean.** Brightpost's mean is **1,700 ms**, only 200 ms above its **1,500 ms** median. Its **p99 is 4,800 ms**: 2,000 of 200,000 daily requests wait longer. (A computed model with an illustrative spread.)
- **Users feel two numbers.** **Time to first token (TTFT)**, 400 ms for Brightpost, is the blank screen. **Tokens per second (TPS)**, 227, is how fast the text flows. Alert on each separately.
- **Measure with histograms.** You can add histograms across servers; you cannot average percentiles. Compute p95 and p99 from merged buckets.
- **Count four token classes per request:** uncached input, cached input, visible output and reasoning output. Providers report them in different fields, and Anthropic's `input_tokens` leaves the cache out while OpenAI's includes it.
- **Queue depth warns first.** It rises before p99 does. Tie it to a shed or scale action.
- **Write the SLO on what users see:** for example "95 % of answers complete within 3,500 ms and 95 % start within 800 ms", measured per route.

<KeyIdea>Read latency as percentiles from merged histograms, alert on the wait (TTFT) and the flow (TPS) separately, and count all four token classes, because the mean, the total and the answer length each hide where users wait and where money goes.</KeyIdea>

## Mental model

- Picture a restaurant that serves 200 tables a night.
- If the manager asks "how long does the average table wait?" and hears 28 minutes, nothing looks wrong.
- If she asks "how long did the slowest table in a hundred wait?" and hears 80 minutes, she knows a few tables are leaving angry.
- Percentiles are that second question. Splitting TTFT from TPS is the difference between "how long until the bread arrives" and "how long between courses": a guest tolerates a short wait for the bread and a steady pace, and is upset by a long silence or a stall in the middle.

## Core concepts

1. **End-to-end latency.** From the request's arrival to the last token. Measure it at the client edge, since that is what the customer lives with.
2. **Time to first token (TTFT).** Queue wait plus the prefill pass over the prompt. It grows with prompt length. See [Streaming Responses & Time to First Token](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/streaming-and-time-to-first-token).
3. **Time per output token (TPOT) and TPS.** The average gap between tokens after the first, and its inverse. Brightpost: 4.4 ms, so 1,000 ÷ 4.4 ≈ 227 tokens/s.
4. **Inter-token latency (ITL).** One sample per streamed event. Its p99 shows the stalls an average hides.
5. **Percentile.** p95 is the value 95 % of requests beat. p99 is the value 99 % beat. At volume the tail is a crowd: 1 % of 200,000 is 2,000 people.
6. **Histogram.** Counts of requests per latency bucket. Histograms from many servers add up; percentiles do not.
7. **Token metrics.** Input, cached input, output and reasoning tokens per request, route, model and tenant. They drive cost, capacity and rate limits.
8. **Saturation signals.** Queue depth, running sequences, KV-cache use and batch size. They move before latency does.
9. **SLO and error budget.** A target on a user-visible number, and the share of requests allowed to miss it. The practice is the same as for any service; see [SLOs, alerting and on-call](/cloud/operations/slos-alerting-and-on-call).

## Architecture

The first picture is why the second sentence of every dashboard review is "what is p99?".

/>

**How to read it:**

- The curve is the share of requests at each latency.
- Most requests sit near the peak, at about 1,200 ms. A thin tail runs to the right.
- The mean, 1,700 ms, is pulled out a little by that tail but stays close to the median, so a "mean latency" chart looks calm.
- The red points show the cost: **10,000 requests a day** are slower than p95 (3,414 ms) and **2,000** are slower than p99 (4,800 ms).

The pipeline that produces these numbers has five steps:

1. **Every layer timestamps the request**: the gateway, the engine and the client. The span tree on [Tracing & Logging](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/tracing-logging) carries them.
2. **Each server counts latencies into histogram buckets** and exports counters, not percentiles.
3. **A metrics store merges the buckets** across servers and computes p50, p95 and p99 over a window.
4. **Dashboards and alerts read those series**, split by route, model, prompt-length band and tenant. See [Alerting & Dashboards](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/alerting-dashboards).
5. **Token counts from the provider's response** go to a cost and capacity store with the same labels.

## Technical mechanics

### Why the mean misleads

- Latency is skewed: a floor near the fast end and a long tail from long prompts, cold caches, retries and queues.
- Skew puts the mean between the median and the tail, where no real request sits.
- Three habits follow. Plot p50, p95 and p99 together.
- Set the SLO on a high percentile. Slice by prompt length, because a 32,000-token prompt belongs to a different distribution from a 2,200-token one, and mixing them blurs both.

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  Merging percentiles from servers by averaging them is a common error: an average of two p99 values is not the p99 of the combined traffic. Merge histogram buckets, then compute the percentile.
</Callout>

### The two numbers users feel

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/llmops/ttft-tps"
  caption="TTFT is the wait, TPS is the flow. Different things move each, so give each its own alert."
/>

**How to read it:**

- The teal side is the prefill phase: queueing, reading 2,200 prompt tokens and cache hits all change TTFT.
- The green side is decode: batch size, model size and how fast the GPU can read its weights change TPS.
- End-to-end time is TTFT plus the other 249 tokens at 4.4 ms each: 400 + 1,095.6 ≈ 1,500 ms. The warning box matters in interviews: engine **throughput** is all tokens per second across all requests; **TPS** is one reader's speed.
- A bigger batch raises throughput and lowers TPS, so a team that watches only throughput can raise it while customers see slower text.
- The mechanics are on [LLM Inference Engine](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/llm-inference-engine).

### Token accounting

Every response carries a usage object. Read it on every call and keep four classes apart, because they cost different amounts and mean different things.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/llmops/token-accounting"
  caption="One reasoning-on request: 2,200 input tokens (1,000 cached) and 310 output tokens (60 reasoning). Field names checked against both providers' docs on 16 November 2026; prices illustrative."
/>

**How to read it:**

- The left column is the class, the middle two are where each provider reports it, the last is cost.
- Anthropic's `input_tokens` counts only tokens that were not read from or written to the cache, and its docs give total input as `input_tokens` plus `cache_creation_input_tokens` plus `cache_read_input_tokens`.
- OpenAI's `input_tokens` is the total, with `input_tokens_details.cached_tokens` inside it.
- Reasoning tokens are billed as output: Anthropic reports them as `thinking_tokens` and OpenAI as `reasoning_tokens`, both under `output_tokens_details` and each inside the output total.

- At the illustrative price of \$2 in and \$10 out per million tokens, with a cache read at a tenth of the input price, the request costs 1,200 × \$2 ÷ 1 M + 1,000 × \$0.2 ÷ 1 M + 310 × \$10 ÷ 1 M = \$0.0024 + \$0.0002 + \$0.0031 = **\$0.0057**.
- Reasoning is \$0.0006 of it, about a tenth, and it is invisible if you chart only the answer length.

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  Summing input tokens from two providers as if they meant the same thing: Anthropic's `input_tokens` leaves the cache out and OpenAI's includes it. Normalise to one meaning in your store (the OpenTelemetry convention uses the total) and keep the cache and reasoning counts as separate columns.
</Callout>

- The caching mechanics are on [Prompt Caching](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/prompt-caching).

### Worked example: sale day

Brightpost's SLO is "95 % of answers complete within 3,500 ms". On a normal day the model says 4.5 % miss it, inside the 5 % allowance: p95 is 3,414 ms, an 86 ms margin.

1. A sale lifts load and the median rises 20 %, from 1,500 to 1,800 ms. In a log-normal model every percentile scales by the same factor.
2. The **mean** goes from 1,700 to 2,040 ms. On a mean chart this looks like a mild drift.
3. The **p95** goes to 3,414 × 1.2 = 4,097 ms and the **p99** to 4,800 × 1.2 = 5,760 ms.
4. The share over 3,500 ms rises from 4.5 % to about 9.2 %, which is about 18,000 requests a day against an allowance of 10,000. The error budget burns at nearly twice the rate it allows.
5. The queue-depth chart showed the climb an hour earlier, which is the cue to add replicas or shed batch work. See [Load Balancing & Autoscaling](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/load-balancing-autoscaling).

These are modelled numbers from the figure's illustrative spread, not measurements. The pattern is real: a modest shift in the middle is a large shift in the tail.

### SLOs and queue depth

- Write targets on what the user sees, per route: interactive chat on TTFT and on the gap between chunks, batch jobs on completion time and backlog age.
- Alert on the **burn rate** of the error budget, not on a single slow request.
- Keep canary traffic out of the measurement or label it.
- Queue depth, running sequences and KV-cache use are the leading signals; pair each with an action, such as admit fewer long prompts or add a replica, so an alert leads to a playbook.

## Trade-offs

<Compare caption="How to compute percentiles, what to set the SLO on, and how hard to batch: what each choice buys and costs.">

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Percentiles from histograms | Merge across servers; cheap to store | Resolution limited by bucket edges | Default for latency |
| Exact percentiles from traces | Precise, per request | Needs all traces; costly at volume | Spot checks and investigations |
| SLO on end-to-end time | Matches the customer's total wait | Hides whether the wait or the flow is bad | Batch and non-streaming routes |
| SLO on TTFT plus TPS | Matches how streamed answers feel | Two targets to tune | Interactive chat |
| Per-request token metrics | Exact cost and capacity per tenant | More labels, more series | Always, with bounded labels |
| Aggressive batching | Higher throughput, lower cost per token | Worse TPS and tail latency for small requests | Batch work and off-peak traffic |

</Compare>

## Production systems

- **vLLM** reports time to first token and time per output token as histogram metrics, so percentiles can be computed from them; its docs define TPOT as the average gap after the first token.
- **Anthropic's Messages API** returns `usage` with input, cache creation, cache read and output counts on every response, plus rate-limit headers for remaining capacity.
- **OpenAI's Responses API** returns `usage` with `cached_tokens` and `reasoning_tokens` inside the input and output totals.
- **Prometheus histograms** are the usual store; `histogram_quantile` computes a percentile from merged buckets.
- **Used in our case studies:** [Content Moderation System](/genai-system-design/case-studies/content-moderation-system)

## Failure modes & pitfalls

- **Charting the mean.** It stays calm while 2,000 customers a day wait almost five seconds.
- **Averaging percentiles across servers.** Merge buckets instead.
- **One latency for all prompt lengths.** A long prompt has a different distribution; slice by length band.
- **Watching throughput, not TPS.** A bigger batch can raise one and lower the other.
- **Summing input tokens from two providers as if they meant the same thing.** One includes the cache and one does not.
- **Ignoring reasoning tokens.** They are billed as output and take time, but do not show in the answer length.

## Interview framework

1. **Say "percentiles, from histograms".** Name p50, p95 and p99, and give the reason with the tail-as-a-crowd number.
2. **Split TTFT from TPS** and say what moves each one.
3. **List the token classes**: input, cached input, output, reasoning, each from the provider's usage fields.
4. **Define an SLO on user-visible numbers** per route, and alert on burn rate.
5. **Add the leading signals**, queue depth and saturation, each tied to an action.

**What interviewers listen for:** percentiles over means, the histogram-merge point, TTFT against TPS, cached and reasoning tokens counted separately, leading indicators.

**Red flags:** "average latency is fine"; one alert on total latency; no slicing by prompt length; assuming all providers report input tokens alike.

  <InterviewQuestion question="Why are p95 and p99 more important than the average latency for LLM APIs?" category="Concepts" level="Junior">
    - Latency is skewed, so the mean sits near the median and hides the tail.
    - In the Brightpost model the mean is 1,700 ms while the p99 is 4,800 ms, and 1 % of 200,000 daily requests is 2,000 customers.
    - Percentiles expose long prompts, cold caches, retries and queueing.

  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Why can't you average the p99 of ten servers to get the fleet's p99?" category="Concepts" level="Mid">
    - A percentile depends on how many requests each server handled and on the shape of its distribution, so averaging two values gives a number that corresponds to no real set of requests.
    - Export histogram buckets from each server, add the bucket counts, and compute the percentile from the merged histogram.
    - The cost is resolution: the answer is only as fine as the bucket edges, so choose edges around your SLO thresholds.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How would you define an SLO for a streaming chat product and for a batch summarisation job?" category="Trade-offs" level="Mid">
    - For chat, set a TTFT target for a high share of requests, for example 95 % under 800 ms, plus a bound on the gap between streamed chunks so the text never looks frozen.
    - For batch, use completion time per job-size class and backlog age, since nobody watches a single job.
    - Measure per route, leave out or label canary traffic, and alert on the burn rate of the error budget rather than on individual misses.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Throughput on your inference fleet went up 30 % and customers say answers feel slower. What happened?" category="Failure modes" level="Senior">
    - Throughput adds up tokens per second across all requests, while a reader feels the speed of their own stream, which is TPS.
    - A larger batch raises throughput but lowers each request's TPS, and it can lengthen the queue and TTFT for small requests.
    - Check batch-size and queue-depth histograms and the TPS and TTFT percentiles by route.
    - The fix is to cap batch size or concurrency for interactive traffic and move batch work to separate capacity.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What do you record from the provider's usage object, and why?" category="Concepts" level="Mid">
    - Record uncached input, cached input, visible output and reasoning tokens, each with the model, route, tenant and prompt version.
    - They are priced differently, cached input is cheap and reasoning is billed as output, and they drive rate limits as well as cost.
    - Be careful with provider differences: Anthropic's `input_tokens` excludes the cache while OpenAI's includes it, so normalise to one meaning on write.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Which metrics would you look at first when p99 latency jumps after a deploy?" category="Failure modes" level="Mid">
    - Split p99 by prompt-length band to see whether the shape of traffic changed.
    - Then check queue depth and batch-size distributions, the prefill against decode time split, cache hit rate, retry and 429 counts, and any config change such as maximum sequence length or quantisation.
    - Compare the deploy timestamp with the first bad bucket, and look at one slow trace to find the widest span.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="A reasoning model made answers better but the bill rose 40 % with the same traffic. How do you find out why?" category="Trade-offs" level="Senior">
    - Break output tokens into visible and reasoning counts per route.
    - Reasoning tokens are billed as output but never shown, so they raise cost and latency without changing the answer length.
    - For Brightpost's request 60 reasoning tokens add about a tenth to the cost.
    - Then decide per route: keep reasoning for hard cases, cap it or turn it off for simple lookups, and check on the golden set that quality holds.
