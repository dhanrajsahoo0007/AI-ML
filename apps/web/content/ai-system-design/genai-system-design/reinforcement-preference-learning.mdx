Supervised fine-tuning teaches a model what a good answer looks like, but it cannot say which of two plausible answers is better.

- Interviewers ask about preference tuning because a strong answer explains how comparisons become a training signal, why a KL penalty exists, and when DPO is enough.

## Overview

- **Preference tuning learns from comparisons, not from single right answers.** A person or a judge says that answer A is better than answer B for the same prompt, and the model learns the gap.
- **RLHF has three stages:** supervised fine-tuning, a reward model trained on comparisons, then reinforcement learning (usually PPO) against that reward model. InstructGPT's **1.3 B model was preferred to the 175 B GPT-3** by human raters on the authors' prompts (Ouyang et al., 2022).
- **A KL penalty keeps the policy near a reference model.** Without it the policy finds text the reward model over-scores, which is reward hacking.
- **DPO skips the reward model and the sampling loop.** It trains on pairs with one classification loss and a frozen reference (Rafailov et al., 2023). It is simpler, not free: it still needs clean, diverse pairs.
- **Preference data is the real cost.** Pairs come from human ranking, user signals and AI feedback, and each source carries its own noise.
- **When the answer is checkable, use a verifier instead.** Maths, code and rule checks need no learned reward model (see RLVR).

## Mental model

- A restaurant critic cannot write the perfect dish, but can taste two and say which is better.
- Collect enough of those verdicts and a junior chef learns what the critic likes.
- RLHF trains a second person, the reward model, to imitate the critic, then lets the chef cook, taste and adjust against that stand-in.
- The risk is that the chef learns what the stand-in loves (more salt, bigger portions) rather than what the critic loves; the KL penalty is the head chef saying "stay close to how you cooked last week".
- DPO skips the stand-in: the chef studies the critic's pairs directly.

## Core concepts

### 1. Supervised fine-tuning first

- RLHF starts from a model that already follows instructions.
- In InstructGPT, labellers wrote demonstrations and GPT-3 was fine-tuned on them.
- This is the **SFT model**. It is the starting point of the policy and, frozen, the **reference model**.
- See [Fine-Tuning LLMs](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/fine-tuning-llms) and [LoRA, QLoRA & PEFT](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/lora-qlora-and-peft) for how to build it cheaply.

### 2. Comparisons and the reward model

- Labellers rank several answers to one prompt.
- InstructGPT ranked between 4 and 9 answers per prompt, which gives K choose 2 pairs each.
- A **reward model** (6 B in that paper) is trained so the preferred answer scores higher: the loss is −log σ(r(x, y_w) − r(x, y_l)), where y_w won and y_l lost.
- The score is a stand-in for human judgement, and it is only as good as the pairs it saw.

### 3. Policy optimisation with a KL penalty

- The policy writes answers, the reward model scores them, and **PPO** nudges the policy toward higher-scoring text.
- The reward is reduced by β times the log-ratio between the policy and the reference, per token.
- This keeps the policy near text the reward model has seen and limits reward hacking.
- Gao et al. (2022) measured how optimising too hard against a learned reward model hurts the true reward, which is why the penalty and early stopping matter.

### 4. Direct preference optimisation

- DPO reparameterises the RLHF objective so the best policy has a closed form, then trains it with a classification loss on pairs: raise the preferred answer's probability against the reference and lower the rejected one's.
- It needs the pairs and a frozen reference, but no reward model and no sampling during training.
- The derivation assumes a Bradley-Terry model of preferences and a KL-regularised target.
- The paper reports DPO matching or beating PPO-based RLHF on sentiment control, summarisation and single-turn dialogue; teams report mixed results elsewhere, so test it on your task.

### 5. Preference data

The pairs are the product. They come from human ranking, from user signals such as thumbs and agent edits, and from AI feedback.

**How to read it:**

- Three sources feed one store. Agent edits are high quality but few.
- Thumbs are many but noisy, since people click for reasons that are not quality.
- A judge model scales cheaply and brings its own bias.
- The filter drops ties, signals that disagree and answers that break a hard rule.

- **The numbers:** 2,000 + 6,000 + 4,000 = 12,000 pairs; 12,000 − 3,000 = **9,000** kept, so 25 % were dropped. All counts are illustrative.

### 6. AI feedback and constitutions

- Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) replaces human harm labels with a written list of principles.
- In a supervised phase the model critiques and revises its own answers.
- In an RL phase a model judges which of two answers better follows the principles, and those AI preferences train the preference model (RL from AI feedback, RLAIF).
- The paper states that the only human oversight is the list of rules.
- AI feedback is cheap, but it copies the judge's biases, so spot-check it with people.

### 7. Online and offline learning

- **Offline** learning uses frozen logs: safe and repeatable, but the data goes stale and describes an older policy.
- **Online** learning samples from the live policy and asks for fresh comparisons: it tracks drift, but worse intermediate policies can reach users unless a gate stands in front of them (see [Automated Retraining & Fine-Tuning](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/automated-retraining-finetuning)).

## Architecture

The RLHF pipeline has three stages, and each trains something different: the SFT model copies demonstrations, the reward model learns to rank, and the policy learns to score well without drifting.

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  The policy is optimised against a stand-in. If the reward model rewarded length or flattery in its pairs, the policy farms both. Out-of-distribution prompts expose a reward model that never saw them. Audit samples with people, and watch the KL and answer length during training.
</Callout>

## Technical mechanics

### DPO in one pair

DPO needs no reward model, so the whole method fits on one pair. Take Brightpost's refund question and two answers: one sends the £42 refund to an agent, as policy requires; the other says it is done.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/finetune/dpo-pair"
  stepper
  caption="DPO trains on the pair directly. Log-ratios and the loss value are illustrative."
/>

**How to read it:**

- Step 1 is the pair. Step 2 shows how far each answer's probability has moved from the frozen reference: the policy already likes the preferred answer more (+2.0) and the rejected one less (−3.0). Step 3 is the loss.

**The numbers:**

- With β = 0.1 the margin is 0.1 × (2.0 − (−3.0)) = 0.5. σ(0.5) = 0.622, so the loss is −ln 0.622 = **0.474**.
- Before training both log-ratios are 0, the margin is 0 and the loss is −ln 0.5 = 0.693.
- Training lowers the loss by widening the gap.
- A larger β keeps the policy closer to the reference.

```json
{"prompt": "My kettle arrived cracked (order 4471, £42). Can I have my money back?",
 "chosen": "I'm sorry about the kettle. A refund over £30 needs approval, so I have sent your request to an agent.",
 "rejected": "Done, your £42 refund is on its way.",
 "source": "agent_edit"}
```

This is one record in the common prompt, chosen, rejected layout that most DPO tooling reads; field names vary by library.

### RLHF against RLVR

- RLVR keeps the RLHF loop and swaps the learned reward model for a program that checks the answer.
- Where a check exists, such as maths or unit tests, it is exact.
- Where it does not, such as tone or helpfulness, RLHF is still the tool.
- Both can be gamed: the policy exploits the reward model's blind spots in one case and the checker's loopholes in the other.
- See [RLVR & Reasoning Post-Training](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/rlvr-and-reasoning-post-training) for the verifier side.

### PPO or DPO

- PPO fits when you need reward shaping, multi-step environments or a reward that comes from a simulator, and when the team already runs RL infrastructure.
- It brings variance, hyperparameter sensitivity and a heavier loop.
- DPO fits stable updates from offline pair logs with a smaller engineering surface, and it can overfit when pairs are noisy or narrow.
- Training also needs the reference model in memory beside the policy, and the reference digest should be recorded with every checkpoint.
- For how pretraining and scale shape the starting point, see [Scaling Laws & Training](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/scaling-laws-and-training).

### Worked example: fixing Brightpost's refund promises

- The fine-tuned model (5,000 tickets, [Fine-Tuning LLMs](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/fine-tuning-llms)) sometimes says "Done, your refund is on its way" for refunds over £30.
- Supervised data did not rule this out, since it only shows good replies.

1. **Collect pairs.** 12,000 pairs from agent edits, thumbs and a judge, filtered to 9,000 (figure above). Pairs where the rejected answer breaks the refund rule are kept on purpose.
2. **Pick the method.** An offline log and a small team point to DPO with the SFT model as reference.
3. **Train.** The loss on the example pair falls from 0.693 towards 0 as the margin grows; the team watches that the average answer length does not grow.
4. **Evaluate.** On the golden set, 40 of 200 tickets involve refunds (illustrative). Rule breaks fall from 9 of 40 (22.5 %) to 2 of 40 (5 %). With only 40 tickets the difference is thin, so grow the set before relying on it (see [Building Eval Datasets](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/building-eval-datasets)).
5. **Ship behind a gate.** Canary on live traffic before full rollout.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SFT only | Simple; stable | Cannot rank good against better | Format and tone from clean examples |
| RLHF with PPO | Flexible reward; online sampling | Costly loop; reward hacking; tuning | Large budgets; multi-step or shaped rewards |
| DPO | One loss; no reward model; stable | Needs good pairs; can overfit | Offline pair logs; small teams |
| RLAIF (AI feedback) | Cheap, scalable labels | Inherits the judge's bias | Broad principles; spot-check with people |
| RLVR | Exact reward, no reward model | Only for checkable tasks | Maths, code, rule checks |

## Production systems

- **InstructGPT (2022).** SFT, a 6 B reward model trained on rankings of 4 to 9 answers per prompt, then PPO with a KL penalty; its 1.3 B model beat the 175 B GPT-3 in human preference.
- **Constitutional AI (Anthropic, 2022).** Human oversight reduced to a written list of principles; AI preferences replace human harm labels in the RL phase.
- **DPO (2023).** Matches or beats PPO-based RLHF on sentiment, summarisation and single-turn dialogue in its own experiments, with a simple loss.
- **Reward-model over-optimisation (Gao et al., 2022).** Measured how true reward falls when a policy is optimised too hard against a learned reward model, for several reward-model sizes.

## Failure modes & pitfalls

- Training the reward model on narrow pairs, then optimising far past what it knows.
- Letting answers grow longer because longer answers won more pairs.
- Setting β too low, so the policy drifts, or too high, so nothing changes.
- Treating thumbs as quality labels without filtering.
- Using DPO on pairs the reference model never produced and expecting clean gains.
- Skipping the reference's version: a checkpoint is not reproducible without it.

## Interview framework

1. Define preference tuning: learning from "A is better than B" instead of a single right answer.
2. Walk RLHF in three stages and say what each trains and what stays frozen.
3. Explain the KL penalty and the failure it prevents (reward hacking).
4. Explain DPO in two sentences and the one-pair loss; say when you would still use PPO.
5. Finish with the data: where pairs come from, how you filter them and how you evaluate on slices.

**What interviewers listen for:** the reward model as a stand-in, the KL term, DPO's trade-off against PPO, and attention to pair quality.

**Red flags:** "RLHF makes the model smarter", no mention of reward hacking, or choosing PPO or DPO without a reason.

    - Inference rarely needs the reference. Training jobs should log the effective KL and stop early when it blows up.
    - The penalty stops the policy drifting into text the reward model over-scores.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Explain DPO and what it removes from RLHF." category="Concepts" level="Mid">

    - It raises the preferred answer's probability relative to a frozen reference and lowers the rejected one's, scaled by β.
    - It removes the separate reward model and the sampling loop during training, so it is simpler and more stable.
    - It still needs good, diverse pairs and can overfit noisy ones.
