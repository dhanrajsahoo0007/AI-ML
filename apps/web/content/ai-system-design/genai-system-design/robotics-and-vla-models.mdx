A vision-language model can name the kettle on a shelf. A robot has to pick it up. Interviewers ask about vision-language-action (VLA) models because they test whether you see what changes when output is movement: actions are numbers, the loop runs many times a second, and a mistake breaks real things.

## TL;DR

- A **VLA model** takes a camera image and an instruction and returns an **action**: usually a small movement of the arm and a gripper command.
- **Actions can be tokens.** RT-2 and OpenVLA cut each action dimension into **256 bins** and predict the bin numbers like words. OpenVLA's action is **7 tokens**.
- **The loop sets the speed.** OpenVLA (7 B parameters) runs at about **6 Hz** on one RTX 4090, **167 ms** per action. RT-2's 55 B model ran at **1 to 3 Hz**.
- **Chunks and flow matching** go faster: π0 (3 B VLM plus a 300 M action expert) predicts **50 actions at once** and controls robots at up to **50 Hz**.
- **Data is the bottleneck.** OpenVLA trained on **970 k robot episodes**; the internet gives a VLA its language and visual knowledge, not its motor skill.
- **Safety is outside the model.** Speed limits, force limits, stop buttons and tests on real hardware decide whether a VLA may run near people.

## The idea in plain English

Think of teaching someone to pack boxes by phone. You can describe a picture to them (a vision-language model does this), but the packer also needs to move their hands. A VLA model is a packer who has read the whole internet and then spent weeks watching people pack. Every fraction of a second it looks at the scene, reads the instruction, and decides one small hand movement. Then it looks again. It is not planning a whole motion in advance. It is correcting as it goes, which is why the speed of the "look, decide, move" loop matters so much.

## Core concepts

### From vision-language to action

A VLA starts as a vision-language model (see [Vision-Language Models](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/vision-language-models)): image patches become tokens, a language model reads them with the instruction. Fine-tune that model on robot data, where each example is a camera image, an instruction and the action a person or controller took, and it learns to emit actions. RT-2 did this with PaLI-X and PaLM-E, training on web data and robot data together so the robot kept its web knowledge (it could act on objects it had never been shown in robot data). The model never sees "joint angles" as a new kind of thing: it sees more tokens.

### Action representations: tokens, chunks and diffusion policies

Three ways to write an action. **Tokens**: discretise each dimension into bins and let the language model predict bin numbers one at a time (RT-2, OpenVLA). It reuses the language model unchanged, but 7 tokens in a row cost 7 decode steps. **Chunks**: predict several future actions at once (π0 predicts 50), which cuts the number of model calls and smooths motion. **Diffusion or flow policies**: generate a continuous action chunk by refining noise over several steps, so there is no rounding to bins and the output can be smooth and multi-modal (two good ways to grasp). π0 uses flow matching, a close relative of diffusion, in a separate action expert.

### Data: teleoperation, simulation and cross-embodiment sets

Robot data is scarce, because someone or something must produce each episode. **Teleoperation** has a person drive the robot while it records images and actions: slow but high quality. **Simulation** gives unlimited episodes cheaply but must cross the sim-to-real gap (below). **Cross-embodiment sets** pool episodes from many robots: OpenVLA trained on 970 k episodes from the Open X-Embodiment collection, and π0 trained on data from several robot types, so one model can control more than one body. Fine-tuning on a few hundred episodes of your own robot and tasks is the usual last step.

## Deep dive

### How an action becomes tokens

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/multimodal/action-tokenization"
  stepper
  caption="A sideways move of +0.012 m falls in bin 204 of 256. Seven dimensions give seven tokens. Values and ranges are illustrative; the 256 bins are RT-2's and OpenVLA's."
/>

**How to read it.** Step 1 is one continuous number. Step 2 cuts the range into 256 equal bins and finds the one the number falls in: (0.012 + 0.02) ÷ 0.04 × 256 = 204.8, so bin 204. Step 3 repeats for all seven dimensions and writes the bins in a fixed order.

**The numbers.** A bin is 0.04 ÷ 256 = 0.156 mm wide, so the robot receives the bin's centre and is off by at most half a bin, 0.078 mm. That rounding is small next to the arm's own error. OpenVLA sets each range from the 1st to the 99th percentile of the training actions, so a few wild actions do not stretch every bin. The language model has no spare "action words", so RT-2 (for PaLM-E) and OpenVLA overwrite the 256 least-used tokens of the tokenizer with the bin numbers. At 6 actions a second, 7 tokens each, that is 42 tokens a second, written one after another.

### Two designs, side by side

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/multimodal/vla-architecture"
  stepper
  caption="OpenVLA predicts action tokens with a Llama 2 backbone. π0 adds a 300 M-parameter action expert that makes a 50-action chunk by flow matching."
/>

**How to read it.** Both rows start with a vision-language backbone. OpenVLA (top) is a Prismatic VLM: SigLIP and DinoV2 vision encoders, a two-layer MLP projector and Llama 2 7 B; the highlighted block is the only new idea, the action tokens. π0 (bottom) keeps PaliGemma (3 B) and adds a separate action expert of about 300 M parameters, trained from scratch, which reads the backbone's features and produces a chunk of 50 actions by flow matching with 10 steps. The paper reports 73 ms on-board per chunk on an RTX 4090 (14 ms image encoders, 32 ms observation pass, 27 ms flow).

**What the numbers mean.** OpenVLA needs 15 GB in bfloat16 and gives about 6 Hz; it beat the 55 B RT-2-X by 16.5 points of absolute success over 29 tasks while having 7 times fewer parameters. π0 runs the slow backbone once per chunk and executes the actions at 50 Hz for dexterous work: the paper re-plans every 0.5 s (after 25 actions) on its 50 Hz robots. The two designs trade simplicity (tokens) for speed and smoothness (chunks and flow).

### Fast and slow systems: planning and control

A single model at 6 Hz cannot hold a cup steady on its own. Real systems layer loops. A **slow loop** (a VLA or a language model, around 1 to 10 Hz) decides what to do next: which shelf, which grasp. A **fast loop** (a learned or classical controller, often hundreds of hertz or more) tracks the target smoothly and reacts to contact. Action chunks are the bridge: the slow model sends 50 future targets and the fast loop plays them. A language model can also sit above the VLA as a planner that splits "fill order 4471" into pick, check, pack; see [Agent Architectures](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/agent-architectures).

### Sim-to-real transfer

Simulators produce cheap episodes, but images, friction and contact differ from the real world. Three tools shrink the gap: **domain randomisation** (vary textures, lighting, mass and friction in simulation so the real world looks like one more variation), **realistic assets and physics** for the objects that matter, and **real-world fine-tuning** on a small set of real episodes. Test on the real arm before trusting any simulation score: contact with a thin ceramic lid is where simulators are weakest.

### Safety and evaluation on real hardware

A wrong token in text gives a bad sentence; a wrong action can crush a kettle or hit a person. Put safety outside the model: **limits** on speed, force and workspace enforced by the controller, a **hardware stop**, **separation** from people or force-limited collaborative modes, and **checks** on the model's output (reject an action outside the bin range or a sudden jump). Safety standards for industrial and collaborative robots, such as ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066, apply to the whole cell, not the model. Evaluate on hardware: run each task many times, record success, partial progress and failures, and vary the scene (new objects, lighting, clutter), because a policy that succeeds on the training shelf can fail on a new one.

### Worked example: a warehouse picking robot

Brightpost's warehouse has an arm that picks the kettle for order 4471. The instruction is "pick the kettle for order 4471". The camera sees a shelf of boxes.

The model returns, on one pass, the seven tokens of the figure above: bins 204, 102, 32, 128, 140, 192, 255. Decoded, that is a move of +0.012 m in x, −0.004 m in y and −0.015 m in z, a small turn, and a closed gripper (illustrative ranges). At 6 Hz that is 0.012 × 6 = 0.072 m a second in x alone, so a 0.6 m reach at that step size takes about 8 s (0.6 ÷ 0.072; illustrative, and a real path is not a straight line). Each pass sees the new image, so if the box shifts, the next action corrects.

What the model does not decide: whether the order is valid. The agent that owns the order has already checked that order 4471 exists and that the kettle is in stock; the VLA only moves. The cell limits arm speed and grip force, stops if a person enters the zone, and logs the camera frame and action at every pass. When the arm cannot grasp after three tries, it stops and calls a person, in the same way the support assistant sends a £42 refund over its £30 limit to approval.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Action tokens (RT-2, OpenVLA) | Reuses an LLM unchanged; simple to train and serve | One decode step per token; 256-bin rounding; about 6 Hz for 7 B | Research and tabletop tasks at low control rates |
| Action chunks with a flow or diffusion head (π0) | Smooth, fast (up to 50 Hz), handles several valid actions | A second model to train; open-loop between chunks | Dexterous, high-rate tasks |
| Small policy trained from scratch | Fast, cheap, easy to certify | No web knowledge; one task or one robot | A fixed, repeated task |
| Teleoperated data | High quality, on your robot | Slow and costly to collect | Fine-tuning for your tasks |
| Simulated data | Unlimited and cheap | Sim-to-real gap | Pre-training and rare cases |
| Slow VLA plus fast controller | Smooth motion with semantic planning | Two loops to integrate and test | Production cells |

## Real-world examples

- **RT-2 (Google DeepMind, 2023).** PaLI-X and PaLM-E fine-tuned to output actions as tokens in 256 bins; the 55 B model ran at 1 to 3 Hz and a 5 B version at about 5 Hz.
- **OpenVLA (2024).** A 7 B open-source VLA trained on 970 k episodes from Open X-Embodiment on 64 A100 GPUs for 14 days; about 6 Hz on one RTX 4090; checkpoints and training code are open.
- **π0 (Physical Intelligence, 2024).** A 3.3 B flow-matching VLA with a 300 M action expert, 50-action chunks and control up to 50 Hz, shown on tasks such as folding laundry.
- **Brightpost's picking arm (this page's running example).** An illustrative design: a fine-tuned VLA behind a safety-limited controller, with every pass logged.

## Common pitfalls

- Judging a VLA by language benchmarks. It can describe the shelf perfectly and still miss the grasp. Measure task success on the real arm.
- Ignoring the control rate. A model that needs 500 ms per action cannot follow a moving target.
- Letting the model be the only safety layer. Limits and stops belong in the controller.
- Training on one scene and testing on it. Vary objects, lighting and clutter in the test set.
- Trusting simulation scores for contact-heavy tasks without a real test.
- Forgetting that data is the bottleneck: plan teleoperation time, not only GPU time.

## How to answer in an interview

1. Describe the loop: image and instruction in, one action out, the robot moves, a new image comes in.
2. Explain action tokens with numbers: 256 bins, a bin width, 7 tokens per action, and what that costs in speed.
3. Compare tokens with chunks and flow matching: OpenVLA's 6 Hz against π0's 50-action chunks.
4. Cover data: teleoperation, simulation, cross-embodiment sets, and the sim-to-real gap.
5. Close with safety and evaluation on hardware, and a fast controller under a slow model.

**What interviewers listen for:** the control rate as a design constraint, the bin arithmetic, the fast and slow split, and safety enforced outside the model.

**Red flags:** treating a robot as "a chatbot with arms", no mention of latency, and no real-hardware evaluation.
