A person can read 200 answers; nobody can read 20,000 after every prompt change.

- Interviewers ask about LLM-as-judge because it is the usual answer to that gap, and because a judge that nobody has checked is a second model with its own bugs.

## Overview

- An **LLM judge** is a model call that reads a question, an answer and a **rubric**, then returns a **reason and a verdict**. It scores in minutes what a person scores in days.
- It is only as good as its **agreement with people**. Measure it on a labelled set with **agreement and Cohen's kappa**: on Brightpost's 200 tickets an 88 % agreement (illustrative counts) is **κ = 0.71**, and a judge that always says "pass" also scores 70 % agreement but **κ = 0**.
- Judges have **known biases**: **position** (the first answer wins), **verbosity** (padding wins) and **self-preference** (its own writing wins). Each has a cheap test and a fix.
- **Pointwise** grading scores one answer against a rubric and suits release gates. **Pairwise** grading picks the better of two and suits choosing between prompts, but its cost grows with the **square** of the candidates.
- Use a judge to **scale** human judgment, never to replace it. Keep a person on the **disagreements** and on a sample of the **agreements**.

## Mental model

- A restaurant inspector cannot taste every dish, so the owner trains a sous-chef to check plates against a written standard.
- The sous-chef is fast, but has habits: favours the plate that came first, is impressed by a big portion, and likes dishes cooked in the house style.
- So the owner checks the sous-chef: the inspector and the sous-chef grade the same 200 plates, and the owner counts where they differ.
- Only a sous-chef who matches the inspector, and a standard that is written down, is trusted with the rest of the plates.

## Core concepts

- Brightpost's support assistant answers tickets and handles refunds.
- Its **golden set** is 200 real tickets with reference answers (see [Building Eval Datasets](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/building-eval-datasets)).
- A judge grades each new answer against the rubric, so a prompt change gets a score in minutes.

1. **Rubric.** The written standard the judge applies: for each criterion, what a pass and a fail look like, with one example of each. A vague rubric ("is it helpful?") gives vague scores.
2. **Pointwise judging.** The judge sees one answer and returns a verdict or a score. The result is comparable across runs, so it works as a gate.
3. **Pairwise judging.** The judge sees two answers and picks one, or a tie. Relative choices are steadier than absolute scores, which drift between prompts and judge versions.
4. **Reference-free and reference-based.** A reference-free judge sees only the question and the answer (plus retrieved context for RAG). It works when no gold answer exists but can share the generator's blind spots. A reference-based judge also sees a correct answer and is better for factual tasks, at the price of writing the references.
5. **Agreement.** The share of items where the judge and a person give the same verdict. **Cohen's kappa** corrects it for the agreement luck would give.
6. **Bias.** A systematic preference unrelated to quality. Three are well documented: position, verbosity and self-preference.
7. **Calibration.** Mapping raw judge scores to what a person would say, so a threshold means the same thing across judge versions. A "4 out of 5" from one judge prompt is not a "4" from another.

### A judge prompt and its rubric

- A judge prompt is a few lines of instructions, the three inputs and a fixed output shape.
- This one is our own wording for Brightpost; it asks for the reason before the verdict so the model works through the criteria first.

```text
You grade one answer from Brightpost's support assistant.
Ticket: {ticket}
Policy excerpt: {policy}
Answer: {answer}

Pass only if all three hold:
1. The answer agrees with the policy excerpt.
2. A refund over £30 is sent for approval, never promised.
3. Every claim appears in the policy excerpt or the ticket.
Otherwise fail. Length and tone earn nothing.

Reply as JSON: {"reason": "<two sentences>", "verdict": "pass" | "fail"}
```

The placeholders in braces are filled by code. Pass or fail with named criteria is easier for a judge to apply, and for you to audit, than a 1 to 5 scale.

## Technical mechanics

### Pointwise or pairwise

- Pointwise asks "is this answer good?"; pairwise asks "which of these two is better?".
- Zheng et al. (2023) describe both, and a third variant where the judge also gets a reference solution.
- They note that pairwise comparison scales badly because the number of pairs grows quadratically, and that single-answer grading may miss subtle differences.
- In their agreement tests single-answer grading matched pairwise judgments closely.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/eval/judge-pairwise-vs-pointwise"
  stepper
  caption="Pairwise matches pointwise at two candidates and falls behind fast: five candidates need four times the calls, ten need nine times."
/>

<StepCards>
  <StepCard n={1} title="What each asks the judge">
    Pointwise gives each answer a verdict against a fixed rubric, so it works as a release gate with a threshold. Pairwise picks a winner between two answers, so it needs no absolute scale and suits "is prompt B better than prompt A?".
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={2} title="What each costs as candidates grow">
    On the 200-ticket set, pointwise needs 200 × k calls for k candidates. Pairwise with both orders needs k(k − 1) ÷ 2 pairs × 2 orders × 200 = 200 × k(k − 1). That is 400 and 400 for two candidates, but 1,000 against 4,000 for five, and 2,000 against 18,000 for ten.
  </StepCard>
</StepCards>

- The rule of thumb: **pointwise to gate, pairwise to choose**.
- Gate a release on pointwise pass rate. Choose between two prompts with a pairwise head-to-head.
- To rank many systems from pairwise results, fit a Bradley-Terry or Elo model instead of counting raw wins, because a plain win count ignores who each system met.

### Biases, with numbers

- Three biases are documented in the MT-Bench study (Zheng et al., 2023).
- The numbers are for the 2023-era judges tested, so treat them as the size of the problem then, not now.
- The bias is real; its size is something to measure on your own judge.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/eval/judge-biases"
  stepper
  caption="Numbers are from one study of 2023-era judges (Zheng et al., Tables 2 and 3 and section 3.3); the size changes with the judge, the bias does not go away, and each has a cheap test you can run on your own judge."
/>

<StepCards>
  <StepCard n={1} title="Position bias">
    In the study the judges preferred the first answer on 30.0 % of pairs for GPT-4, 50.0 % for GPT-3.5 and 75.0 % for Claude-v1, with the default prompt. Test it by swapping the order of every pair. Count a win only when both orders agree, and report the share that flips as a quality number for the judge.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={2} title="Verbosity bias">
    - The authors padded 23 answers with a repeated list and no new content.
    - The judges failed to see through it on 91.3 % of answers for Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 and 8.7 % for GPT-4.
    - A later study found that GPT-4 favoured longer answers more than people did (Saito et al., 2023).
    - Test it by padding a good answer and checking the score does not rise; the AlpacaEval team raised its agreement with Chatbot Arena rankings from 0.94 to 0.98 by controlling for length (Dubois et al., 2024).
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={3} title="Self-preference">
    - GPT-4 won about 10 points more often when its own answers were in the pair, Claude-v1 about 25, GPT-3.5 not at all.
    - The authors say their data cannot settle whether it is a true bias.
    - A later study found that models that recognise their own text better prefer it more (Panickssery et al., 2024).
    - The cheap defence is a judge from a different family than the writer.
  </StepCard>
</StepCards>

- Two more failures do not need a figure. **Limited reasoning**: on math questions the default judge prompt failed 14 of 20 tests in the same study, chain of thought cut that to 6, and giving the judge a reference solution cut it to 3.
- **Eloquent wrong answers**: a weak judge can be swayed by fluent text that is wrong.
- Neither is fixed by a longer prompt alone; both are found by measuring against people.

### Multi-judge and calibration

- Several judges, or one judge with several prompts, lower the variance and catch one model's blind spots.
- The cost is linear in the number of judges unless they run in parallel.
- The usual compromise is a **cheap judge for triage and a strong judge for borderline cases**, with a person on anything the two disagree about.
- **Disagreement is itself a signal**: tickets where judges split are the ambiguous ones, which are the most worth a person's time.

- Calibration closes the loop. Take the 200 human labels, compare them with the judge's verdicts, and set the pass threshold where the judge agrees best with people.
- Redo it every time the judge model or prompt changes, because a new version has a different scale.
- Open-weight judges fine-tuned for the job exist; Prometheus (Kim et al., 2023) reports a Pearson correlation of 0.897 with human evaluators on 45 custom rubrics for a 13 B model, against 0.882 for GPT-4 in the same setup.
- That is one benchmark, not a guarantee for your task.

### Worked example: is Brightpost's judge good enough to gate a release?

- Brightpost grades its 200 golden tickets with the judge above, and people have already labelled them (the labelling is on [Human Evaluation & Feedback](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/human-evaluation-and-feedback)): 60 fail, 140 pass.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/eval/judge-agreement"
  stepper
  caption="Agreement is the diagonal, 176 of 200; kappa removes the agreement that luck would give, and the 14 missed failures are the cell to read first. Counts are illustrative."
/>

<StepCards>
  <StepCard n={1} title="Where the judge and the person agree">
    The judge and the person both said fail on 46 tickets and both said pass on 130. Agreement is (46 + 130) ÷ 200 = **88 %**. With 200 tickets, a normal approximation puts the 95 % interval at 88 % ± 4.5 points, about 83.5 to 92.5 %.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={2} title="Failures caught, and agreement beyond chance">
    Of the 60 tickets people failed, the judge caught 46: **77 %**. Chance agreement is (60 × 56 + 140 × 144) ÷ 200² = 0.588, so κ = (0.88 − 0.588) ÷ (1 − 0.588) = **0.71**. On one published reading of kappa, 0.60 to 0.79 is moderate and below 0.60 is inadequate (McHugh, 2012).
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={3} title="Why agreement alone is not enough">
    A judge that says "pass" to everything agrees on the 140 passes, 70 %, and its chance agreement is also 70 %, so κ = 0. On a set that is mostly passes, 88 % sounds good and says little.
  </StepCard>
</StepCards>

**The decision:**

- κ = 0.71 clears 0.60, so the judge can screen prompt changes.
- The 14 failures it passes are the risk: in this illustration most of them are invented policies.
- Brightpost adds a rubric line and two examples for that case, re-runs the 200 tickets, and sends every ticket where the judge says pass but a second judge says fail to a person.
- A release gate would read pass rate and also **audit a random 10 % of passes** each week, because a judge that has drifted looks the same as a good one until a person checks.

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  The judge is tuned on the same 200 tickets it is measured on, and agreement climbs to 95 % (illustrative numbers). In production it falls back to 84 %, because the rubric now fits those tickets. Keep a **held-out slice** of labelled tickets that no prompt edit sees, and re-measure agreement on it. A judge is validated on data it was not tuned on.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pointwise, pass or fail | Cheap (one call per answer); comparable across runs; a gate has a meaning | Weak at separating two good answers | Release gates, regression suites, dashboards |
| Pairwise, order swapped | Steadier relative preferences; no absolute scale needed | Two calls a pair; cost grows with the square of the candidates | Choosing between two prompts or models |
| Reference-free | No gold answers needed; portable | Can share the generator's blind spots | Open-ended tasks, new products |
| Reference-based | Anchored to ground truth | Writing references is slow and costly | Factual tasks, and the golden set |
| Single strong judge | Simple to run and explain | Concentrates one model's biases | Early work, low-risk checks |
| Cheap judge plus strong judge plus person | Spends effort where judges disagree | More moving parts to maintain | High volume or high-risk domains |

## Production systems

- **MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena (Zheng et al., 2023).** The paper that named the approach: a strong LLM judges answers on a multi-turn question set, and the authors report over 80 % agreement with human preferences, the same level as between humans. It is also the source of the three biases above.
- **AlpacaEval, length-controlled (Dubois et al., 2024).** An automatic evaluator that adjusts for answer length to cut verbosity bias, raising its Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98.
- **Prometheus (Kim et al., 2023).** An open-weight 13 B evaluator model that takes a reference answer and a custom score rubric, with a reported 0.897 Pearson correlation with human evaluators on its 45-rubric test.
- **InstructGPT labellers (Ouyang et al., 2022).** The human side of the comparison: the labellers agreed with each other on 72.6 ± 1.5 % of comparisons, so a judge's agreement with people is limited by how much people agree with each other.

## Failure modes & pitfalls

- **Trusting the judge with no human check.** Agreement and kappa on a labelled set come first; the first run of a judge is a hypothesis.
- **Reporting agreement alone.** On an imbalanced set it flatters a lazy judge. Report kappa, the failures caught and the interval.
- **Tuning and testing on the same tickets.** The score rises and production quality does not. Hold out a labelled slice.
- **A judge from the same family as the writer.** It favours its own style. Use a different family, or mix two.
- **Skipping the order swap.** A pairwise result with one order is partly an artefact of position.
- **Changing the judge silently.** A new judge model or prompt changes every score. Version both and re-calibrate.

## Interview framework

1. Say what it is for: scaling human judgment, with a rubric, a reason and a verdict, not replacing people.
2. Name the two grading modes and when each wins: pointwise for gates, pairwise for choices.
3. List the three biases with a test for each: swap the order, pad an answer, use a different family.
4. Show how you validate: a labelled set, agreement and kappa with an interval, a held-out slice.
5. Close with the loop: sample verdicts for people, route disagreements to them, re-calibrate on every change.

**What interviewers listen for:** kappa and not just agreement, a named test per bias, a held-out slice, and a person left in the loop.

**Red flags:** "the judge scores it 8 out of 10, so it is good", one model grading its own outputs, and no mention of how the judge itself is checked.

  <InterviewQuestion question="Why is pairwise judging often preferred to absolute 1-5 scores when using an LLM as a judge?" category="Concepts" level="Junior">
    - Relative comparisons reduce calibration drift because the judge anchors on two concrete options in the same context rather than mapping to an abstract numeric scale that shifts across sessions.
    - Pairwise designs also map naturally to preference learning for reward models.
    - The downside is cost: pairs grow with the square of the candidates, and with both orders judged, five candidates on 200 tickets need 4,000 calls against 1,000 for pointwise.
    - Pairwise can also be intransitive if it is not aggregated carefully.
  </InterviewQuestion>

    - Run each pair twice with the order swapped and count a win only when both orders agree; report the share that flips as a quality number for the judge.
    - Use neutral labels that do not hint which system is the baseline.
    - Measure the size of the bias against a subset that people adjudicated, and track it when the judge model changes.
    - To rank many systems, fit Bradley-Terry or Elo to the pairwise results instead of counting raw wins.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What are the main trade-offs of multi-judge consensus versus a single strong judge model?" category="Trade-offs" level="Mid">
    - Multiple judges reduce variance and catch idiosyncratic failures, but the cost rises linearly with the number of judges unless they run in parallel.
    - A single strong judge is simpler to run but concentrates one model's biases.
    - A common hybrid is a cheap judge for triage and a strong judge for borderline cases or high-risk domains, with a person reviewing anything the judges disagree about.
    - Disagreement marks the ambiguous items, which are the most useful ones for people to label.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Your judge agrees with human labels 88 % of the time. Is that good enough to gate releases?" category="Concepts" level="Mid">
    - Agreement alone does not say. If 70 % of tickets pass, a judge that always says pass agrees 70 % of the time, so compute Cohen's kappa: with 60 human fails and 140 passes and 46 fails caught, κ is 0.71, enough to screen changes.
    - Then read the cell that hurts, the failures the judge passes (14 of 60 here), and put an interval on the estimate, about ± 4.5 points at 200 tickets.
    - A gate also needs a held-out set the judge was not tuned on and a weekly audit of the passes.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="A verbose model wins every pairwise comparison, but customers prefer a competitor. What do you check?" category="Failure modes" level="Senior">
    - Suspect verbosity bias first. Pad a known good answer with a repeated list and see whether the judge's score rises; in the MT-Bench study two of three judges failed that test on 91.3 % of answers.
    - Add a rubric line that padding earns nothing, control for length in the comparison, and compare the judge's winners with customer preferences on a labelled sample.
    - If agreement with customers is low, the judge is measuring style, so re-calibrate it against their choices, not against the judge's own.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="How do you stop a judge from favouring the model that wrote the answers?" category="Architecture" level="Senior">
    - Use a judge from a different model family from the writer, or two judges from two families whose verdicts must agree.
    - Strip any model names or signatures from the answers before judging.
    - Measure the effect: grade a set where the same ideas are written by different models and check whether the judge's own family wins more than people say it should.
    - Research reports that models which recognise their own text prefer it more, so blind formatting also helps.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="What do you do when a new judge model gives every score two points higher?" category="Scaling" level="Senior">
    - Treat it as a scale change, not a quality gain.
    - Re-run the judge on the labelled set, compare to the human labels and set the pass threshold again so the judge's pass rate matches the people's.
    - Keep the old and new judge side by side for one release cycle and plot both on the dashboard.
    - Version the judge model and the prompt in every stored verdict, so old results are never compared with new ones as if they were the same measurement.
  </InterviewQuestion>
