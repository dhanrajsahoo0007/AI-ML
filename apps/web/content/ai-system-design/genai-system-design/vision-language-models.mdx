A support assistant that cannot see a photo of a cracked kettle makes the customer describe it in words. Interviewers ask about vision-language models (VLMs) because the answer joins three things: how a picture becomes tokens, how those tokens reach the language model, and what the tokens cost.

## TL;DR

- A VLM cuts the image into **square patches**, turns each patch into a vector with a **vision encoder**, and passes the vectors through a **projector** so the language model can read them like word embeddings.
- The **patch count is arithmetic**. LLaVA-1.5 uses CLIP ViT-L/14 at 336 px: 336 ÷ 14 = 24 patches a side, **24 × 24 = 576 visual tokens**.
- There are two ways to connect vision to the language model: **put image tokens in the sequence** (LLaVA) or **add cross-attention layers** that read a fixed set of visual tokens (Flamingo, 64 tokens).
- **Resolution is cost.** On one provider's tile rule (checked 15 November 2026), a 4032 × 3024 phone photo becomes **765 input tokens**, about a third of Brightpost's 2,200-token text prompt.
- **CLIP** learns a shared image-text space from **400 million pairs** with a contrastive loss; it retrieves and classifies, but it does not write answers.
- VLMs fail in predictable places: **counting, spatial relations and small text**. Test those before you ship.

## The idea in plain English

Imagine describing a photo to a friend over the phone, but you are only allowed to read out a fixed number of tiny tiles, each as a short code. A vision encoder is the person who turns each tile into its code. The projector is the translator who rewrites those codes in the friend's own vocabulary. The friend, the language model, then reads the codes in order, followed by your question. If you cut the photo into more tiles, the friend sees finer detail, but the call takes longer and costs more.

## Core concepts

### Vision encoders and patch tokens

A **vision encoder** is usually a Vision Transformer (ViT). It splits the image into fixed-size square **patches**, flattens each into a vector, adds position information, and runs self-attention over all patches (see [Self-Attention Explained](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/self-attention-explained)). The output is one vector per patch. "ViT-L/14" means the large model with 14 × 14 px patches. A 336 × 336 image gives 24 × 24 = 576 patches, so 576 vectors. The encoder is normally **pre-trained** (often with CLIP's recipe) and kept frozen or lightly tuned.

### Connecting vision to the language model

Two designs dominate. **A projector** maps each vision vector to the language model's embedding width (a linear layer, or the two-layer MLP in LLaVA-1.5). The projected vectors become **visual tokens** that sit in the sequence next to the text tokens, so ordinary self-attention reads both. **Cross-attention** keeps image tokens out of the sequence. New layers between the frozen language-model layers take queries from the text and keys and values from the image tokens. Flamingo compresses image features to a fixed 64 visual tokens with a **Perceiver Resampler** first. The first design is simpler; the second keeps the text sequence short.

### Resolution, tiling and token cost

More pixels mean more patches, and more patches mean more tokens, more memory for the [KV cache](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/long-context-ring-attention-and-kv-quantization) and a longer time to the first token. Models handle this in three ways: resize everything to one input size (LLaVA-1.5: 336 px, always 576 tokens); cut a large image into **tiles** and encode each (the provider rule in the worked example below); or encode at the image's own size up to a patch budget. A **detail setting** lets the caller trade cost for fidelity.

### Contrastive models such as CLIP versus generative VLMs

**CLIP** has two encoders, one for images and one for text, trained so that a photo and its caption land close together in one vector space. It scores image-text similarity; it cannot answer a question. A **generative VLM** (LLaVA, Flamingo and the multimodal chat models) puts a vision encoder in front of a language model and writes text. Many generative VLMs use a CLIP image encoder as their eyes, so the two families are linked: CLIP gives the vectors, the LLM gives the sentence.

## Deep dive

### Projector or cross-attention

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/multimodal/vlm-fusion"
  stepper
  caption="Left: image tokens join the sequence, so the LLM's own self-attention reads them. Right: new gated cross-attention layers read a fixed 64 visual tokens; the text sequence stays short."
/>

**How to read it.** Orange blocks are attention, using the colours of the Transformer pages; grey blocks are the frozen language-model layers. In the projector design (left) nothing new is added to the LLM, but the sequence grows by 576. In the cross-attention design (right) the LLM is frozen and new layers are trained. Flamingo adds a gated block every fourth layer in its 9 B model and every seventh in its 80 B model, and each gate starts at zero so the frozen model's behaviour is unchanged on day one.

**Which wins today.** Many open VLMs follow the projector design because it is simple to train and to serve: image tokens look like text tokens to the engine. Cross-attention is attractive when many images or video frames would blow up the sequence. The [Multimodal & Voice AI](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/multimodal-and-voice-ai) page puts this choice next to audio.

### Contrastive pre-training, computed

**How to read it.** Each row is a photo, each column a caption. Cells show cosine similarity. A softmax over row 1 at temperature 0.07 gives the right caption a probability of 0.87, so the loss is −ln 0.87 = 0.14. The columns give the same loss in the other direction, which is why CLIP's loss is called symmetric. CLIP's batch held 32,768 pairs, so each photo had 32,767 wrong captions to push away. ### Document, chart and screen understanding

Pictures of text, tables and charts are VLM territory, and they are the hardest case for a fixed small input. A scanned invoice at 336 px turns its small print into a few blurry patches. Models that tile the page, or take many more patches for a document, do better because each character gets enough pixels. Two practical rules: send the page at the resolution the model's guide recommends, and for pure text extraction compare against an OCR step, which is cheaper and gives exact characters. Charts need a check that the model read the axis and the units, not only the shape.

### Grounding and pointing

**Grounding** means tying words to places in the image: the model returns a box, a point or a mask for "the crack on the lid". Some VLMs are trained to write coordinates as text tokens, so a box is four numbers in the answer. This makes answers checkable: draw the box and look. For Brightpost, a grounded answer ("the crack runs along the lid, here") is easier for an agent to audit than "the lid looks damaged". Grounding quality varies by model, so measure it on your own images.

### Failure modes: counting, spatial reasoning and small text

- **Counting.** "How many cracks?" is hard because the answer needs a count over patches, not a global impression. Expect errors beyond a handful of objects.
- **Spatial reasoning.** "Is the handle left of the spout?" depends on the order of patches, which the projector and attention only partly keep. Left and right are common slips.
- **Small text.** A serial number on a kettle base spans a few pixels. Downscaling destroys it. Crop and resend the region at a higher detail.
- **Hallucination.** The model describes plausible detail that is not in the photo. Ask it to quote or point to the evidence, and check with a second pass.

### Worked example: the cracked kettle

A Brightpost customer sends a phone photo of order 4471, a cracked kettle, with the message "it arrived like this". The photo is 4032 × 3024 pixels (assumed size). The provider's tile rule, from OpenAI's image guide for its gpt-4o and gpt-4.1 models (read 15 November 2026), turns it into tokens.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/multimodal/vlm-resolution-cost"
  stepper
  caption="Resize, count 512 px tiles, multiply. The kettle photo costs 765 tokens at high detail and 85 at low detail. Rule dated 15 November 2026; the price is illustrative."
/>

**How to read it.** The photo is scaled to fit 2048 × 2048 (2048 × 1536), then its shortest side is scaled down to 768 (1024 × 768). That makes ceil(1024 ÷ 512) × ceil(768 ÷ 512) = 2 × 2 = 4 tiles.

**The numbers.** Tokens = 85 + 4 × 170 = 765. At low detail the cost is 85 tokens, but a cracked lid is a small feature, so Brightpost uses high detail. The photo adds 765 ÷ 2,200 = 35 % to a normal prompt. If 5 % of the 200,000 daily requests carry a photo (illustrative share), that is 10,000 × 765 = 7.65 M tokens a day, 1.7 % of the 440 M text input tokens. The same guide now lists newer models that count 32 px patches and apply a multiplier (1.2 for several current models), so the formula is model-specific; read the guide for the model you use.

**What the model does with it.** The VLM answers "yes, there is a crack across the lid, running to the handle" and points to it. It does not decide the refund. The price is £42 and refunds over £30 need approval, so the agent routes the case to a person with the photo and the grounded description attached. Brightpost also checks the serial number: a first pass at low detail cannot read it, so the agent crops the base and resends that region at high detail.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Projector (tokens in the sequence) | Simple; works with any LLM engine | Sequence grows by hundreds of tokens per image | General chat with a few images |
| Cross-attention layers | Short text sequence; fixed image cost | New layers to train; trickier to serve | Many images or video frames |
| Fixed small input (one resize) | Predictable cost and latency | Loses small text and fine detail | Photos where the object is large |
| Tiling or high detail | Reads documents and fine print | More tokens and slower first token | Documents, screenshots, serial numbers |
| CLIP-style encoder only | Cheap similarity search | No generation, no reasoning | Retrieval and classification |

## Real-world examples

- **LLaVA-1.5 (2023).** CLIP-ViT-L at 336 px with a two-layer MLP connector on a Vicuna model. The paper reports training on about 1.2 M public examples in about one day on a single 8-A100 node.
- **CLIP (2021).** 400 M image-text pairs; the best model matches the original ResNet-50's ImageNet accuracy zero-shot (76.2 % top-1).
- **Flamingo (2022).** Frozen language model plus gated cross-attention and a Perceiver Resampler of 64 tokens, trained on interleaved web text and images for few-shot prompting.
- **Provider tile rules (November 2026).** One major API charges 85 + 170 per 512 px tile for its tile-based models and counts 32 px patches for newer ones, so image cost is a documented formula you can estimate before you send.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating an image as one token. A photo is hundreds of tokens, and they count against the context window and the bill.
- Sending a huge photo and trusting the default. The provider may downscale it and lose the small text you needed.
- Using low detail to save money, then asking about fine print.
- Trusting counts and left-right answers without a test set of your own images.
- Letting the VLM decide a policy outcome. It describes; the rules and the people decide (a £42 refund needs approval).
- Quoting a token formula from memory. Rules change by model; date the number.

## How to answer in an interview

1. Draw the path: image, patches, vision encoder, projector, visual tokens, language model.
2. Give the arithmetic: image size ÷ patch size, squared, is the token count (336 ÷ 14 = 24, so 576).
3. Name the two connection designs and one cost of each: sequence length against extra layers.
4. Explain contrastive training in one line: matching pairs on the diagonal, every other cell pushed down.
5. Close with failure modes and how you would test them: counting, spatial relations, small text, hallucinated detail.

**What interviewers listen for:** the patch arithmetic, the token cost of resolution, the two fusion designs, and a plan to evaluate on your own images.

**Red flags:** "the model just sees the image", no mention of tokens or cost, and trusting a VLM's answer on fine print.
