A model knows two things on any turn: what it learned in training, and the tokens you put in front of it. Context engineering is the work of choosing those tokens. Prompt engineering asks how to word the instructions; context engineering asks what else belongs next to them on this turn, in what order, and what should be dropped. For an agent that runs for dozens of turns, it is the difference between an assistant that stays sharp and one that slowly drowns in its own tool output.

## TL;DR

- The context window is a **budget**, not a bucket. Every token costs money and latency, and it competes with every other token for the model's attention.
- Context comes from six sources: **instructions, tool definitions, retrieved chunks, memory, conversation history and tool results**. The first four are roughly fixed per turn; the last two grow every turn.
- A context builder does three things on every turn: **select** what is relevant, **order** it (stable parts first, key evidence last) and **compress** what is old or bulky.
- Models use long contexts unevenly: information in the middle is used less well, and accuracy drops as input grows even on easy tasks (**context rot**).
- For long-running agents, three tools keep the window healthy: **compaction** (summarise and restart), **just-in-time retrieval** (fetch when needed, not up front) and **sub-agents** (explore in a separate window, return a summary).

## The idea in plain English

Imagine briefing a colleague who will take over a customer call. You do not hand them every email the customer ever sent, every page of the policy manual and the raw printout of every database lookup. You give them the policy page that matters, the last few messages, the order details that matter, and a one-paragraph summary of everything before that. You put the customer's actual question last, so it is the freshest thing in their head. That briefing is context engineering. The model is the colleague, and it reads every word you give it, useful or not.

## Core concepts

The running example is Brightpost's support agent: a model with four tools (`get_order`, `search_help`, `create_refund`, `send_email`) that answers customers from a help centre of **3,600 chunks** (see [RAG Fundamentals](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/rag-fundamentals)). One conversation starts with a lost phone and, twelve turns later, reaches "My kettle arrived cracked. It's order 4471. Can I get my money back?".

### Context as a budget

A model's context window is the maximum number of tokens it can read in one call, and what it learns from them at request time is covered in [Context Windows & In-Context Learning](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/context-windows-and-in-context-learning). Teams usually run an agent under a lower ceiling than the model allows, because cost and latency grow with every token and quality falls as the window fills. Anthropic's engineering team calls this the model's "attention budget": each new token draws it down, so the goal is the smallest set of high-signal tokens that does the job.

### Sources of context: instructions, knowledge, tools and memory

Every token in the window comes from one of six sources, and they behave in three different ways:

- **Fixed on every turn:** the instructions (role, policy, tone; versioned like code, see [System Prompt Design](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/system-prompt-design)), the tool definitions (name, description and parameter schema of each tool; forty tools at 250 tokens each, illustrative, is 10,000 tokens before the customer says a word) and memory, facts kept across sessions such as "prefers email" (see [Agent Memory](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/agent-memory)).
- **Fetched per question:** retrieved chunks, the RAG part of the window.
- **Growing every turn:** the conversation history, and tool results. An order record or a web page can be thousands of tokens, and agents call tools on most turns, so these two decide how long an agent can run.

### Selecting, ordering and compressing context

The context builder makes three decisions on every turn. Here they are applied to the kettle turn.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/rag/ctx-select-order-compress"
  stepper
  caption="The same turn costs 12,420 tokens as gathered and 5,140 after select, order and compress."
/>

**How to read it.** Step 1 gathers everything the builder could fetch, in the order it arrived: instructions, five password chunks still in the window from turn 1, twelve turns of history, memory, four tool definitions, five fresh chunks about damaged-item refunds, the full record for order 4471 and the new message. Step 2 selects: the password chunks belong to a question already answered, and a reranker keeps the best three refund chunks out of five; a dropped token costs nothing and distracts no one. Step 3 orders: instructions, tools and memory go first because they are the same on every turn, so a [prompt cache](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/prompt-caching) can reuse them, and the evidence goes last, right before the message, where models use it best. Step 4 compresses: twelve turns become a 400-token summary plus the last two turns verbatim, and the 2,000-token order record becomes the four fields a refund needs (item, price, delivery date and status).

**The numbers.** 12,420 − 2,000 (password chunks) − 800 (two weak refund chunks) = 9,620. Then 9,620 − (3,600 − 1,000) − (2,000 − 120) = 5,140 tokens, 41 % of what was gathered. All counts are illustrative.

### Context rot and distraction

More context is not free even when it fits. Liu et al. (2023) found that models used information at the start or the end of a long input better than information in the middle, even models built for long contexts.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/rag/lost-in-the-middle"
  caption="Shape only, after Liu et al. (2023); the same chunk helps more at the edges of the prompt than in its middle."
/>

**How to read it.** The curve is the shape of the finding, not the paper's data: accuracy is highest when the useful chunk sits at either end of the prompt, which is why step 3 above puts the evidence next to the message.

Chroma's "Context Rot" report (Hong et al., 2025) tested 18 models and found that performance grew less reliable as input length grew, even on simple tasks. A single distractor (text that looks relevant but is not) lowered accuracy, and more distractors lowered it further. The five old password chunks in the kettle turn are exactly that kind of distractor. The fix is the same in both studies: send fewer, better tokens.

## Architecture

In a production agent one component, the context builder, assembles the model's input on every turn. Brightpost's builder grows one source at a time.

/>

<StepCards>
  <StepCard n={1} title="Instructions and the message">
    The builder joins a versioned system prompt and the customer's message and sends them to the model. With one turn and no tools, there is nothing to manage yet. The model cannot answer Brightpost-specific questions because it has no evidence.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={2} title="Add retrieved chunks">
    The builder asks the vector index for the top 5 chunks and places them before the message. Answers now cite real articles. The model still cannot look up an order or act on one.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={3} title="Add tools and their results">
    The model can now call tools. Their results come back through the builder, not straight into the next call, so a 2,000-token order record can be trimmed to the fields that matter. This is where the window starts to grow every turn.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={4} title="Add memory and history">
    A memory store supplies facts about this customer, and a history store keeps every turn so the builder can replay the conversation. Two sources now grow without limit: history and accumulated tool results.
  </StepCard>
  <StepCard n={5} title="Compact when the budget runs low">
    When the assembled context passes 80 % of the budget, a compactor (a model call with a summarisation prompt) replaces old turns with a summary in the history store. The builder reads the summary instead of the raw turns from then on.
  </StepCard>
</StepCards>

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  Tool results appended straight into the conversation, with no builder in between, are the most common way agents run out of window. One large page or log can push out the instructions or the customer's question. Route every result through the builder and give each tool a token cap.
</Callout>

## Deep dive

### Compaction and summarization

Compaction summarises a nearly full context and continues with the summary. The hard part is deciding what the summary must keep: decisions already made, open questions, promises to the customer, and identifiers such as order numbers. Anthropic describes Claude Code's compaction as keeping architectural decisions and unresolved bugs and continuing with the five most recently used files; it suggests tuning the summary prompt for recall first, then cutting what is not needed. The lightest form is **tool-result clearing**: old raw tool output is removed and the call is left in place. Anthropic reported that context editing (clearing stale tool calls and results) cut token use by 84 % in a 100-turn web-search evaluation. A third option is **note-taking**: the agent writes progress to a file or memory store outside the window and reads it back later. Resets and state hand-offs are covered in [Context Compaction, Resets & State](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/context-compaction-resets-and-state).

### Just-in-time retrieval versus preloading

**Preloading** retrieves before the model runs: the builder fetches the top 5 chunks for every turn, needed or not, which is how classic RAG works. **Just-in-time** retrieval gives the agent lightweight references (article titles, file paths, order ids) and a tool to open them, so it loads only what it decides it needs. For Brightpost, preloading spends 2,000 tokens on every turn, including "thanks, that's all"; just-in-time spends a few dozen tokens on references and 400 per chunk actually opened. The cost is latency and judgement: each fetch is another model turn, and the agent must know what to look for. Anthropic recommends a hybrid in many cases, with Claude Code loading its instruction files up front and finding everything else with search tools. [Agentic RAG](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/agentic-rag) is the retrieval side of the same idea.

### Isolating context with sub-agents

Some tasks need far more reading than one window holds. A sub-agent is a separate model call with its own fresh window and a narrow task; it explores, then returns a short summary to a lead agent.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/rag/ctx-subagents"
  stepper
  caption="Each sub-agent spends its own window exploring; the lead reads 4,500 tokens of findings instead of 60,000."
/>

**How to read it.** In step 1 the lead agent reads the orders, the refund policy and the past tickets itself, and 60,000 tokens do not fit its 32,000-token budget. In step 2 each source goes to a sub-agent that reads it in its own window and sends back only its findings. The lead sees three short reports and keeps room to reason.

**The numbers.** 3 × 20,000 = 60,000 tokens read in one window, against 3 × 1,500 = 4,500 in the lead's window (illustrative; Anthropic reports sub-agent summaries of often 1,000 to 2,000 tokens). The total spent goes up, not down: Anthropic's research system used about 15 times the tokens of a chat. Sub-agents buy focus, not savings. See [Multi-Agent Systems](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/multi-agent-systems).

## Trade-offs

| Decision | Option A | Option B | Choose A when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Getting knowledge in | Preload top-k every turn | Just-in-time via a tool | Most turns need the knowledge and latency matters |
| Old history | Keep verbatim | Summarise | Conversations are short and exact wording matters |
| Tool results | Keep raw | Trim or clear after use | The model must quote the raw output later |
| Large tasks | One agent, one window | Sub-agents with summaries | The task fits in one window and steps depend on each other |

## Real-world examples

- **Coding agents** such as Claude Code load project instruction files up front, find other files with search tools on demand, and compact long sessions into a summary.
- **Anthropic's multi-agent research system** has a lead agent spawn sub-agents that search in parallel, each in its own context window, and return condensed findings.
- **Support assistants** retrieve help-centre chunks per question, cap tool output and summarise long chats before handing them to a person.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating a larger window as a fix. A million-token window still rots, costs more per call and hides bad selection.
- Leaving every tool result in the history forever.
- Loading dozens of tool definitions on every turn when the task needs three.
- Putting volatile parts (timestamps, retrieved chunks) before stable ones, which breaks [prompt caching](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/prompt-caching).
- No token accounting per source, so nobody knows which part of the window grew.

## How to answer in an interview

1. Define it (choosing the tokens the model sees on each turn), list the sources and say which grow: history and tool results.
2. Walk through select, order and compress with numbers for one turn.
3. Name context rot and lost in the middle as the reason "more context" is not free.
4. For long-running agents, cover compaction, just-in-time retrieval and sub-agents, with the cost of each.

**What interviewers listen for:** a per-source token budget, a compaction trigger, and evidence placed where the model uses it.

**Red flags:** "just use a bigger context window", or no plan for what happens on turn 50.

  <InterviewQuestion question="What is context engineering, and how is it different from prompt engineering?" category="Concepts" level="Junior">
    Prompt engineering is about how to word the instructions and examples. Context engineering is about everything the model sees on a given turn: the instructions, tool definitions, retrieved documents, memory, conversation history and tool results, and which of them to include, in what order and at what length. It matters most for agents, because their context changes and grows on every turn.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Your support agent gets worse after about twenty turns. How do you diagnose and fix it?" category="Architecture" level="Mid">
    First measure: log the token count per source on every turn. Usually history and tool results have grown until they crowd out the instructions and push the evidence into the middle of the window. Fix it in order: trim or clear tool results once used, summarise old turns past a threshold such as 80 % of the budget, keep the last few turns verbatim, and place retrieved evidence right before the latest message. Then check on long conversations in the eval set that quality holds at turn 20 and turn 50.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="When would you retrieve just in time instead of preloading context?" category="Trade-offs" level="Mid">
    Preload when most turns need the same kind of evidence and latency matters, as in a help-centre bot answering one question per turn. Retrieve just in time when the agent's needs vary a lot from turn to turn, when the corpus is large or changes as the agent works (a codebase), or when many turns need nothing. The price is extra model turns and a dependence on the agent knowing what to look for, so a hybrid is common: load a small, stable core up front and let the agent search for the rest.
  </InterviewQuestion>
  <InterviewQuestion question="Do sub-agents save tokens? When are they worth it?" category="Trade-offs" level="Senior">
    No, they usually spend more in total, because each sub-agent reads its own material and the lead still has to coordinate. Anthropic reported its multi-agent research system used about 15 times the tokens of a chat. What they buy is a clean lead context: each sub-agent explores in its own window and returns a summary of a thousand or two tokens, so the lead can reason over findings instead of raw pages. They pay off for broad, parallel research and hurt when steps depend tightly on each other or must share the same detailed state.
  </InterviewQuestion>
