A fine-tuned model is only as good as the examples it trains on, and most teams have too few good ones. Interviewers ask about synthetic data and curation because a strong answer covers where examples come from, how a pipeline keeps them diverse and clean, and how to stop evaluation tickets leaking into training.

## TL;DR

- **Data quality beats data volume.** LIMA fine-tuned a 65 B model on only **1,000 curated examples** and still produced strong answers (Zhou et al., 2023), so what you remove matters as much as what you add.
- **Three sources:** logs of real work, human-written examples, and model-generated (synthetic) examples. Mix them; real data anchors the synthetic part.
- **Synthetic data needs a pipeline.** Self-Instruct starts from **175 seed tasks**, asks a model for new ones and keeps a new instruction only if its ROUGE-L similarity to every earlier one is **below 0.7** (Wang et al., 2022).
- **Raw output is noisy.** In the Self-Instruct paper's own review of 200 samples, only **54 %** of examples had instruction, input and output all valid, which is why filtering and a human spot check are not optional.
- **Leakage ruins evaluation.** Golden-set tickets that reach the seeds or the training data inflate the score; split the golden set off first and check overlap.
- **Training on your own output, round after round, loses the rare cases.** Recursive training that replaces real data loses the rare cases (Shumailov et al., Nature, 2024); keeping real data in the mix avoids it.

## The idea in plain English

A cookery school wants a thick recipe book for its students. It has 150 great recipes from its chefs. A junior writes variations by the hundred. Some are copies of one another, some call for a pan nobody owns, a few would make people ill. The head chef does not taste every page. The school removes copies, runs a checklist, has a senior cook read a sample, and keeps the final exam recipes out of the book. That is a synthetic-data pipeline: cheap variation, hard filters, a person on a sample, and an exam kept secret.

## Core concepts

### Sources: logs, human labels and synthetic generation

- **Logs.** Resolved support tickets with an agent-approved reply. Real language and real mistakes; the volume is large, but the content is messy and carries personal data. Brightpost's 5,000-ticket set comes from here.
- **Human labels.** Support leads write or correct examples for the rare and risky cases. Highest quality, slowest, most expensive per example.
- **Synthetic generation.** A strong model writes new examples from seeds and rules. Cheap, fast, and able to cover cases the logs lack (a refund over £30 in three phrasings), but it inherits the generator's habits and mistakes.

Use logs for the bulk, humans for the cases that matter most and for the golden set, and synthetic data to fill gaps. A model that only ever sees its own kind of text learns little that is new.

### Generating diverse synthetic examples

The Self-Instruct recipe is the template: start from a small hand-written seed pool (175 tasks in the paper), prompt a model with a few seeds and ask for new instructions and answers, filter, and add the survivors to the pool. The paper generated over 52,000 instructions and 82,439 instances this way and fine-tuned GPT-3 on them, a 33 % absolute improvement on Super-NaturalInstructions and a 5 % absolute gap behind InstructGPT-001 on its expert-written set. Stanford's Alpaca followed the same idea with text-davinci-003: 52,000 instruction-output pairs for under \$500.

The danger is sameness. A generator asked the same thing again and again drifts to its favourite phrasings. Vary the prompt on purpose: name a topic, a customer type, a tone and a difficulty each time.

### Filtering and quality checks

Filter in order from cheapest to dearest: format (valid JSON, right length, language), business rules (refunds over £30 need approval, so a reply that promises one without approval fails), duplicates, then a judge model for tone and correctness (see [LLM-as-Judge Evaluation](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/llm-as-judge-evaluation)). Filters also protect privacy: a ticket with a card number is removed or scrubbed before it reaches a training file (see [Data Privacy & PII Handling](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/data-privacy-pii-handling)).

### De-duplication and train/test leakage

Duplicates waste training steps and teach the model to repeat itself. Lee et al. (2021) found that language-model datasets hold many near duplicates and that deduplicated training made models emit memorised text about ten times less often. Use exact hashing first, then near-duplicate methods (n-gram overlap such as ROUGE-L, MinHash or embedding similarity).

The same tools catch **leakage**: examples that also sit in the evaluation set. With synthetic data a leak spreads, because one golden ticket used as a seed becomes dozens of variants.

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/finetune/synth-leakage"

**The numbers.** Twenty of the 150 seeds were copied from the golden set. The teacher writes 8,000 ÷ 150 = 53.3 candidates per seed, so 1,067 from those 20. The pipeline keeps 52.5 %, which leaves about 560 near copies among the 4,200 kept (13.3 %). On those 20 tickets the student passes 20 of 20; an honest 88 % would be 17.6. The score is inflated by 2.4 ÷ 200 = **1.2 points**, enough to hide a real regression.

<Callout tone="red" title="What breaks">
  A leak does not fail loudly. The offline score rises, the launch is approved, and live traffic disappoints. The golden set must be split off before the seed pool is chosen, and every candidate must be compared with it.
</Callout>

## Deep dive

### How much data is enough

Less than most teams expect, if the data is good. LIMA (Zhou et al., 2023) fine-tuned a 65 B model on 1,000 curated prompts and responses and concluded that nearly all of a model's knowledge comes from pretraining. Style, format and policy are quick to learn; new facts are not (see [Fine-Tuning LLMs](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/fine-tuning-llms)). A practical rule: start with hundreds of excellent examples, train, read the errors, and add examples for the failing slices. Plot score against dataset size on a fixed golden set, and stop when the curve flattens.

### Model collapse and synthetic-data bias

Shumailov et al. (Nature, 2024) trained models repeatedly on the output of the previous model and found that the tails of the original distribution disappear, an effect they call model collapse. Rare cases go first, and for Brightpost those are the odd, costly refund cases. Gerstgrasser et al. (2024) show collapse is not inevitable: when each round's synthetic data is added to the original real data instead of replacing it, test error stays bounded in their experiments.

Bias is quieter. A generator that is polite and verbose produces polite, verbose data, and a judge from the same family prefers it. Keep real data in the mix, vary the prompts, use a different model as judge where you can, and check the result on slices.

### Dataset versioning and data cards

A dataset is code. Version every build with its inputs: the seed list, the generator model and prompt, the filter thresholds and the golden-set hash. Store counts at each stage so a change in the funnel is visible. Write a data card (the "datasheet" of Gebru et al., 2018): motivation, composition, how it was collected, known gaps, and approved uses. When a model misbehaves, you can then name the data it learned from.

```json
{"id": "syn-04102", "source": "synthetic", "seed": "tkt-1187", "generator": "teacher-v3",
```

### Worked example: Brightpost's rare cases

Brightpost's 5,000 logged tickets contain few refunds over £30 that were escalated correctly. The model is weakest there.

1. **Seed.** Support leads choose 150 resolved tickets on refunds and damaged goods, none in the golden set.
2. **Generate.** The teacher writes 8,000 variants, naming a topic (kettle, toaster, parcel lost), a customer type (calm, angry, non-native speaker) and an amount above or below £30.
3. **Deduplicate and filter.** 8,000 − 1,800 = 6,200, then 6,200 − 2,000 = 4,200. A reply that promises a £42 refund with no approval step fails the rule check.
4. **Spot check.** A lead reads 100 and finds 3 bad. The team changes the prompt and reruns.
5. **Mix and train.** The 4,200 synthetic examples join the real 5,000 tickets (about 46 % synthetic: 4,200 ÷ 9,200). Real data stays in the mix.
6. **Leak check.** Every kept example is compared with the 200 golden tickets before training; any with ROUGE-L 0.7 or higher is dropped.

All counts are illustrative. The stages match the figure in the Architecture section.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Logs only | Real language; cheap | Few rare cases; noisy; personal data | Plenty of resolved, clean traffic |
| Human-written | Highest quality | Slow, costly | Rare, risky cases; the golden set |
| Synthetic from a strong model | Cheap; fills gaps | Inherits generator bias; leakage and collapse risk; provider terms | Coverage gaps, with real data in the mix |
| Strict filtering | Clean data; smaller files | Discards useful rare cases if too tight | Always; tune thresholds on a sample |
| Loose filtering | More data | Bad examples teach bad habits | Early experiments only |

## Real-world examples

- **Self-Instruct (2022).** 175 seed tasks grew to over 52,000 instructions; GPT-3 tuned on the result gained 33 % absolute on Super-NaturalInstructions.
- **Alpaca (2023).** 52,000 pairs from text-davinci-003 for under \$500, from the same seed set; released for research use.
- **LIMA (2023).** 1,000 curated examples were enough to tune a 65 B model to strong answers, arguing for quality over volume.
- **C4 and other web corpora.** Lee et al. (2021) found one 61-word sentence repeated over 60,000 times in C4, and reported over 4 % overlap between some validation and training sets.

## Common pitfalls

- Putting a golden-set ticket in the seed pool, the prompts or the judge's examples.
- Running one generator prompt 8,000 times and calling it a dataset.
- Trusting a judge from the same model family as the generator.
- Training on synthetic data alone, round after round.
- Skipping the human read: raw Self-Instruct data was valid in all fields only 54 % of the time.
- Keeping no record of the seeds, prompt and thresholds, so the dataset cannot be rebuilt.

## How to answer in an interview

1. Name the three sources and say what each is best for.
2. Walk the pipeline: seed, generate with deliberate variety, deduplicate, rule filters and judge, human sample.
3. Raise leakage yourself: golden set split first, overlap checked, the 1.2-point inflation example.
4. Mention model collapse and the fix: keep real data, accumulate rather than replace.
5. Close with versioning, a data card and a check of the provider's terms for generated outputs (see [Distillation & Small Models](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/distillation-and-small-models)).

**What interviewers listen for:** quality over volume, diversity measured not assumed, a leak check, and a human in the loop.

**Red flags:** "just generate more data", no held-out set, no dedupe, or trusting synthetic data without a sample read.
