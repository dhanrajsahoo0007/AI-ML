A phone agent is only as good as its ears and its mouth.

- Interviewers ask about speech recognition (ASR, automatic speech recognition) and speech synthesis (TTS, text to speech) because each hides a trap: one wrong digit in an order number, or a pause before every reply, breaks the call.

## Overview

- **ASR turns sound into text.** Whisper resamples audio to **16,000 Hz**, builds an **80-bin log-mel spectrogram** on 25 ms windows every 10 ms, and runs an encoder-decoder Transformer over **30-second chunks**.
- **A spectrogram is the input, not the waveform.** 30 s of audio is 480,000 samples but only 3,000 frames of 80 numbers (240,000), which the encoder can read like a picture.
- **Word error rate (WER) = (S + D + I) ÷ N**: substitutions, deletions and insertions over the words in the reference. It counts edits, not meaning, so check the words that matter, such as order numbers.
- **TTS is the reverse:** write the text out as words, plan the sound as acoustic frames, then let a **vocoder** draw the waveform (240 samples per 10 ms frame at 24 kHz).
- **Batch and streaming are different jobs.** Batch recognition reads a whole recording; streaming recognition emits partial text while the caller is still talking, and that is what a live call needs.
- **Voice is personal data.** Cloning a voice needs consent, and a synthetic voice on a phone call has legal rules in some countries.

## Mental model

- Imagine a court stenographer who hears a call and types it, and a voice actor who reads a script aloud.
- The stenographer does not listen to raw air pressure.
- She hears pitch and loudness over short moments, like a musical score.
- A spectrogram is that score: time runs left to right, pitch runs up the page, and darker cells mean more energy.
- The encoder reads the score; the decoder types the words.
- The voice actor works backwards: from the script she decides pace and stress, then performs the sound.
- Both can slip: the stenographer mishears "four four seven one" as "for four seven one", and the actor reads "£42" as "pounds forty two" unless someone tells her the right order.

## Core concepts

### ASR architectures: CTC, transducers and encoder-decoder models

- Three designs cover most recognisers. **CTC** (connectionist temporal classification) predicts one symbol per audio frame, including a "blank", and collapses repeats.
- It is fast and streams well, but each frame is guessed with little help from the words around it.
- A **transducer** (RNN-T and its relatives) adds a small prediction network that reads the words already written, so it keeps streaming and uses language context.
- An **encoder-decoder** model such as Whisper encodes the whole chunk, then a decoder writes tokens one at a time while attending to the encoder, as in a translation model.
- It is the most flexible (language, task and timestamps are tokens) and needs the whole chunk first.

### Streaming versus batch recognition

- **Batch** recognition gets a finished file and may take its time: it can read the whole chunk twice, pick the best of several guesses and add punctuation.
- **Streaming** recognition must emit **partial results** while audio arrives, then a **final** result when the speaker pauses.
- Partials change as more audio comes in. Whisper reads fixed 30-second windows, so live use means re-running it on a growing buffer or choosing a model built for streaming.
- The partial-to-final gap feeds directly into the voice latency budget (see [Real-Time Voice Agents](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/real-time-voice-agents)).

### TTS: from text to waveform

- A classic pipeline has three stages. **Normalisation** writes digits, symbols and abbreviations as words.
- An **acoustic model** predicts acoustic frames (for example mel spectrogram frames) that carry pitch, pace and stress.
- A **vocoder** turns frames into waveform samples.
- A newer design lets a language model write discrete **audio-codec tokens**, and a codec decoder produces the waveform.
- Streaming TTS begins playing after the first sentence or clause, not after the whole reply (see [Streaming and Time to First Token](/ai-system-design/genai-system-design/streaming-and-time-to-first-token)).

### Metrics: word error rate, MOS and latency

- **WER** is the standard recognition metric (worked below).
- **MOS** (mean opinion score) rates how natural a voice sounds, from 1 to 5, by averaging scores from human listeners.
- A third family is latency: for ASR, the time from the end of speech to the final text; for TTS, the time from text to the first audio chunk, and the **real-time factor** (seconds to generate one second of audio; below 1 means faster than real time).

## Technical mechanics

### What a spectrogram is

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/multimodal/spectrogram"
  stepper
  caption="A toy signal: a 500 Hz tone, then a 2,000 Hz tone. Overlapping 25 ms windows each become one column of band energies. Whisper uses 80 bands at 16 kHz; the toy uses 8 at 8 kHz."
/>

**How to read it:**

- The top trace is the waveform: the first half wiggles slowly, the second half fast.
- The three bars are the first three windows. Each window is passed through a Fourier transform, its energy is summed into bands spaced on the mel scale, and the logarithm is taken.
- The result is a grid. Dark cells in a low row mean a low tone; the jump to a higher row means the pitch rose.
- The spec computes every cell from the signal, so nothing is drawn by hand.

**The numbers:**

- 1,600 samples with a 200-sample window and an 80-sample hop give (1,600 − 200) ÷ 80 + 1 = 18 columns.
- In Whisper's real setting a window is 400 samples (25 ms × 16,000) and the hop is 160 samples.
- Taking a logarithm matters because ears and speech vary over orders of magnitude of loudness; the log squeezes that into a range a network can learn from.

### Noise, accents and domain vocabulary

- Accuracy drops where the training audio was thin.
- **Noise** (a warehouse, a street, a speakerphone) hides the weak consonants that separate "fifteen" from "fifty".
- **Accents** that the training data held little of raise error rates.
- **Domain words** are the hardest: product names, order formats and policy terms were rarely in the training text.
- Three levers help: send audio at the quality the model expects (not over-compressed), add a **custom vocabulary** or biasing list where the service allows it (Brightpost's product names and the "order" plus four digits format), and fine-tune on your own labelled calls if the gap is large.
- Measure on your own recordings split by accent and noise level; one average WER hides the weak group.

### Diarization and timestamps

- **Diarization** answers "who spoke when". The usual method cuts audio into short segments, turns each into a **speaker embedding**, and clusters the embeddings; end-to-end neural models exist too.
- It matters for two-party calls (agent and caller) and for meetings.
- **Timestamps** give a start and end for each word or segment, which you need for captions, for jumping to the moment a refund was promised, and for trimming audio.
- Word timestamps are estimated, not measured, so treat them as good to a fraction of a second.

### Voice cloning and consent

- A TTS model that can copy a voice from a short sample is useful (a brand voice, a person who lost theirs) and dangerous (fraud).
- Rules to build in: **consent first**, recorded and stored, for any real person's voice; no cloning from audio you do not have rights to; a **watermark or disclosure** that the voice is synthetic; and a log of who generated what.
- Rules also apply on the phone: in the United States, the FCC ruled in February 2024 that AI-generated voices in calls are "artificial" voices under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, so the same consent requirements apply as for pre-recorded calls. Check the rules where your callers live.

### Worked example: Brightpost's phone line

- A customer phones about order 4471. She says: "i want a refund for order four four seven one".
- The recogniser sends partial results while she talks, then a final result when she stops.
- The message below is an illustrative shape, not one vendor's API.

```json
{"type": "partial", "text": "i want a refund for order four", "at_ms": 2100}
{"type": "final",
 "text": "i want refund for order for four seven one please",
 "confidence": 0.71, "at_ms": 2750}
```

The WER arithmetic for this exact turn:

<AnimatedDiagram
  spec="animated/multimodal/wer-worked-example"
  stepper
  caption="Align the words, count three kinds of edit, divide by the ten words said: 3 ÷ 10 = 30 %. The digit that was lost is the error that matters."
/>

**How to read it:**

- The top row is what she said (N = 10 words). The second row is what was written.
- Red cells are edits: the word "a" is missing (a **deletion**), the first "four" became "for" (a **substitution**), and "please" was invented (an **insertion**).
- The alignment is the one with the fewest edits; the spec checks it against an edit-distance count.

**The numbers:**

- WER = (S + D + I) ÷ N = (1 + 1 + 1) ÷ 10 = 30 %.
- That is a poor-looking score for a sentence a person would understand.
- But the real damage is smaller in words and larger in effect: "four seven one" is 471, so the lookup would fail or, worse, find the wrong order.
- The agent therefore reads the number back ("order four four seven one, is that right?") and confirms it, and Brightpost reports **digit-string accuracy** next to WER.

**The reply:**

- The agent writes "Refund of £42 for order 4471 approved".
- The normaliser turns it into "forty-two pounds" and "four four seven one", so the voice reads the digits one at a time, as people do for an order number.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Pros | Cons | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CTC model | Fast, streams, small | Weak language context; more errors on rare words | On-device or very low latency |
| Transducer | Streams with language context | Harder to train and tune | Live calls and captions |
| Encoder-decoder (Whisper-style) | Accurate, multilingual, timestamps and translation as tokens | Needs the whole chunk; heavier | Recordings, voicemail, meetings |
| Streaming ASR | Partials in milliseconds | Partials change; more errors than a final pass | Voice agents |
| Two-stage TTS (frames, vocoder) | Controllable, fast, well understood | Two models to maintain | Brand voices with fixed style |
| Codec language-model TTS | Expressive, can copy style from a sample | Harder to control; more consent risk | Rich voices where review is possible |

## Production systems

- **Whisper (OpenAI, 2022).** Trained on 680,000 hours of labelled audio, of which 117,000 hours cover 96 languages other than English. An encoder-decoder Transformer reading 80-bin log-mel input in 30-second chunks.
- **Brightpost's call recorder (assumed design).** Streaming recognition for the live agent, and a batch Whisper-style pass over the recording afterwards for the quality-review set and the golden transcripts.
- **Live voice APIs (November 2026).** Gemini Live takes 16-bit PCM at 16 kHz in and returns 24 kHz audio out; OpenAI's Realtime API takes 24 kHz mono PCM. Both stream audio in both directions (docs read 16 November 2026).
- **The FCC ruling (February 2024).** AI-generated voices on calls are treated as artificial voices under the TCPA.
- **Used in our case studies:** [Voice Agent for a Call Centre](/genai-system-design/case-studies/voice-agent-call-centre)

## Failure modes & pitfalls

- Reporting one WER for all audio. Split by noise, accent and topic, or the worst group stays hidden.
- Scoring raw text without normalising case, punctuation and numbers ("42" against "forty two"). Normalise both sides the same way before counting edits.
- Treating a low WER as proof the call works. A single lost digit in an order number fails the task at a WER of 10 %.
- Sending narrowband phone audio to a model trained on wideband audio and expecting studio accuracy. Test on real call recordings.
- Letting the TTS read digits or symbols as it likes. Write the formats out (order numbers digit by digit, £42 as "forty-two pounds").
- Cloning a voice without recorded consent, or using a synthetic voice on calls without checking local rules.

## Interview framework

1. Draw the chain: waveform, spectrogram, encoder, decoder, tokens. Give two numbers (16 kHz, 80 bins, 30 s).
2. Name the three architectures and the one trade-off that separates them: streaming against context.
3. Define WER as (S + D + I) ÷ N, compute one example, and say what it misses.
4. Describe TTS as normalise, acoustic frames, vocoder, with streaming from the first clause.
5. Close with failure modes: noise and accents, domain words, consent and disclosure.

**What interviewers listen for:** the spectrogram as the model's input, the WER formula with a computed example, the streaming and batch distinction, and a plan to evaluate on your own call audio.

**Red flags:** "the model hears the audio directly", WER as the only quality number, no mention of numbers and names, and no thought for consent.

  <InterviewQuestion question="Explain what a vocoder does and why TTS can start before the sentence is finished." category="Architecture" level="Mid">
    - In a two-stage system the acoustic model predicts frames, for example one every 10 ms, and the vocoder turns each frame into waveform samples: at 24 kHz that is 240 samples a frame.

    - With text arriving from a streaming language model, the system can speak the first clause as soon as it exists.
    - The cost is that prosody for a clause is planned with less context than for the whole sentence.
